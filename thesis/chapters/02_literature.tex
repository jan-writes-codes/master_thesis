\chapter{Related Literature}
\label{ch:literature}

In this chapter, we position our thesis in the literature. It draws together
three strands of research. The first is the rejection of the expectations
hypothesis in US Treasury markets and the search for the right
return-forecasting state variable. The second is the macro-finance work that
anchors yields to slow-moving inflation trends. The third is the international
evidence on common, global bond risk premia. A fourth concern is
methodological, as out-of-sample evaluation cuts across all three and shapes our
research design. We review each in turn, moving from the US evidence to the
international gap our thesis aims to fill. The technical framework we build on
these ideas (the yield decomposition, the factor hierarchy, the return
definitions, and the predictive regressions) is developed in
\Cref{ch:methodology}.

\section{The Expectations Hypothesis and Bond Return Predictability}
\label{sec:lit-eh}

The expectations hypothesis holds that long yields are averages of expected
future short rates plus at most a constant term premium, so that excess
returns on long bonds should not be forecastable. The empirical rejection
of this hypothesis is one of the oldest and most robust findings in fixed
income. \citet{famabliss1987} show that the forward--spot spread forecasts
the one-year excess return on the matched-maturity bond. Their paper
introduces both the founding result and the zero-coupon data construction
that became the standard in the field. \citet{campbell1991} contribute to the puzzle. In their regressions of yield changes on
the scaled yield spread, the EH predicts slope coefficients of one.
However, the estimates are significantly negative and increasingly so with
maturity. When the spread is high, long yields tend to fall, which
amplifies rather than offsets the return on the long bond. Together, these
results establish that the term premium is not constant. It is time-varying and, as the subsequent literature documented, also
countercyclical, rising in recessions and falling in booms.

Once predictability is accepted, the next question is which variable
forecasts returns. We can distinguish three positions. The first position holds
that the information is contained in the yield curve itself and can be
distilled into a single combination of forward rates
(\Cref{sec:lit-cp2005}). The second position holds that macroeconomic
variables matter beyond the curve. \citet{ludvigson2009} extract factors
from a large panel of macro series and show that real-activity and
inflation factors forecast excess returns over and above forward rates and
yields. This is the empirical origin of ``unspanned'' macro risk, which is
predictive information that the current yield curve does not reveal.
\citet{joslin2014} give this idea a no-arbitrage form, with output and
inflation unspanned by the yield curve yet shaping the prices of level,
slope, and curvature risk. The
third position, articulated by \citet{duffee2011}, holds that a
substantial part of risk-premium variation is hidden from the current
curve altogether. Such a hidden factor can move expected short rates and term
premia in offsetting directions, leaving the yield curve nearly unchanged.
The change in term premia can still be used to forecast returns. The macro-anchored cycle factor at the centre
of our thesis (\Cref{sec:lit-cp2015}) can be seen as a sharpened version
of the second position, since it relies on one specific, economically
interpretable macro variable, namely trend inflation.

Another challenge is an econometric
one. \citet{bauerhamilton2018} re-examine the published evidence for
predictors beyond the level, slope, and curvature of the yield curve and
argue that much of it is a small-sample artefact. Persistent regressors,
overlapping returns, and the small-sample bias that \citet{stambaugh1999}
identified for predictive regressions with a persistent, predetermined
regressor make standard tests over-reject the spanning null,
and under a bootstrap designed for this null the case for unspanned
predictors weakens considerably. This critique applies with full force to
any study of the cycle factor, because the cycle is a persistent,
estimated regressor in an overlapping twelve-month regression. The
methodological choices we make in \Cref{ch:methodology} are, therefore,
in large part a response to this.

\section{Forward-Rate Predictors: Cochrane and Piazzesi (2005)}
\label{sec:lit-cp2005}

\citet{cochrane2005} transformed the predictability literature by showing
that a single linear combination of forward rates, with the well-known
tent shape in its loadings, forecasts one-year excess returns on two- to
five-year US Treasuries with an in-sample $R^{2}$ of $0.35$ to $0.44$.
This roughly triples the explanatory power of the single-spread
regressions that preceded it. The same factor prices the entire maturity
spectrum, as the loadings of individual bonds' returns on the factor rise
smoothly with maturity. This smooth maturity pattern points to one
return-forecasting factor rather than maturity-specific premia. For the
research that followed, two features of the CP factor stand out. It is largely
orthogonal to the level, slope, and curvature factors
that summarise the cross-section of yields, which started the spanning
debate of \Cref{sec:lit-eh}. It is a reduced-form object, that is,
a combination of prices that predicts returns on those same prices, with
no economic mechanism attached. It tells us that premia vary, without
saying why. \citet{rebonato2025} trace the factor's predictive power to the
cointegration among the near-unit-root forward rates rather than to the
tent shape of the loadings, and connect this mechanism to the cycle factor
we turn to next. This open question is where the macro-anchored literature,
and ultimately our thesis, comes in. The CP factor nonetheless remains the benchmark. We therefore replicate it in \Cref{ch:replication}
and compare its global version with the global cycle factor in
\Cref{ch:robustness}.

\section{The Macro-Anchored Cycle Factor: Cieslak and Povala (2015)}
\label{sec:lit-cp2015}

\citet{cieslak2015} provide the economic mechanism behind this
predictability. Their starting point is a trend--cycle view of nominal
yields. While the level of yields is anchored by long-run inflation
expectations, which move only slowly across decades, the return-relevant
variation is the transitory deviation of yields from this anchor.
Operationally, trend inflation is a discounted moving average of past
year-on-year core CPI inflation. This construction goes back to \citet{kozicki2001}, who
showed that long-rate dynamics are dominated by a shifting perceived
``endpoint'' tied to inflation expectations, and that discounting past
inflation tracks survey measures of these expectations closely
\citep{kozicki2012}.
Projecting each maturity's yield on this trend yields maturity-specific
\emph{cycles}. A single \emph{cycle factor}, the fitted combination of
the short-maturity cycle and the average cycle, then forecasts one-year
excess returns with in-sample fits that exceed the forward-rate
benchmark, peak at long maturities, and subsume the CP factor in
direct comparisons. Beyond its fit, the construction is
attractive. Economically, the factor has a clear
interpretation. The premium is the distance of the curve from its macro
anchor, and it mean-reverts at business-cycle frequency.
Econometrically, the detrending reduces the near-unit-root persistence of
yields that complicates predictive regressions, because the cycle is
stationary while the yield is not.

The structural (model-based) counterpart to this reduced-form approach is
\citet{bauer2020}. In their shifting-endpoint term-structure model, the
risk-neutral long-run mean of the short rate moves with trend inflation
and the equilibrium real rate ($i^{*}=\pi^{*}+r^{*}$). Accounting for
these ``falling stars'' simultaneously raises measured return
predictability, produces more plausible term-premium estimates, and
attributes the great post-1980s yield decline largely to the falling
anchors rather than to the premium. We can view the reduced-form cycle
factor and the structural falling-stars model as two views of the same
mechanism. It is this mechanism-based
character that leads to the testable international prediction we make use of
in this thesis. If the mechanism is fundamental, it should not be unique to the US.

\section{Global Integration: Dahlquist and Hasseltoft (2013)}
\label{sec:lit-dh2013}

That international bond returns are predictable, and predictable by
common instruments, has been known since \citet{ilmanen1995}, who
documents forecastability in six markets with a small set of global
variables and finds that expected returns are highly correlated across
countries. The forward-rate factor of \citet{cochrane2005} has itself been
taken abroad. \citet{kessler2009} and \citet{sekkel2011} find that it
forecasts excess returns across developed markets, though with more modest
and more country-specific power than in the United States. The modern
factor-based statement of this result is \citet{dahlquist2013}, which forms
the international backbone of our thesis. They construct local CP factors for the United States, Germany,
Switzerland, and the United Kingdom, aggregate them into a GDP-weighted
\emph{global} CP factor, and establish two results that define the
integration agenda. First, the global factor predicts excess returns in
every market. Second, in joint regressions it largely subsumes the local
factors, meaning that once the common component is included,
country-specific forward-rate information adds little. They interpret
this as evidence of market integration. Internationally diversified
investors price a common risk, so compensation accrues to global rather
than to local exposure. Furthermore, they tie the global factor to US
premia and the international business cycle. \citet{zhu2015} extends this
result to real time and shows that the GDP-weighted global CP factor
predicts out of sample across developed markets where local predictors
fail. We later mirror this pattern with the global cycle factor, which
predicts out of sample where the local cycle factors do not
(\Cref{sec:res-oos}).

\citet{dahlquist2013} also address currency risk, which is the issue that
separates international from domestic bond economics. A US-dollar
investor who holds foreign bonds unhedged earns the local bond return
plus a currency return that is an order of magnitude more volatile. The
currency strand of the literature establishes that this exposure is at
best partially compensated. \citet{campbellviceira2010} show that for a
global bond investor the risk-minimising currency position is close to a
full hedge, and \citet{lustig2019} show that the currency carry premium
shrinks as foreign bond maturity lengthens, so that long-maturity
unhedged positions earn little compensation for their currency risk.
Against this background, \citet{dahlquist2013} construct a
\emph{dollar-return} global factor, which is a version of the global factor
fitted to dollar-denominated returns, and show that it recovers much of
the predictability that the currency conversion destroys, while remaining
only moderately correlated with the unadjusted factor. The dollar-return
cycle factor we build is the direct analogue of this construction.

\section{International Macro-Anchored Predictability and the Gap}
\label{sec:lit-gap}

The two research areas described above, macro-anchored
predictability and global aggregation, have only begun to be combined.
\citet{zhang2021} take the falling-stars logic to international data.
Using the same discounted moving-average inflation trend plus an estimated real-rate trend, they show that adding
both trends to excess-return regressions delivers notable
out-of-sample gains in Canada, Germany, Japan, and the United Kingdom.
Related evidence extends the trend-augmented approach to Chinese
government bonds \citep{zhang2022} and documents, for emerging markets, that
global factors dominate local ones in explaining bond risk premia
\citep{iania2021}. These papers establish that the macro
anchor matters outside the United States. However, they stop short of
combining the trends into a single macro-anchored factor. They add raw
trends to yield-based
regressions market by market rather than constructing the cycle factor
itself. They cover four markets instead of the G10 and they build
neither a global aggregate nor a currency-adjusted factor. Closest in
spirit, \citet{cejnek2020} aggregate \citet{cochrane2005} bond factors
across four regions and, in an extension, apply the \citet{cieslak2015}
decomposition to bond and dividend claims, but they do not construct a
global cycle factor for government bonds.

From this review, we can identify a specific gap in the literature. To
our knowledge, no study takes the \citet{cieslak2015} cycle factor itself
across the G10, no study builds a GDP-weighted \emph{global cycle factor}
in the manner of \citet{dahlquist2013}, and no study constructs a
dollar-return global cycle factor for the unhedged dollar investor.
The existing international macro-anchored evidence has also not
been subjected to the full out-of-sample discipline that the
methodological literature demands. Because of this gap, we want to add to
the field by constructing the local, global, and dollar-return global cycle
factors for eleven G10 markets and by evaluating them in the three-phase
design of \Cref{sec:fw-hypotheses}. We hold every result to the real-time
standard, i.e. estimating it out-of-sample
\citep{campbellthompson2008}.
