\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:literature}

This thesis sits at the intersection of three literatures: the rejection of
the expectations hypothesis in US Treasury markets and the search for the
right return-forecasting state variable; the macro-finance work that anchors
yields to slow-moving inflation trends; and the international evidence on
common, global bond risk premia. A fourth, methodological strand---the
out-of-sample and small-sample scrutiny of predictive regressions---cuts
across all three and shapes the thesis's research design. This chapter
reviews each in turn, following the arc from the US evidence to the
international gap the thesis fills.

% ===========================================================================
\section{The Expectations Hypothesis and Bond Return Predictability}
\label{sec:lit-eh}

The expectations hypothesis holds that long yields are averages of expected
future short rates plus at most a constant term premium, so that excess
returns on long bonds are unforecastable. Its empirical rejection is one of
the oldest and most robust findings in fixed income. \citet{famabliss1987}
show that the forward--spot spread forecasts the one-year excess return on
the matched-maturity bond, introducing both the founding result and the
zero-coupon data construction that became the field standard.
\citet{campbell1991} provide the cleanest statement of the puzzle: in their
regressions of yield changes on the scaled yield spread, the EH predicts
slope coefficients of one, yet the estimates are significantly
\emph{negative} and increasingly so with maturity---when the spread is high,
long yields tend to fall, amplifying rather than offsetting the long bond's
return. Together these results establish that the term premium is not
constant: it is large, time-varying, and---as the subsequent literature
documented---countercyclical, rising in recessions and falling in booms.

Once predictability is accepted, the question becomes \emph{what} forecasts
returns, and the literature has split along this question ever since.
Three positions define the field. The first holds that the information is
in the yield curve itself, distilled into a single combination of forward
rates (\Cref{sec:lit-cp2005}). The second holds that macroeconomic
variables matter \emph{beyond} the curve: \citet{ludvigson2009} extract
factors from a large panel of macro series and show that real-activity and
inflation factors forecast excess returns over and above forward rates and
yields---the empirical origin of ``unspanned'' macro risk. The third,
articulated by \citet{duffee2011}, holds that a substantial part of
risk-premium variation is \emph{hidden} from the current curve altogether:
a state variable can move expected short rates and term premia in
offsetting directions, leaving yields nearly unchanged while still
forecasting returns. The macro-anchored cycle factor at the centre of this
thesis (\Cref{sec:lit-cp2015}) is a sharpened version of the second
position: one specific, economically interpretable macro variable---trend
inflation---rather than a statistical factor panel.

This debate is not settled, and the strongest challenge is econometric.
\citet{bauerhamilton2018} re-examine the published evidence for predictors
beyond the level, slope, and curvature of the yield curve and argue that
much of it is a small-sample artefact: persistent regressors, overlapping
returns, and the endogeneity highlighted by \citet{stambaugh1999} make
standard tests over-reject the spanning null, and under a bootstrap
designed for that null the case for unspanned predictors weakens
considerably. This critique applies with full force to any cycle-factor
study---the cycle is a persistent, estimated regressor in an overlapping
twelve-month regression---and the thesis's methodological choices
(\Cref{ch:methodology}) are, in large part, a response to it.

\section{Forward-Rate Predictors: Cochrane and Piazzesi (2005)}
\label{sec:lit-cp2005}

\citet{cochrane2005} transformed the predictability literature by showing
that a \emph{single} linear combination of forward rates---famously
tent-shaped in its loadings---forecasts one-year excess returns on two- to
five-year US Treasuries with an in-sample $R^{2}$ of $0.35$ to $0.44$,
roughly tripling the explanatory power of the single-spread regressions
that preceded it. The same factor prices the entire maturity spectrum: the
loadings of individual bonds' returns on the factor rise smoothly with
maturity, the signature of one return-forecasting factor rather than
maturity-specific premia. Two features of the CP factor matter for what
followed. First, it is largely orthogonal to the level, slope, and
curvature factors that summarise the cross-section of yields, which seeded
the spanning debate of \Cref{sec:lit-eh}. Second, it is a reduced-form
object: a combination of prices that predicts returns on those same
prices, with no economic mechanism attached. It answers \emph{whether}
premia vary but not \emph{why}---the opening that the macro-anchored
literature, and ultimately this thesis, walks through. The CP factor
nonetheless remains the canonical benchmark, and the thesis replicates it
(\Cref{ch:replication}) and races its global version against the global
cycle factor (\Cref{ch:robustness}).

\section{The Macro-Anchored Cycle Factor: Cieslak and Povala (2015)}
\label{sec:lit-cp2015}

\citet{cieslak2015} supply the economic mechanism. Their starting point is
a trend--cycle view of nominal yields: the \emph{level} of yields is
anchored by long-run inflation expectations, which move slowly across
decades, while the \emph{return-relevant} variation is the transitory
deviation of yields from that anchor. Operationally, trend inflation is a
discounted moving average of past year-on-year core CPI inflation, with
monthly decay $v=0.987$ over a ten-year window---a construction descending
from \citet{kozicki2001}, who showed that long-rate dynamics are dominated
by a shifting perceived ``endpoint'' tied to inflation expectations, and
that discounting past inflation tracks survey measures of those
expectations closely. Projecting each maturity's yield on this trend
yields maturity-specific \emph{cycles}, and a single \emph{cycle
factor}---the fitted combination of the short-maturity cycle and the
average cycle---forecasts one-year excess returns with in-sample fits that
exceed the forward-rate benchmark, peaking at long maturities, and that
subsume the CP factor in head-to-head comparisons. Two properties make
the construction attractive beyond its fit. Economically, the factor has
an interpretation: the premium is the distance of the curve from its macro
anchor, mean-reverting at business-cycle frequency. Econometrically,
detrending tames the near-unit-root persistence of yields that bedevils
predictive regressions, because the cycle is stationary where the yield is
not.

The structural counterpart is \citet{bauer2020}. In their
shifting-endpoint term-structure model, the risk-neutral long-run mean of
the short rate moves with trend inflation and the equilibrium real rate
($i^{*}=\pi^{*}+r^{*}$), and accounting for these ``falling stars''
simultaneously raises measured return predictability, produces more
plausible term-premium estimates, and attributes the great post-1980s
yield decline largely to the falling anchors rather than to the premium.
The reduced-form cycle factor and the structural falling-stars model are
two views of the same mechanism, and their agreement is the strongest
argument that the trend--cycle decomposition captures something real
rather than a fortunate parameterisation. It is precisely this
mechanism-based character that generates the testable international
prediction the thesis exploits: if the mechanism is fundamental, it cannot
be a US idiosyncrasy.

\section{Global Integration: Dahlquist and Hasseltoft (2013)}
\label{sec:lit-dh2013}

That international bond returns are predictable, and predictable by
\emph{common} instruments, has been known since \citet{ilmanen1995}, who
documents forecastability in six markets with a small set of global
variables and finds expected returns highly correlated across countries.
The modern factor-based statement is \citet{dahlquist2013}, the
international backbone of this thesis. They construct local CP factors
for the United States, Germany, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom,
aggregate them into a GDP-weighted \emph{global} CP factor, and establish
the two results that define the integration agenda. First, the global
factor predicts excess returns in every market. Second, in joint
regressions it largely \emph{subsumes} the local factors: once the common
component is included, country-specific forward-rate information adds
little. Their interpretation is market integration---internationally
diversified investors price a common risk, so compensation accrues to
global rather than local exposure---and they tie the global factor to US
premia and the international business cycle. \citet{zhu2015} extends the
result to real time, showing that the GDP-weighted global CP factor
predicts \emph{out of sample} across developed markets where local
predictors fail, a finding the thesis will mirror precisely for the cycle
family.

\citet{dahlquist2013} also confront currency risk, the issue that
separates international from domestic bond economics. A US-dollar
investor who holds foreign bonds unhedged earns the local bond return
plus a currency return that is an order of magnitude more volatile, and
the currency strand of the literature establishes that this exposure is
at best partially compensated: \citet{campbellviceira2010} show that for
a global \emph{bond} investor the risk-minimising currency position is
close to a full hedge, and \citet{lustig2019} show that the currency
carry premium shrinks as foreign bond maturity lengthens, so that
long-maturity unhedged positions earn little for their currency risk.
Against this background, \citet{dahlquist2013} construct an
\emph{FX-adjusted} global factor---a version of the global factor fitted
to dollar-denominated returns---and show that it recovers much of the
predictability that currency conversion destroys, while remaining only
moderately correlated with the unadjusted factor. The FX-adjusted
\emph{cycle} factor built in this thesis is the direct analogue, and the
contrast between the two families' FX adjustments turns out to be one of
the thesis's findings (\Cref{sec:res-phase3}).

\section{International Macro-Anchored Predictability and the Gap}
\label{sec:lit-gap}

The two programmes just described---macro-anchored predictability and
global aggregation---have only begun to be combined. \citet{zhang2021}
take the falling-stars logic international: using the same discounted
moving-average inflation trend ($v=0.987$, ten-year window) plus an
estimated real-rate trend, they show that adding both trends to canonical
excess-return regressions delivers notable out-of-sample gains in Canada,
Germany, Japan, and the United Kingdom. Related evidence extends the
trend-augmented approach to Chinese government bonds \citep{zhang2022}.
These papers establish that the macro anchor matters outside the United
States, but they stop short of the factor-level synthesis: they add raw
trends to yield-based regressions market by market, rather than
constructing the cycle factor itself; they cover four markets, not the
G10; and they build no global aggregate and no currency-adjusted factor.
On the asset-class side, \citet{randl2025} derive a stochastic discount
factor for currency-hedged developed-market government bond portfolios
and document that the priced risks of international bonds are
fundamentally distinct from those of currencies, that the market price of
bond risk peaks in crises and varies with inflation levels and
cross-market inflation dispersion, and that common sources of return
\emph{variation} are largely unpriced. Their work prices the asset class
unconditionally; it does not ask which observable state variable tracks
the conditional premium in real time---the question this thesis poses.

A final strand disciplines how any such question should be answered.
\citet{welch2008} showed for the equity premium that most celebrated
predictors fail out of sample against the historical mean;
\citet{campbellthompson2008} introduced the out-of-sample $R^{2}$ against
the recursive prevailing mean that has become the standard yardstick, and
showed that even small positive values are economically meaningful for an
investor. For bonds specifically, \citet{thornton2012} found that
forward-rate predictability of the CP type generates little systematic
economic value for a real-time investor---a sobering benchmark for any
factor whose case rests on in-sample fit. Combined with the
\citet{bauerhamilton2018} small-sample critique of \Cref{sec:lit-eh},
the message for research design is unambiguous: in-sample fit is weak
evidence, and a credible predictability claim must survive recursive
real-time estimation, demanding inference, and a simulated
no-predictability benchmark.

The gap, then, is specific. No study takes the \citet{cieslak2015} cycle
factor itself across the G10; none builds a GDP-weighted \emph{global
cycle factor} in the manner of \citet{dahlquist2013}; none constructs an
FX-adjusted global cycle factor for the unhedged dollar investor; and the
international macro-anchored evidence that exists has not been subjected
to the full out-of-sample discipline that the methodological literature
demands. This thesis fills that cell: it constructs the local, global,
and FX-adjusted global cycle factors for eleven G10 markets, evaluates
them in the three-phase design of \Cref{sec:fw-hypotheses}, and holds
every result to the real-time standard---fully-recursive estimation of
the entire generated-regressor chain, scored against the prevailing mean
\citep{campbellthompson2008}, with bootstrap and Monte-Carlo inference
calibrated to the overlapping-return environment.
