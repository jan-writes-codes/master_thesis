\chapter{Economic Value of the Global Cycle Factor}
\label{ch:strategy}

\Cref{sec:res-oos} established the one statistical fact that matters for an
investor: of the cycle factors, only the \emph{global} factor beats the
recursive prevailing mean out of sample. A positive out-of-sample $R^{2}$ is
precisely the condition under which a real-time investor can convert
predictability into utility \citep{campbellthompson2008}. This chapter asks
whether that conversion is economically meaningful by building a fully
out-of-sample market-timing strategy around the global cycle factor and scoring
it against a passive benchmark. The exercise is deliberately self-contained: it
uses only the recursive factor and forecasts of \Cref{sec:meth-oos}, takes no
position that an investor could not have taken in real time, and is implemented
in \texttt{strategy.R}.

\section{Strategy Design}
\label{sec:strat-design}

Three features of the evidence dictate the design. First, the predictability
that survives out of sample is in \emph{local-currency} returns; the dollar
signal is far weaker (\Cref{sec:res-phase3}). The strategy is therefore run for a
\emph{currency-hedged} global investor, with the unhedged dollar case reported as
a contrast in \Cref{sec:strat-fx}. Second, the global cycle factor is a single
time series, so it carries no information about which country to favour---it
times \emph{aggregate} bond exposure. The investable asset is accordingly a
single GDP-weighted, ten-year global government-bond portfolio, whose one-year
excess return is the weighted average of the national returns. Third, the signal
must be usable in real time, so both the factor and the forecasting regression
respect time~$t$.

Each month the investor forms a real-time forecast of the portfolio's
twelve-month excess return from the recursive regression
$\rx_{t+12}=a+b\,\GCF^{\mathrm{oos}}_{t}+\varepsilon$, estimated only on
outcomes realised by~$t$, and sets a mean--variance exposure
\begin{equation}
  w_{t} \;=\; \frac{1}{\gamma}\,
              \frac{\widehat{\E}_{t}\!\left[\rx_{t+12}\right]}
                   {\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{t}},
  \label{eq:strat-weight}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{t}$ is the recursive return variance and $\gamma$
the risk aversion. Because both the factor and the regression are recursive, the
exposure is ``doubly out-of-sample''. The benchmark is the same investor using
the recursive prevailing-mean forecast, and a passive buy-and-hold position. The
Sharpe ratio, which is invariant to a constant rescaling of $w_{t}$, is reported
as is; the certainty-equivalent (CER) return is reported at equal average
exposure across strategies so that the variance penalty is comparable.

\section{Performance}
\label{sec:strat-performance}

\Cref{tab:strat-perf} reports the out-of-sample performance over 2005--2024, the
period for which the fully-recursive factor is available. The global bond
portfolio itself has an out-of-sample $R^{2}$ of $0.125$ against the recursive
mean---higher than the pooled panel figure of \Cref{sec:res-oos}, because
aggregating the eleven national cycles into one global signal averages away
estimation noise. That statistical edge translates into economic value. Timing
the portfolio with the global cycle factor raises the annual Sharpe ratio from
$0.31$ for the passive position to $0.39$, and from $0.34$ for an investor who
times on the recursive mean. For a mean--variance investor with risk aversion
$\gamma=5$, the certainty-equivalent gain is $0.58\%$ per year over buy-and-hold
and $0.37\%$ over the mean-timing benchmark; the global cycle factor retains the
highest certainty equivalent at $\gamma=10$ as well.

\Cref{fig:strat-cumret} shows the cumulative wealth of the timed and passive
portfolios on a non-overlapping annual basis: the timing strategy ends the period
ahead, and most of its outperformance accrues by avoiding the worst of the
bond-market drawdowns rather than by amplifying the good years.
\Cref{fig:strat-exposure} makes the mechanism explicit---the global cycle factor
cuts bond exposure when it signals a low risk premium and restores it when the
signal turns favourable, whereas the mean-timing investor holds a nearly constant
position. The gains are achieved with low turnover, because the global cycle
factor is highly persistent, so realistic transaction costs would erode little of
the advantage.

\section{The Role of Currency Risk}
\label{sec:strat-fx}

For an unhedged US-dollar investor the same strategy is markedly weaker. Running
the identical rule on the dollar excess return \eqref{eq:rx-usd} of the global
portfolio roughly halves the out-of-sample $R^{2}$, from $0.125$ to $0.077$, and
compresses the Sharpe-ratio improvement to $0.25$ from a passive $0.20$. This
mirrors the in-sample finding of \Cref{sec:res-phase3}: the volatility of the
currency return dilutes the cyclical signal in the bond. The practical
implication is direct---the economic value of the global cycle factor is
available primarily to an investor who hedges the currency exposure of the
international bond position, and currency risk, not a failure of the signal, is
what limits the unhedged investor.

\section{Caveats}
\label{sec:strat-caveats}

The result should be read as a proof of concept, not a tradable system, and
three limitations bound it. First, the magnitude is modest---a Sharpe-ratio
improvement of roughly $0.08$ and a certainty-equivalent gain below one percent a
year---which is typical of bond-return predictability and consistent with the
small out-of-sample $R^{2}$. Second, the forecasts use overlapping twelve-month
returns, so the supporting precision is overstated relative to non-overlapping
data, and the strategy is evaluated gross of transaction costs (mitigated, but
not eliminated, by the low turnover noted above). Third, because the global cycle
factor is a single series the strategy can only time aggregate exposure; a
cross-sectional strategy would require a factor with country-specific variation,
which the out-of-sample evidence does not support for the local factor. With
these caveats, the conclusion stands: the predictability identified in
\Cref{ch:results} is not merely statistical but carries genuine, if moderate,
economic value for a currency-hedged global bond investor. The broader
interpretation is taken up in \Cref{ch:discussion}.
