\chapter{Portfolio Construction}
\label{ch:strategy}

In \Cref{sec:res-oos} I established that the one statistical result that matters
for an investor is the global cycle factor. A positive out-of-sample $R^{2}$ is
the condition under which a real-time investor can convert
predictability into utility \citep{campbellthompson2008}. In this chapter, I
ask whether this conversion is economically meaningful. To answer this
question, I build a fully out-of-sample market-timing strategy around the
global cycle factor and compare it against a passive benchmark. I keep this
exercise self-contained. I use only the recursive factor and
forecasts of \Cref{sec:meth-oos}, entering positions with information known
only at time~$t$.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Strategy Design}
\label{sec:strat-design}

The following three findings guide the design of the strategy. To begin with, the
predictability that survives out of sample is in local-currency
returns (\Cref{sec:res-phase3}). I
therefore run the strategy for a currency-hedged global investor (GCF)
and analyse the unhedged US-dollar investor (FXGCF) separately in
\Cref{sec:strat-fx}.
Local-currency excess returns, in which each bond position is funded in the
local money market, are the standard representation of an FX-risk-hedged
international bond portfolio and are equivalent to managing the currency
exposure with FX forwards \citep{randl2025}. Second, the global cycle factor
is a single time series, so it carries no information about which country to
favour and can only time aggregate bond exposure. The investable asset
is accordingly a single GDP-weighted, ten-year global government bond
portfolio, whose one-year excess return is the weighted average of the
national returns. And third, the signal must be usable in real
time, so both the factor and the forecasting regression respect time~$t$.

Each month, the investor forms a real-time forecast of the portfolio's
twelve-month excess return from the recursive regression
$\rx_{t+12}=a+b\,\GCF^{\mathrm{oos}}_{t}+\varepsilon$, and sets a mean--variance exposure
\begin{equation}
  w_{t} \;=\; \frac{1}{\gamma}\,
              \frac{\widehat{\E}_{t}\!\left[\rx_{t+12}\right]}
                   {\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{t}},
  \label{eq:strat-weight}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{t}$ is the recursive return variance and $\gamma$
the risk aversion. As benchmarks, I use the same
investor with the recursive prevailing-mean forecast, and a passive
buy-and-hold position. The Sharpe ratio is invariant to a constant rescaling
of $w_{t}$, so I report it directly. The certainty-equivalent (CER) return is
not scale-invariant, so I report it at equal average exposure across
strategies. This makes the variance penalty comparable.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:strat-implementation}

In this section, I cover the implementation. Each
month $t$, using only information available at $t$: \emph{(i)} I re-estimate
the full generated-regressor chain on outcomes realised by $t$ (a twelve-month
lag), with a sixty-month minimum training history (\Cref{sec:meth-oos}). This
regards trend inflation \eqref{eq:trendinf}, the yield-cycle decomposition
\eqref{eq:decomp}, the local-factor regressions \eqref{eq:cf-reg}, and the GDP aggregation \eqref{eq:gcf}. \emph{(ii)} I form the forecast
$\widehat{\E}_{t}[\rx_{t+12}]=\hat a_{t}+\hat b_{t}\,\GCF^{\mathrm{oos}}_{t}$
from the recursive regression, \emph{(iii)} I estimate the variance
$\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{t}$ on an expanding window, \emph{(iv)} I set the
target weight to the mean-variance exposure \eqref{eq:strat-weight}, truncated
at zero (long-only) and rescaled to unit average exposure for reporting, and
\emph{(v)} I rebalance monthly, with one-way turnover $|w_{t}-w_{t-1}|$.

Anything the strategy earns is therefore attributable to the real-time
information content of the global cycle factor.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Performance}
\label{sec:strat-performance}

In \Cref{tab:strat-perf}, I report the out-of-sample performance over
2005--2024, the period for which the fully-recursive factor is available. The
global bond portfolio itself has an out-of-sample $R^{2}$ of $0.127$ against
the recursive mean. This outcome translates into economic value. Timing the portfolio with the global
cycle factor raises the annual Sharpe ratio from $0.34$ for the passive
position to $0.40$, and from $0.36$ for an investor who times on the recursive
mean. For a mean--variance investor with risk aversion $\gamma=5$, the
certainty-equivalent gain is $0.48\%$ per year over buy-and-hold and $0.30\%$
over the mean-timing benchmark. At $\gamma=10$, however, the more risk-averse
investor slightly prefers the mean-timing benchmark ($0.17\%$ against $0.15\%$),
as the sharper variance penalty offsets the timing gain.

The drawdown profile confirms that the value of the signal is defensive. In
\Cref{fig:strat-drawdown}, I plot the drawdown from peak of the same monthly
wealth curves. The maximum drawdown of the timed portfolio is $20.3\%$,
against $24.9\%$ for the mean-timing investor and $30.7\%$ for buy-and-hold.
The deepest drawdowns fall in the 2021--2022 global hiking cycle, the worst
bond-market episode in the sample. The timed investor enters it with reduced
exposure because the global cycle factor had already turned
(\Cref{fig:strat-exposure}), which is what the mechanism predicts. The factor
forecasts low premia when yields sit far
below their inflation anchor, as they did in 2021. I quantify where the
strategy trades, and what this trading costs, in \Cref{sec:strat-costs}.

\section{Subperiods, Turnover, and Transaction Costs}
\label{sec:strat-costs}

A full-sample Sharpe ratio does not reveal in which periods a strategy
actually generates its performance. In \Cref{tab:strat-sub}, I therefore
split the evaluation window in half and additionally isolate the 2021--2022
hiking cycle. The split separates two very different regimes. In the strong bond
decade of 2005--2014, timing did not pay off on a risk-adjusted basis. The
timed portfolio earned a full percentage point a year more than buy-and-hold
($6.5\%$ against $5.3\%$) but took proportionally more risk, so its Sharpe
ratio was lower ($0.93$ against $1.09$). But the ranking reverses where an investor most needs
it to. Over 2015--2024, a decade in which the passive portfolio lost money,
the timed strategy preserves a higher mean and the highest Sharpe ratio, and
in the 2021--2022 drawdown it has the least negative Sharpe ratio. The full-sample advantage of
\Cref{tab:strat-perf} is therefore not a uniform edge but a defensive one. It is concentrated in the regime of falling and volatile bond prices.
\Cref{fig:strat-rollsr} shows this characterisation. In the rolling five-year
Sharpe ratios, the passive portfolio holds the upper hand in the early,
high-premium windows, the two series then track each other through the middle
of the sample, and the timed portfolio pulls ahead only as the hiking cycle
unfolds. Across all windows, the timed portfolio is not a clear winner.
The full-sample edge is therefore real, but it comes from a handful
of episodes rather than steady accrual.

The trading intensity of the strategy is modest. Because the global cycle
factor is highly persistent, the target weight moves slowly. The average
one-way turnover of the timed portfolio is $9.1\%$ of notional per month
(mean-timing: $1.3\%$), which corresponds to roughly one full portfolio turn
per year. In \Cref{tab:strat-costs}, I convert this into net-of-cost
performance by charging a proportional one-way cost on every unit of notional
traded. At $10$ basis points, which is already conservative for ten-year G10
government bond futures, where quoted half-spreads on the underlying market are
of the order of one basis point \citep{fleming2003}, the Sharpe ratio of the
timed strategy falls from $0.41$ to
$0.39$ and the certainty-equivalent gain over buy-and-hold shrinks by about
eleven basis points a year. Even at punitive $50$ basis points,
the timed strategy still matches or beats buy-and-hold on both metrics,
although at this cost level the near-static mean-timing rule becomes the
better implementation. Transaction costs therefore qualify the magnitude of
the economic value but do not overturn its existence.

\section{The US-Dollar Investor and the Dollar-Return Factor}
\label{sec:strat-fx}

A US-based investor who holds the global bond portfolio unhedged earns the
dollar excess return \eqref{eq:rx-usd}, which adds the currency return to the
local bond return. The signal for this investor is the dollar-return
global cycle factor $\FXGCF_{t}$, which I built in \Cref{sec:fw-fxgcf} to
target dollar returns directly. In \Cref{tab:strat-usd}, I run the timing
rule of \Cref{sec:strat-design} on the dollar portfolio, using the recursive
$\FXGCF^{\mathrm{oos}}$ signal. The
dollar-return signal shares the five-year recursive burn-in of the global
factor, so I evaluate the dollar strategy over the same out-of-sample window
(2005--2024) as the hedged strategy of \Cref{sec:strat-performance}.

Over this sample the
unhedged dollar bond premium is modest but positive. The buy-and-hold portfolio
earns about $1.4\%$ a year in excess return for a Sharpe ratio near $0.18$, as
the strength of the dollar through the 2010s offsets much, though not all, of
the foreign-bond return for a US investor. The two dollar signals also
diverge at the aggregate level. Timing on the unadjusted recursive global
factor works: the GCF-timed dollar strategy earns a Sharpe ratio of $0.24$
against the passive $0.18$, with an aggregate-portfolio out-of-sample $R^{2}$
of $0.078$. The dollar-return factor, in contrast, does not time the
aggregate dollar portfolio well. Its forecast of the GDP-weighted
portfolio return has an out-of-sample $R^{2}$ of $-0.01$, and the FXGCF-timed
strategy earns a Sharpe ratio of $0.18$, no better than buy-and-hold.

This does not contradict Phase~III. It reflects where the
dollar-return factor's strength lies. Its in-sample recovery
(\Cref{sec:res-phase3}) and its pooled out-of-sample edge
(\Cref{ch:robustness}) come from the per-country USD regressions, that is,
from the cross-section of dollar returns. I therefore reach the same conclusion
as elsewhere in this thesis. Timing adds value for the dollar investor
as well, but currency risk, and not a failure of the cycle signal, caps the
level it can reach. Both dollar strategies remain well below the $0.40$ of
the currency-hedged investor in \Cref{sec:strat-performance}. The economic
value of the global cycle factor accrues largely to the investor who
hedges the currency exposure of the international bond position.

\paragraph{Caveats.}
\label{sec:strat-caveats}
This result should be read as a proof of concept rather than a tradable system, and
two limitations qualify it. First, the magnitude is modest. The portfolio shows a
Sharpe-ratio improvement of about $0.06$ and a certainty-equivalent gain below
one percent a year. Its edge is episodic, concentrated in the poor bond decade
and the 2021--2022 drawdown. Second, the
forecasts use overlapping twelve-month returns, so the supporting precision is
overstated and the subperiod statistics are descriptive. Transaction costs
(\Cref{tab:strat-costs}) erode but do not eliminate the advantage. With these caveats, the predictability of \Cref{ch:results} carries economic value for a currency-hedged global bond investor. I take up the broader interpretation in \Cref{ch:discussion}.
