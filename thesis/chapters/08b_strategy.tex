% Ch. Portfolio Construction. Turns the OOS result of the Results chapter into

\chapter{Portfolio Construction}
\label{ch:strategy}

\Cref{sec:res-oos} established the one statistical fact that matters for an
investor: of the cycle factors, only the \emph{global} factor beats the
recursive prevailing mean out of sample. A positive out-of-sample $R^{2}$ is
precisely the condition under which a real-time investor can convert
predictability into utility \citep{campbellthompson2008}. This chapter asks
whether that conversion is economically meaningful by building a fully
out-of-sample market-timing strategy around the global cycle factor and scoring
it against a passive benchmark. The exercise is deliberately self-contained: it
uses only the recursive factor and forecasts of \Cref{sec:meth-oos}, takes no
position that an investor could not have taken in real time, and is implemented
in \texttt{strategy.R} and \texttt{strategy\_ext.R}.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Strategy Design}
\label{sec:strat-design}

Three features of the evidence dictate the design. First, the predictability
that survives out of sample is in \emph{local-currency} returns; the dollar
signal is far weaker (\Cref{sec:res-phase3}). The strategy is therefore run for a
\emph{currency-hedged} global investor, with the unhedged US-dollar investor
analysed separately in \Cref{sec:strat-fx}; local-currency excess returns, in
which each bond position is funded in the local money market, are the standard
representation of an FX-risk-hedged international bond portfolio, equivalent
to managing the currency exposure with FX forwards \citep{randl2025}. Second,
the global cycle factor is a single
time series, so it carries no information about which country to favour---it
times \emph{aggregate} bond exposure. The investable asset is accordingly a
single GDP-weighted, ten-year global government-bond portfolio, whose one-year
excess return is the weighted average of the national returns; even this naive
weighting already captures substantial diversification benefits from
imperfectly correlated term-structure movements across markets
\citep{randl2025}. Third, the signal
must be usable in real time, so both the factor and the forecasting regression
respect time~$t$.

Each month the investor forms a real-time forecast of the portfolio's
twelve-month excess return from the recursive regression
$\rx_{t+12}=a+b\,\GCF^{\mathrm{oos}}_{t}+\varepsilon$, estimated only on
outcomes realised by~$t$, and sets a mean--variance exposure
\begin{equation}
  w_{t} \;=\; \frac{1}{\gamma}\,
              \frac{\widehat{\E}_{t}\!\left[\rx_{t+12}\right]}
                   {\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{t}},
  \label{eq:strat-weight}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{t}$ is the recursive return variance and $\gamma$
the risk aversion. Because both the factor and the regression are recursive, the
exposure is ``doubly out-of-sample''. The benchmark is the same investor using
the recursive prevailing-mean forecast, and a passive buy-and-hold position. The
Sharpe ratio, which is invariant to a constant rescaling of $w_{t}$, is reported
as is; the certainty-equivalent (CER) return is reported at equal average
exposure across strategies so that the variance penalty is comparable.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:strat-implementation}

This section makes the mapping from signal to position explicit. At the end of
each month $t$ the investor executes five steps, each using only information
available at $t$.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), itemsep=2pt]
  \item \emph{Update the signal.} The entire generated-regressor chain is
    re-estimated on data through $t$: trend inflation \eqref{eq:trendinf} is
    extended by the latest core-CPI observation (it is backward-looking by
    construction); the yield--cycle decomposition \eqref{eq:decomp} is re-fit
    for every country--maturity pair; the local factor regressions
    \eqref{eq:cf-reg} are re-fit using only excess-return outcomes realised by
    $t$ (a twelve-month outcome lag, so the last usable observation is dated
    $t-12$); and the local factors are aggregated with the GDP weights
    \eqref{eq:gcf} into the recursive global factor $\GCF^{\mathrm{oos}}_{t}$.
    Every layer requires a minimum training history of sixty months, which
    produces the five-year burn-in described in \Cref{sec:meth-oos}.
  \item \emph{Form the return forecast.} The forecasting regression
    $\rx_{t+12}=a+b\,\GCF^{\mathrm{oos}}_{t}+\varepsilon$ is re-estimated by
    OLS on the same lag-respecting training sample, and the forecast is
    $\widehat{\E}_{t}[\rx_{t+12}]=\hat a_{t}+\hat b_{t}\,\GCF^{\mathrm{oos}}_{t}$.
  \item \emph{Estimate the risk.} The variance $\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{t}$ is
    the sample variance of the portfolio's excess returns realised by $t$,
    computed on an expanding window with the same twelve-month outcome lag.
  \item \emph{Set the position.} The target weight is the mean--variance
    exposure \eqref{eq:strat-weight}, truncated at zero (long-only): the
    strategy de-risks into cash when the signal implies a negative premium but
    never shorts the global bond portfolio. For reporting, each strategy's
    weight path is rescaled to an average exposure of one, which leaves the
    Sharpe ratio unchanged and makes the variance penalty in the CER
    comparable across strategies.
  \item \emph{Rebalance.} The portfolio is rebalanced to the target weight
    once a month. The one-way turnover of the month is
    $|w_{t}-w_{t-1}|$ times the portfolio notional; because the global cycle
    factor is highly persistent, target weights move slowly and turnover is
    low, a property quantified in \Cref{sec:strat-costs}.
\end{enumerate}

Two implementation details deserve emphasis. First, no step involves a choice
fitted to the evaluation sample: the risk-aversion parameter enters only
through the reported CER, the truncation at zero is imposed a priori, and the
sixty-month minimum training window is inherited from the out-of-sample
protocol of \Cref{sec:meth-oos} rather than tuned. Second, the strategy is
deliberately the \emph{simplest} implementation consistent with the evidence:
one asset, one signal, one linear forecasting regression. Anything the
strategy earns is therefore attributable to the real-time information content
of the global cycle factor, not to construction complexity.

\section{Performance}
\label{sec:strat-performance}

\Cref{tab:strat-perf} reports the out-of-sample performance over 2005--2024, the
period for which the fully-recursive factor is available. The global bond
portfolio itself has an out-of-sample $R^{2}$ of $0.125$ against the recursive
mean---higher than the pooled panel figure of \Cref{sec:res-oos}, because
aggregating the eleven national cycles into one global signal averages away
estimation noise. That statistical edge translates into economic value. Timing
the portfolio with the global cycle factor raises the annual Sharpe ratio from
$0.31$ for the passive position to $0.38$, and from $0.34$ for an investor who
times on the recursive mean. For a mean--variance investor with risk aversion
$\gamma=5$, the certainty-equivalent gain is $0.51\%$ per year over buy-and-hold
and $0.30\%$ over the mean-timing benchmark; the global cycle factor retains the
highest certainty equivalent at $\gamma=10$ as well.

The drawdown profile confirms that the value of the signal is defensive.

against $24.2\%$ for the mean-timing investor and $27.3\%$ for buy-and-hold,

three troughs occur in the 2021--2022 global hiking cycle, the deepest
bond-market drawdown in the sample; the timed investor enters it with reduced
exposure because the global cycle factor had already turned
(\Cref{fig:strat-exposure}), which is precisely the behaviour the mechanism
promises---the factor forecasts low premia when yields sit far below their
inflation anchor, as they did in 2021. Where the strategy trades, and what
that trading costs, is quantified in \Cref{sec:strat-costs}.

\section{Subperiods, Turnover, and Transaction Costs}
\label{sec:strat-costs}

A full-sample Sharpe ratio can conceal where a strategy earns its keep.
\Cref{tab:strat-sub} therefore splits the evaluation window in half and
isolates the 2021--2022 hiking cycle. The split is revealing, and it is worth
being candid about what it shows. In the strong bond decade of 2005--2014,
timing did \emph{not} pay on a risk-adjusted basis: the timed portfolio earned
a full percentage point a year more than buy-and-hold ($6.5\%$ against
$5.3\%$) but took proportionally more risk, so its Sharpe ratio was lower
($0.93$ against $1.09$)---when premia are persistently high, the best a
timing rule can do is stay invested, and any variation in exposure adds
volatility. The ranking reverses exactly where an investor needs it to: over
2015--2024, a decade in which the passive portfolio lost money, the timed
strategy preserves a higher mean and the least-bad Sharpe ratio, and in the
2021--2022 drawdown it loses least. The full-sample advantage of
\Cref{tab:strat-perf} is therefore not a uniform edge but a defensive one,
concentrated in the regime of falling and volatile bond prices---a
characterisation \Cref{fig:strat-rollsr} makes visible. In the rolling
five-year Sharpe ratios, the passive portfolio holds the upper hand in the
early, high-premium windows, the two series then track each other through the
middle of the sample, and the timed portfolio pulls ahead only as the hiking
cycle unfolds; across all windows the timed portfolio is on top in barely
half. The full-sample edge is real, but it is bought in a handful of
episodes, not accrued steadily.

The strategy's trading intensity is modest. Because the global cycle factor is
highly persistent, the target weight moves slowly: the average one-way
turnover of the timed portfolio is $9.2\%$ of notional per month
(mean-timing: $1.3\%$), roughly one full portfolio turn per year.
\Cref{tab:strat-costs} converts this into net-of-cost performance by charging
a proportional one-way cost on every unit of notional traded. At $10$ basis
points---already conservative for ten-year G10 government bond futures, where
quoted half-spreads are of the order of one basis point---the Sharpe ratio of
the timed strategy falls from $0.39$ to $0.37$ and the certainty-equivalent
gain over buy-and-hold shrinks by about ten basis points a year. Even at an
implausibly punitive $50$ basis points the timed strategy still matches or
beats buy-and-hold on both metrics, although at that cost level the
near-static mean-timing rule becomes the better implementation. Transaction
costs therefore qualify the magnitude of the economic value but do not
overturn its existence.

\section{The US-Dollar Investor and the FX-Adjusted Factor}
\label{sec:strat-fx}

A US-based investor who holds the global bond portfolio unhedged earns the dollar
excess return \eqref{eq:rx-usd}, which adds the currency return to the local bond
return. The natural signal for this investor is the FX-adjusted global cycle
factor $\FXGCF_{t}$, built in \Cref{sec:fw-fxgcf} to target dollar returns
directly. \Cref{tab:strat-usd} runs the timing rule of \Cref{sec:strat-design}
on the dollar portfolio, using the recursive $\FXGCF^{\mathrm{oos}}$ signal, with
the unadjusted $\GCF^{\mathrm{oos}}$ reported alongside to isolate the value of
the FX adjustment. The FX-adjusted signal shares the five-year recursive burn-in
of the global factor, so the dollar strategy is evaluated over the same
out-of-sample window (2005--2024) as the hedged strategy of
\Cref{sec:strat-performance}.

Two facts shape the result. First, over this sample the unhedged dollar bond
premium is modest but positive: the passive portfolio earns about $1.5\%$ a year
in excess return for a Sharpe ratio near $0.20$, as the strength of the dollar
through the 2010s offsets much---though not all---of the foreign-bond return for a
US investor. Second, the FX adjustment does what it was designed to do
\emph{statistically}: the FX-adjusted factor raises the out-of-sample $R^{2}$ to
$0.120$, above the $0.073$ of the unadjusted global factor, confirming that it
carries genuine incremental information about dollar returns (the in-sample
message of \Cref{sec:res-phase3}, now out of sample).

That statistical edge does not, however, translate into superior risk-adjusted
performance over the unadjusted factor. Because the FX-adjusted signal is noisier
in dollars than in local currency, the FXGCF-timed strategy runs a markedly higher
volatility and earns a Sharpe ratio of $0.24$---a clear improvement on the passive
and mean-timing benchmarks (both near $0.20$), but no better than the $0.25$ of
the simpler GCF-timed dollar strategy, and well below the $0.38$ of the
currency-hedged investor in \Cref{sec:strat-performance}. The conclusion is
unambiguous and consistent throughout the thesis: timing adds value for the dollar
investor too, but currency risk---not a failure of the cycle signal---caps the
level it can reach. The FX adjustment sharpens the forecast without raising the
risk-adjusted return, and the economic value of the global cycle factor accrues
overwhelmingly to the investor who hedges the currency exposure of the
international bond position.

\section{Caveats}
\label{sec:strat-caveats}

The result should be read as a proof of concept, not a tradable system, and
three limitations bound it. First, the magnitude is modest---a Sharpe-ratio
improvement of roughly $0.07$ and a certainty-equivalent gain below one percent a
year---which is typical of bond-return predictability and consistent with the
small out-of-sample $R^{2}$. Moreover, as \Cref{sec:strat-costs} shows, the
edge is defensive and episodic rather than uniform: it is earned in the poor
bond decade and the 2021--2022 drawdown, not in the calm years. Second, the
forecasts use overlapping twelve-month returns, so the supporting precision is
overstated relative to non-overlapping data, and subperiod statistics are
descriptive; transaction costs are quantified in \Cref{tab:strat-costs} and
erode, but do not eliminate, the advantage at realistic cost levels. Third,
because the global cycle
factor is a single series the strategy can only time aggregate exposure; a
cross-sectional strategy would require a factor with country-specific variation,
which the out-of-sample evidence does not support for the local factor. The
ceiling for richer constructions is, however, considerably higher:
\citet{randl2025} show that a full mean--variance-efficient portfolio across
hedged developed bond markets attains a Sharpe ratio above one, against an
average of $0.46$ for individual markets, and that the crucial input to such
constructions is precisely the expected-return forecast rather than the
covariance estimate. A real-time expected-return signal with the
out-of-sample stability documented here is therefore a natural candidate
input to cross-sectionally optimised portfolios, even though the present
chapter deliberately confines it to the simplest timing use. With
these caveats, the conclusion stands: the predictability identified in
\Cref{ch:results} is not merely statistical but carries genuine, if moderate,
economic value for a currency-hedged global bond investor. The broader
interpretation is taken up in \Cref{ch:discussion}.
