\chapter{Portfolio Construction}
\label{ch:strategy}

In \Cref{sec:res-oos} we established the one statistical result that matters
for an investor. Of the cycle factors, only the \emph{global} factor beats the
recursive prevailing mean out of sample. A positive out-of-sample $R^{2}$ is
precisely the condition under which a real-time investor can convert
predictability into utility \citep{campbellthompson2008}. In this chapter, we
ask whether this conversion is economically meaningful. To answer this
question, we build a fully out-of-sample market-timing strategy around the
global cycle factor and compare it against a passive benchmark. We keep the
exercise deliberately self-contained: we use only the recursive factor and
forecasts of \Cref{sec:meth-oos}, we take no position that an investor could
not have taken in real time, and we implement everything in
\texttt{strategy.R} and \texttt{strategy\_ext.R}.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Strategy Design}
\label{sec:strat-design}

Three features of our evidence guide the design of the strategy. First, the
predictability that survives out of sample is in \emph{local-currency}
returns, while the dollar signal is far weaker (\Cref{sec:res-phase3}). We
therefore run the strategy for a \emph{currency-hedged} global investor and
analyse the unhedged US-dollar investor separately in \Cref{sec:strat-fx}.
Local-currency excess returns, in which each bond position is funded in the
local money market, are the standard representation of an FX-risk-hedged
international bond portfolio and are equivalent to managing the currency
exposure with FX forwards \citep{randl2025}. Second, the global cycle factor
is a single time series, so it carries no information about which country to
favour and can only time \emph{aggregate} bond exposure. The investable asset
is accordingly a single GDP-weighted, ten-year global government-bond
portfolio, whose one-year excess return is the weighted average of the
national returns. Even this naive weighting already captures substantial
diversification benefits from imperfectly correlated term-structure movements
across markets \citep{randl2025}. Third, the signal must be usable in real
time, so both the factor and the forecasting regression respect time~$t$.

Each month, the investor forms a real-time forecast of the portfolio's
twelve-month excess return from the recursive regression
$\rx_{t+12}=a+b\,\GCF^{\mathrm{oos}}_{t}+\varepsilon$, estimated only on
outcomes realised by~$t$, and sets a mean--variance exposure
\begin{equation}
  w_{t} \;=\; \frac{1}{\gamma}\,
              \frac{\widehat{\E}_{t}\!\left[\rx_{t+12}\right]}
                   {\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{t}},
  \label{eq:strat-weight}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{t}$ is the recursive return variance and $\gamma$
the risk aversion. Because both the factor and the regression are recursive,
the exposure is ``doubly out-of-sample''. As benchmarks, we use the same
investor with the recursive prevailing-mean forecast, and a passive
buy-and-hold position. We report the Sharpe ratio, which is invariant to a
constant rescaling of $w_{t}$, as is. The certainty-equivalent (CER) return,
in contrast, is reported at equal average exposure across strategies so that
the variance penalty is comparable.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:strat-implementation}

In this section, we make the mapping from signal to position explicit. At the
end of each month $t$, the investor executes five steps, each of which uses
only information available at $t$.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), itemsep=2pt]
  \item \emph{Update the signal.} We re-estimate the entire
    generated-regressor chain on data through $t$. Trend inflation
    \eqref{eq:trendinf} is extended by the latest core-CPI observation, as it
    is backward-looking by construction. The yield--cycle decomposition
    \eqref{eq:decomp} is re-fit for every country--maturity pair. The local
    factor regressions \eqref{eq:cf-reg} are re-fit using only excess-return
    outcomes realised by $t$ (a twelve-month outcome lag, so the last usable
    observation is dated $t-12$). Finally, the local factors are aggregated
    with the GDP weights \eqref{eq:gcf} into the recursive global factor
    $\GCF^{\mathrm{oos}}_{t}$. Every layer requires a minimum training history
    of sixty months, which produces the five-year burn-in described in
    \Cref{sec:meth-oos}.
  \item \emph{Form the return forecast.} We re-estimate the forecasting
    regression $\rx_{t+12}=a+b\,\GCF^{\mathrm{oos}}_{t}+\varepsilon$ by OLS on
    the same lag-respecting training sample, and the forecast is
    $\widehat{\E}_{t}[\rx_{t+12}]=\hat a_{t}+\hat b_{t}\,\GCF^{\mathrm{oos}}_{t}$.
  \item \emph{Estimate the risk.} The variance $\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{t}$ is
    the sample variance of the portfolio's excess returns realised by $t$,
    which we compute on an expanding window with the same twelve-month outcome
    lag.
  \item \emph{Set the position.} The target weight is the mean--variance
    exposure \eqref{eq:strat-weight}, truncated at zero (long-only). The
    strategy therefore de-risks into cash when the signal implies a negative
    premium but never shorts the global bond portfolio. For reporting
    purposes, we rescale each strategy's weight path to an average exposure of
    one, which leaves the Sharpe ratio unchanged and makes the variance
    penalty in the CER comparable across strategies.
  \item \emph{Rebalance.} The portfolio is rebalanced to the target weight
    once a month. The one-way turnover of the month is
    $|w_{t}-w_{t-1}|$ times the portfolio notional. Because the global cycle
    factor is highly persistent, target weights move slowly and turnover is
    low, a property we quantify in \Cref{sec:strat-costs}.
\end{enumerate}

We want to emphasise two implementation details. First, no step involves a
choice that is fitted to the evaluation sample. The risk-aversion parameter
enters only through the reported CER, the truncation at zero is imposed a
priori, and the sixty-month minimum training window is inherited from the
out-of-sample protocol of \Cref{sec:meth-oos} rather than tuned. Second, we
deliberately choose the simplest implementation that is consistent with the
evidence, with one asset, one signal, and one linear forecasting regression.
Anything the strategy earns is therefore attributable to the real-time
information content of the global cycle factor and not to construction
complexity.

\section{Performance}
\label{sec:strat-performance}

In \Cref{tab:strat-perf}, we report the out-of-sample performance over
2005--2024, the period for which the fully-recursive factor is available. The
global bond portfolio itself has an out-of-sample $R^{2}$ of $0.125$ against
the recursive mean. This is higher than the pooled panel figure of
\Cref{sec:res-oos} because aggregating the eleven national cycles into one
global signal averages away estimation noise. We find that this statistical
edge translates into economic value. Timing the portfolio with the global
cycle factor raises the annual Sharpe ratio from $0.31$ for the passive
position to $0.38$, and from $0.34$ for an investor who times on the recursive
mean. For a mean--variance investor with risk aversion $\gamma=5$, the
certainty-equivalent gain is $0.51\%$ per year over buy-and-hold and $0.30\%$
over the mean-timing benchmark. The global cycle factor also retains the
highest certainty equivalent at $\gamma=10$.

The drawdown profile confirms that the value of the signal is defensive. In
\Cref{fig:strat-drawdown}, we plot the drawdown from peak of the same annual
wealth curves. The maximum drawdown of the timed portfolio is $21.9\%$,
against $24.2\%$ for the mean-timing investor and $27.3\%$ for buy-and-hold,
and the worst single twelve-month loss shrinks from $20.9\%$ to $19.1\%$. All
three troughs occur in the 2021--2022 global hiking cycle, the deepest
bond-market drawdown in the sample. The timed investor enters it with reduced
exposure because the global cycle factor had already turned
(\Cref{fig:strat-exposure}). This is precisely the behaviour we would expect
from the mechanism, as the factor forecasts low premia when yields sit far
below their inflation anchor, as they did in 2021. We quantify where the
strategy trades, and what this trading costs, in \Cref{sec:strat-costs}.

\section{Subperiods, Turnover, and Transaction Costs}
\label{sec:strat-costs}

A full-sample Sharpe ratio does not show us in which periods a strategy
actually generates its performance. In \Cref{tab:strat-sub}, we therefore
split the evaluation window in half and additionally isolate the 2021--2022
hiking cycle. The results of this split are revealing. In the strong bond
decade of 2005--2014, timing did not pay off on a risk-adjusted basis. The
timed portfolio earned a full percentage point a year more than buy-and-hold
($6.5\%$ against $5.3\%$) but took proportionally more risk, so its Sharpe
ratio was lower ($0.93$ against $1.09$). When premia are persistently high,
the best a timing rule can do is stay invested, and any variation in exposure
adds volatility. However, the ranking reverses exactly where an investor needs
it to. Over 2015--2024, a decade in which the passive portfolio lost money,
the timed strategy preserves a higher mean and the least-bad Sharpe ratio, and
in the 2021--2022 drawdown it loses least. The full-sample advantage of
\Cref{tab:strat-perf} is therefore not a uniform edge but a defensive one,
concentrated in the regime of falling and volatile bond prices. We can observe
this characterisation in \Cref{fig:strat-rollsr}. In the rolling five-year
Sharpe ratios, the passive portfolio holds the upper hand in the early,
high-premium windows, the two series then track each other through the middle
of the sample, and the timed portfolio pulls ahead only as the hiking cycle
unfolds. Across all windows, the timed portfolio is on top in barely half.
The full-sample edge is therefore real, but it is generated in a small number
of episodes rather than accrued steadily.

The trading intensity of the strategy is modest. Because the global cycle
factor is highly persistent, the target weight moves slowly. The average
one-way turnover of the timed portfolio is $9.2\%$ of notional per month
(mean-timing: $1.3\%$), which corresponds to roughly one full portfolio turn
per year. In \Cref{tab:strat-costs}, we convert this into net-of-cost
performance by charging a proportional one-way cost on every unit of notional
traded. At $10$ basis points, which is already conservative for ten-year G10
government bond futures, where quoted half-spreads are of the order of one
basis point, the Sharpe ratio of the timed strategy falls from $0.39$ to
$0.37$ and the certainty-equivalent gain over buy-and-hold shrinks by about
ten basis points a year. Even at an implausibly punitive $50$ basis points,
the timed strategy still matches or beats buy-and-hold on both metrics,
although at this cost level the near-static mean-timing rule becomes the
better implementation. Transaction costs therefore qualify the magnitude of
the economic value but do not overturn its existence.

\section{The US-Dollar Investor and the FX-Adjusted Factor}
\label{sec:strat-fx}

A US-based investor who holds the global bond portfolio unhedged earns the
dollar excess return \eqref{eq:rx-usd}, which adds the currency return to the
local bond return. The natural signal for this investor is the FX-adjusted
global cycle factor $\FXGCF_{t}$, which we built in \Cref{sec:fw-fxgcf} to
target dollar returns directly. In \Cref{tab:strat-usd}, we run the timing
rule of \Cref{sec:strat-design} on the dollar portfolio, using the recursive
$\FXGCF^{\mathrm{oos}}$ signal, and report the unadjusted
$\GCF^{\mathrm{oos}}$ alongside to isolate the value of the FX adjustment. The
FX-adjusted signal shares the five-year recursive burn-in of the global
factor, so we evaluate the dollar strategy over the same out-of-sample window
(2005--2024) as the hedged strategy of \Cref{sec:strat-performance}.

Two observations help us interpret the result. First, over this sample the
unhedged dollar bond premium is modest but positive. The passive portfolio
earns about $1.5\%$ a year in excess return for a Sharpe ratio near $0.20$, as
the strength of the dollar through the 2010s offsets much, though not all, of
the foreign-bond return for a US investor. Second, the two dollar signals
part ways at the aggregate level. Timing on the unadjusted recursive global
factor works: the GCF-timed dollar strategy earns a Sharpe ratio of $0.25$
against the passive $0.20$, with an aggregate-portfolio out-of-sample $R^{2}$
of $0.073$. The FX-adjusted factor, in contrast, does not time the
\emph{aggregate} dollar portfolio well. Its forecast of the GDP-weighted
portfolio return has an out-of-sample $R^{2}$ of $-0.01$, and the FXGCF-timed
strategy earns a Sharpe ratio of $0.19$, no better than buy-and-hold.

This is not a contradiction of Phase~III but a reflection of where the
FX-adjusted factor's strength lies. Its in-sample recovery
(\Cref{sec:res-phase3}) and its pooled out-of-sample edge
(\Cref{ch:robustness}) come from the per-country USD regressions, that is,
from the cross-section of dollar returns. Aggregating the portfolio before
forecasting averages away exactly the country-level currency variation the
factor is built to capture, and what remains is timed at least as well by the
simpler unadjusted factor. We therefore arrive at a clear conclusion that is
consistent throughout our thesis. Timing adds value for the dollar investor
as well, but currency risk, and not a failure of the cycle signal, caps the
level it can reach; both dollar strategies remain well below the $0.38$ of
the currency-hedged investor in \Cref{sec:strat-performance}. The economic
value of the global cycle factor accrues overwhelmingly to the investor who
hedges the currency exposure of the international bond position.

\section{Caveats}
\label{sec:strat-caveats}

We would suggest reading this result as a proof of concept rather than as a
tradable system, and we see three limitations that bound it. First, the
magnitude is modest, with a Sharpe-ratio improvement of roughly $0.07$ and a
certainty-equivalent gain below one percent a year, which is typical of
bond-return predictability and consistent with the small out-of-sample
$R^{2}$. Moreover, as we have seen in \Cref{sec:strat-costs}, the edge is
defensive and episodic rather than uniform, as it is earned in the poor bond
decade and the 2021--2022 drawdown and not in the calm years. Second, the
forecasts use overlapping twelve-month returns, so the supporting precision is
overstated relative to non-overlapping data, and the subperiod statistics are
descriptive. Transaction costs are quantified in \Cref{tab:strat-costs} and
erode, but do not eliminate, the advantage at realistic cost levels. Third,
because the global cycle factor is a single series, the strategy can only time
aggregate exposure. A cross-sectional strategy would require a factor with
country-specific variation, which the out-of-sample evidence does not support
for the local factor. However, the ceiling for richer constructions is
considerably higher. \citet{randl2025} show that a full
mean--variance-efficient portfolio across hedged developed bond markets
attains a Sharpe ratio above one, against an average of $0.46$ for individual
markets, and that the crucial input to such constructions is precisely the
expected-return forecast rather than the covariance estimate. A real-time
expected-return signal with the out-of-sample stability documented here is
therefore a natural candidate input to cross-sectionally optimised portfolios,
even though in this chapter we deliberately confine it to the simplest timing
use. With these caveats, we conclude that the predictability identified in
\Cref{ch:results} is not merely statistical but carries genuine, if moderate,
economic value for a currency-hedged global bond investor. We take up the
broader interpretation in \Cref{ch:discussion}.
