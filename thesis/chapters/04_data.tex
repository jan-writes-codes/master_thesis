\chapter{Data and Methodology}
\label{ch:data}
\label{ch:methodology}

In this chapter, I describe the data and develop the methodology of my
empirical analysis. I first describe the data I use to construct the cycle
factors and to run the predictive regressions of \Cref{ch:results}. I assemble
all series in a single workbook (\texttt{data.xlsx}) and process them into a
monthly country--maturity panel, implemented in \texttt{data\_preparation.R}. I
begin with the cross-sectional universe and the four raw inputs (yields,
inflation, GDP, and exchange rates) with their sources and units, then present
the sample coverage, my cleaning and alignment choices, and summary statistics.
I then develop the technical framework, which covers the excess-return
definitions and the expectations-hypothesis null they are tested against, the
cycle-factor hierarchy, and the predictive specifications. I close with the estimation and
inference used to evaluate them.

\section{Data and Variable Construction}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Universe and sources.}
My cross-section is the G10 government-bond universe, comprising eleven
countries. That is Belgium (BE), Canada (CA), Switzerland (CH), Germany (DE), France
(FR), the United Kingdom (GB), Italy (IT), Japan (JP), the Netherlands (NL),
Sweden (SE), and the United States (US). Five of these countries share the euro
(BE, DE, FR, IT, NL). The remaining currencies are the Canadian dollar (CAD),
Swiss franc (CHF), pound sterling (GBP), Japanese yen (JPY), Swedish krona
(SEK), and US dollar (USD). The US dollar serves as the home currency for the
international investor.

I obtain four raw inputs for my analysis, drawn from two data providers:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em, style=nextline]
  \item[Zero-coupon yields (Bloomberg).] I obtain end-of-month yields from
    Bloomberg's fitted zero-coupon government curves,
    one curve per country, at maturities of $1,2,4,5,9$ and $10$ years. Each
    country maps to a Bloomberg curve identifier (e.g.\ \texttt{I25} for the US,
    \texttt{I22} for the UK, \texttt{I18} for Japan), which I record in the
    workbook's \texttt{curve\_translation} sheet. Yields are continuously
    compounded and quoted in percent per annum.
  \item[Core CPI (FRED).] I use monthly seasonally adjusted core
    consumer price indices, one per country. The workbook stores the \emph{index level}.
    Japan is the one exception and I treat it explicitly. Its FRED core-CPI series
    ends in June 2021, and leaving it there would drop every Japanese observation
    after that date, and with them a market carrying roughly a fifth of the GDP
    weight, from the last three years of the estimation sample. I therefore extend the Japanese series after
    June 2021 with the LSEG core-CPI index, multiplied by the ratio of the two index
    levels in the splice month so that the level matches the FRED base and
    year-on-year inflation is continuous across the join. Over their common
    1990--2021 span the two series' year-on-year rates correlate $0.98$. No other
    country is spliced. I compute year-on-year
    inflation
    $\pi_{i,t}=\big(\mathrm{CPI}_{i,t}/\mathrm{CPI}_{i,t-12}-1\big)\times 100$
    in-sample, and trend inflation $\trendinf_{i,t}$ then follows from the EWMA
    in Equation~\eqref{eq:trendinf} below.
  \item[Nominal GDP (LSEG Refinitiv).] I use annual nominal GDP in local
    currency (e.g.\ \texttt{aUSWDALB5A}) to form the time-varying global weights
    $w_{i,t}$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:gcf} below. Before weighting, I convert GDP to a common
    US-dollar basis using year-end exchange rates.
  \item[Spot exchange rates (Bloomberg).] I obtain end-of-month spot rates
    quoted as US dollars per unit of foreign currency (\texttt{EURUSD},
    \texttt{CADUSD}, \texttt{JPYUSD}, \texttt{GBPUSD}, \texttt{SEKUSD},
    \texttt{CHFUSD}), with the USD rate fixed at unity. These series give the
    log currency return in the dollar-investor excess
    return of Equation~\eqref{eq:rx-usd} below and the FX conversion of GDP.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Sample and coverage.}
\label{sec:data-coverage}
My panel is monthly and sampled at month-end. Although the raw yield workbook
extends back to 1980 and the inflation indices to 1955, the usable span is
governed at the early end by the availability of each country's Bloomberg
zero-coupon curve and at the recent end by the annual GDP weights used for the
global factor. The United States and Japan enter the sample first, in March and
April 1989 respectively. The remaining nine countries begin in December 1994,
and the estimation sample runs through April 2024, although the raw yield curves
themselves extend to April 2026 (\Cref{fig:yield-ts}). This yields $422$ monthly
observations for the US, $421$ for Japan, and $353$ for each of the other
countries. The excess-return regressions use somewhat fewer after the factor
construction and the twelve-month return horizon. In
\Cref{tab:data-coverage} I summarise the effective coverage.

My panel is therefore unbalanced. I handle this throughout by computing
cross-country objects only over the countries that are present at each date.
The global weights $w_{i,t}$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:gcf} are, thus, renormalised each
month to sum to one over the available panel, and I compute the summary
correlations below on a pairwise-complete basis. For the five
euro-area members I apply the euro rate throughout, including the pre-1999
years before the single currency, so their pre-1999 dollar-investor currency
returns reflect a single vendor-backfilled euro series rather than the legacy
national currencies. In
\Cref{fig:coverage} I visualise the coverage, and in \Cref{fig:yield-ts} I
show the resulting yield series.

\paragraph{Cleaning and alignment.}
I align the raw inputs to a common monthly grid keyed on year--month and
reshape the panel to long form (country $\times$ maturity $\times$ month). Four
steps in my construction need spelling out:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{Trend inflation.} I compute year-on-year core inflation from
    the CPI index and smooth it with the backward-looking EWMA
    of Equation~\eqref{eq:trendinf}, with window $M=120$ months and decay $v=0.987$.
  \item \textbf{Cycles.} For each country--maturity pair, I project yields on
    trend inflation by ordinary least squares (OLS), as in
    Equation~\eqref{eq:decomp}. The residual is the cycle
    $\cyc^{(n)}_{i,t}$. The one-year cycle and the average cycle over
    $\{2,4,5,9,10\}$ years, Equation~\eqref{eq:cbar}, are the inputs to the cycle
    factor.
  \item \textbf{Excess returns.} I form one-year holding-period excess returns
    from Equation~\eqref{eq:rx} for $n\in\{2,5,10\}$ (the maturities for which the adjacent
    shorter yield $y^{(n-1)}$ is observed), standardise them by duration
    $D^{(n)}=n$, and average them as in
    Equation~\eqref{eq:rxbar}. For the dollar investor, I
    add the realised currency return and net out the US short rate,
    Equation~\eqref{eq:rx-usd}.
  \item \textbf{GDP weights.} I convert annual nominal GDP to US dollars at
    the year-end exchange rate and divide it by the cross-sectional total to
    obtain $w_{i,t}$. I hold the weights constant within a calendar year and
    renormalise them over the available panel.
\end{enumerate}

Missing values arise from the unbalanced panel and from the twelve-month leads
and lags in the return construction. I propagate them as \texttt{NA} and drop
them pairwise in each regression. The Japanese core-CPI splice described above is
the only place where I fill a gap rather than drop it.

\paragraph{Summary statistics.}
In \Cref{tab:dh-t1-summary} I report the mean and standard deviation of yields

across countries. First, average yield curves slope upward in maturity. Second,
volatility is broadly flat across the maturity spectrum. The short end is
somewhat more volatile in the United States, the United Kingdom, Italy, and
Japan, whereas in much of the euro area the long end is marginally more

In \Cref{tab:dh-t1-corr} I report the cross-country correlation matrix of the
ten-year yield, computed pairwise-complete over the full per-country sample.
Yields co-move strongly internationally. The correlations are
uniformly high and are tightest within the euro-area bloc (BE, DE, FR, IT, NL).
This co-movement is what motivates a global cycle
factor. If the levels of long yields share a common component across countries,
it seems plausible that the predictable component of their returns does so as
well. I examine this integration hypothesis in \Cref{ch:results}.
