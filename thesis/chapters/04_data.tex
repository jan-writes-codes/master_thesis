\chapter{Data and Methodology}
\label{ch:data}
\label{ch:methodology}

In this chapter, we describe the data and develop the methodology of our
empirical analysis. We first describe the data we use to construct the cycle
factors and to run the predictive regressions of \Cref{ch:results}. We assemble
all series in a single workbook (\texttt{data.xlsx}) and process them into a
monthly country--maturity panel, implemented in \texttt{data\_preparation.R}. We
begin with the cross-sectional universe and the four raw inputs (yields,
inflation, GDP, and exchange rates) with their sources and units, then present
the sample coverage, our cleaning and alignment choices, and summary statistics.
We then develop the , namely the expectations-hypothesis null,
the yield decomposition, the three-factor hierarchy, the excess-return
definitions, and the predictive specifications, and set out the estimation and
inference used to evaluate them.

\section{Universe and Sources}
\label{sec:data-sources}

Our cross-section is the G10 government-bond universe, comprising eleven
countries. That is Belgium (BE), Canada (CA), Switzerland (CH), Germany (DE), France
(FR), the United Kingdom (GB), Italy (IT), Japan (JP), the Netherlands (NL),
Sweden (SE), and the United States (US). Five of these countries share the euro
(BE, DE, FR, IT, NL). The remaining currencies are the Canadian dollar (CAD),
Swiss franc (CHF), pound sterling (GBP), Japanese yen (JPY), Swedish krona
(SEK), and US dollar (USD). The US dollar serves as the home currency for the
international investor.

We obtain four raw inputs for our analysis, drawn from two data providers:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em, style=nextline]
  \item[Zero-coupon yields (Bloomberg).] We obtain end-of-month yields from
    Bloomberg's fitted zero-coupon government curves,
    one curve per country, at maturities of $1,2,4,5,9$ and $10$ years. Each
    country maps to a Bloomberg curve identifier (e.g.\ \texttt{I25} for the US,
    \texttt{I22} for the UK, \texttt{I18} for Japan), which we record in the
    workbook's \texttt{curve\_translation} sheet. Yields are continuously
    compounded and quoted in percent per annum.
  \item[Core CPI (FRED).] We use monthly seasonally adjusted core
    consumer price indices, one per country. The workbook stores the \emph{index level}. We compute year-on-year
    inflation
    $\pi_{i,t}=\big(\mathrm{CPI}_{i,t}/\mathrm{CPI}_{i,t-12}-1\big)\times 100$
    in-sample, and trend inflation $\trendinf_{i,t}$ then follows from the EWMA
    in \eqref{eq:trendinf}.
  \item[Nominal GDP (LSEG Refinitiv).] We use annual nominal GDP in local
    currency (e.g.\ \texttt{aUSWDALB5A}) to form the time-varying global weights
    $w_{i,t}$ of \eqref{eq:gcf}. Before weighting, we convert GDP to a common
    US-dollar basis using year-end exchange rates.
  \item[Spot exchange rates (Bloomberg).] We obtain end-of-month spot rates
    quoted as US dollars per unit of foreign currency (\texttt{EURUSD},
    \texttt{CADUSD}, \texttt{JPYUSD}, \texttt{GBPUSD}, \texttt{SEKUSD},
    \texttt{CHFUSD}), with the USD rate fixed at unity. These series give us the
    log currency return in the dollar-investor excess
    return \eqref{eq:rx-usd} and the FX conversion of GDP.
\end{description}

\section{Sample and Coverage}
\label{sec:data-coverage}

Our panel is monthly and sampled at month-end. Although the raw yield workbook
extends back to 1980 and the inflation indices to 1955, the usable span is
governed at the early end by the availability of each country's Bloomberg
zero-coupon curve and at the recent end by the annual GDP weights used for the
global factor. The United States and Japan enter the sample first, in March and
April 1989 respectively. The remaining nine countries begin in December 1994,
and the estimation sample runs through April 2024, although the raw yield curves
themselves extend to April 2026 (\Cref{fig:yield-ts}). This yields $422$ monthly
observations for the US, $421$ for Japan, and $353$ for each of the other
countries; the excess-return regressions use somewhat fewer after the factor
construction and the twelve-month return horizon. In
\Cref{tab:data-coverage} we summarise the effective coverage.

Our panel is therefore unbalanced. We handle this throughout by computing
cross-country objects only over the countries that are present at each date.
The global weights $w_{i,t}$ in \eqref{eq:gcf} are, thus, renormalised each
month to sum to one over the available panel, and we compute the summary
correlations below on a pairwise-complete basis. For the five
euro-area members we apply the euro rate throughout, including the pre-1999
years before the single currency, so their pre-1999 dollar-investor currency
returns reflect a single vendor-backfilled euro series rather than the legacy
national currencies. In
\Cref{fig:coverage} we visualise the coverage, and in \Cref{fig:yield-ts} we
show the resulting 10-year yield series.

\section{Cleaning and Variable Construction}
\label{sec:data-cleaning}

We align the raw inputs to a common monthly grid keyed on year--month and
reshape the panel to long form (country $\times$ maturity $\times$ month). Four
of our construction steps deserve a note:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{Trend inflation.} We compute year-on-year core inflation from
    the CPI index and smooth it with the backward-looking EWMA
    \eqref{eq:trendinf} with window $M=120$ months and decay $v=0.987$.
  \item \textbf{Cycles.} For each country--maturity pair, we project yields on
    trend inflation by OLS \eqref{eq:decomp}. The residual is the cycle
    $\cyc^{(n)}_{i,t}$. The one-year cycle and the average cycle over
    $\{2,4,5,9,10\}$ years \eqref{eq:cbar} are the inputs to the cycle factor.
  \item \textbf{Excess returns.} We form one-year holding-period excess returns
    \eqref{eq:rx} for $n\in\{2,5,10\}$ (the maturities for which the adjacent
    shorter yield $y^{(n-1)}$ is observed), standardise them by duration
    $D^{(n)}=n$, and average them \eqref{eq:rxbar}. For the dollar investor, we
    add the realised currency return and net the US short rate
    \eqref{eq:rx-usd}.
  \item \textbf{GDP weights.} We convert annual nominal GDP to US dollars at
    the year-end exchange rate and divide it by the cross-sectional total to
    obtain $w_{i,t}$. We hold the weights constant within a calendar year and
    renormalise them over the available panel.
\end{enumerate}

Missing values arise from the unbalanced panel and from the twelve-month leads
and lags in the return construction. We propagate them as \texttt{NA} and drop
them pairwise in each regression, rather than imputing them.

\section{Summary Statistics}
\label{sec:data-summary}

In \Cref{tab:dh-t1-summary} we report the mean and standard deviation of yields
for every country--maturity pair. We can note two features that are consistent
across countries. First, average yield curves slope upward in maturity. Second,
volatility is broadly flat across the maturity spectrum. The short end is
somewhat more volatile in the United States, the United Kingdom, Italy, and
Japan, whereas in much of the euro area the long end is marginally more
volatile. In \Cref{fig:yield-curve-avg} we show the average curve shapes.

In \Cref{tab:dh-t1-corr} we report the cross-country correlation matrix of the
ten-year yield, computed pairwise-complete over the full per-country sample. We
can see that yields co-move strongly internationally. The correlations are
uniformly high and are tightest within the euro-area bloc (BE, DE, FR, IT, NL).
This is precisely the empirical regularity that motivates a \emph{global} cycle
factor. If the levels of long yields share a common component across countries,
it seems plausible that the predictable component of their returns does so as
well. We examine this integration hypothesis in \Cref{ch:results}.
