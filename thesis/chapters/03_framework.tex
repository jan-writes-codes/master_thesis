\section{Theoretical Framework}
\label{ch:framework}

In this section, we develop the theoretical framework that underlies our
empirical analysis. We start from the expectations hypothesis (EH) as a null of
no predictability. We then introduce the \citet{cieslak2015} decomposition of
yields into a slow-moving trend-inflation component and transitory
interest-rate cycles, and we build the three-factor hierarchy that organises
our thesis: the \emph{local} cycle factor $\CF_{i,t}$, the GDP-weighted
\emph{global} cycle factor $\GCF_{t}$, and the \emph{FX-adjusted} global cycle
factor $\FXGCF_{t}$ for a US-dollar investor. In the final subsection, we state
the predictive specifications that operationalise our research questions.

Throughout this section, $i$ indexes countries in the G10 cross-section
$\mathcal{G}$, time $t$ is measured in months, and bond maturity $n$ is
measured in years. The empirical maturity menu is $N=\{1,2,4,5,9,10\}$ years,
which is dictated by data availability (\Cref{ch:data}). All yields are
continuously compounded and expressed in percent per annum.

% ===========================================================================
\subsection{The Expectations Hypothesis as a Null}
\label{sec:fw-eh}

We let $y^{(n)}_{t}$ denote the time-$t$ continuously compounded yield on an
$n$-year zero-coupon bond and $p^{(n)}_{t}=-n\,y^{(n)}_{t}$ the corresponding log
price. Under the expectations hypothesis, the long yield equals the average
expected future short rate, up to a constant term premium:
\begin{equation}
  y^{(n)}_{t} \;\approx\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\E_{t}\!\left[y^{(1)}_{t+i}\right].
  \label{eq:eh}
\end{equation}
A direct implication of \eqref{eq:eh} is that bond excess returns are
unpredictable. In other words, the compensation for holding a longer-maturity
bond cannot be forecast using time-$t$ information. The one-year
holding-period excess return on an $n$-year bond,
\begin{equation}
  \rx^{(n)}_{t+12} \;=\; p^{(n-1)}_{t+12}-p^{(n)}_{t}-y^{(1)}_{t}
                  \;=\; n\,y^{(n)}_{t}-(n-1)\,y^{(n-1)}_{t+12}-y^{(1)}_{t},
  \label{eq:rx}
\end{equation}
should therefore satisfy $\E_{t}\!\left[\rx^{(n)}_{t+12}\right]=\text{const}$.
However, the empirical bond-pricing literature clearly rejects this
restriction. Forward rates \citep{cochrane2005} and, even more powerfully,
macro-anchored cycles \citep{cieslak2015} predict $\rx^{(n)}_{t+12}$ with
economically large $R^{2}$. In the remainder of this chapter, we build the
predictors that we use to document and interpret this predictability in an
international setting. We test them in \Cref{ch:results}, and we return to the
EH itself in \Cref{sec:meth-ehmc}, where it serves as a simulated benchmark for
the magnitude of predictive $R^{2}$.

\subsection{Yield Decomposition and Interest-Rate Cycles}
\label{sec:fw-decomp}

The central premise of \citet{cieslak2015} is that nominal yields are driven by
two forces. The first is a persistent \emph{trend-inflation} component
$\trendinf_{i,t}$ that anchors the level of interest rates over long horizons,
and the second consists of transitory \emph{cycles} $\cyc^{(n)}_{i,t}$ that
capture business-cycle conditions, real-rate movements, and variation in risk
appetite. For each country $i$ and maturity $n$, we decompose yields by the
linear projection
\begin{equation}
  y^{(n)}_{i,t} \;=\; \alpha_{i,n} + \beta_{i,n}\,\trendinf_{i,t}
                    + \cyc^{(n)}_{i,t},
  \qquad
  \cyc^{(n)}_{i,t} \;\equiv\; \varepsilon^{(n)}_{i,t},
  \label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
so that the cycle is, by definition, the residual of yields on trend inflation.
We estimate this projection separately for every country--maturity pair by
ordinary least squares.

We construct trend inflation as a backward-looking, exponentially weighted
moving average of realised year-on-year core inflation $\pi_{i,t}$:
\begin{equation}
  \trendinf_{i,t} \;=\; \frac{1-v}{1-v^{M}}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1} v^{j}\,\pi_{i,t-j},
  \qquad M=120,\quad v=0.987.
  \label{eq:trendinf}
\end{equation}
The window spans $M=120$ months (ten years), and the geometric decay
$v=0.987$ places the bulk of the weight on the most recent two to three years.
Because the average never looks forward, $\trendinf_{i,t}$ is observable at $t$
and introduces no look-ahead bias. The normalising constant $(1-v)/(1-v^{M})$
makes the weights sum to one.

\paragraph{Implementation note.} We run the decomposition in \eqref{eq:decomp}
country-by-country and maturity-by-maturity, so the loadings
$(\alpha_{i,n},\beta_{i,n})$, and thus the cycles, are local objects. The
cross-country structure only enters later, through the global factor. The decay
parameter $v=0.987$ follows \citet{cieslak2015}. In \Cref{ch:robustness}, we
examine the sensitivity of our results to this choice and to the window length
$M$.

\subsection{The Local Cycle Factor}
\label{sec:fw-localcf}

The cycles at different maturities are highly collinear but not identical.
\citet{cieslak2015} summarise the cross-section of cycles with two objects.
The first is the one-year cycle $\cyc^{(1)}_{i,t}$, which tracks the cyclical
position of the short rate, and the second is the \emph{average cycle}
\begin{equation}
  \cycbar_{i,t} \;=\; \frac{1}{|N\setminus\{1\}|}
                      \sum_{n\in N,\, n\neq 1}\cyc^{(n)}_{i,t},
  \label{eq:cbar}
\end{equation}
which captures the common, longer-maturity cyclical variation and attenuates
maturity-specific measurement error. We define the \emph{local cycle factor} as
the fitted value of the one-year-ahead excess return projected on these two
predictors:
\begin{align}
  \rxbar_{i,t+12} &= \gamma_{0} + \gamma_{i,1}\,\cyc^{(1)}_{i,t}
                     + \gamma_{i,2}\,\cycbar_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+12},
                     \label{eq:cf-reg}\\[2pt]
  \CF_{i,t}       &= \hat\gamma_{i,1}\,\cyc^{(1)}_{i,t}
                     + \hat\gamma_{i,2}\,\cycbar_{i,t}.
                     \label{eq:cf}
\end{align}
Here, $\rxbar_{i,t+12}$ is the duration-standardised, maturity-averaged excess
return that we define in \Cref{sec:fw-returns}. Equation \eqref{eq:cf} is the
empirical analogue of the single return-forecasting factor of
\citet{cochrane2005}, but it is anchored in the macroeconomic cycle rather than
in forward rates. $\CF_{i,t}$ is, by construction, the component of the cycle
cross-section that is relevant for predicting returns.

\paragraph{Implementation note.} The average cycle in \eqref{eq:cbar} excludes
the one-year maturity, which already enters \eqref{eq:cf-reg} separately as
$\cyc^{(1)}_{i,t}$. $\cycbar_{i,t}$ is, therefore, the mean cycle over
$\{2,4,5,9,10\}$ years. Because of this, we avoid double-counting the
short-rate cycle and mirror the role of the average forward rate in the
\citet{cochrane2005} factor.

\subsection{The Global Cycle Factor and GDP Weights}
\label{sec:fw-gcf}

If government-bond markets are internationally integrated, investors should be
compensated for exposure to a \emph{common} source of risk rather than for
country-specific fluctuations \citep{dahlquist2013}. To capture this idea, we
aggregate the local cycle factors into a single global factor. We use
time-varying GDP weights so that larger economies contribute proportionally
more:
\begin{equation}
  \GCF_{t} \;=\; \sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}} w_{i,t}\,\CF_{i,t},
  \qquad
  w_{i,t} \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{GDP}_{i,t}}
                     {\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{GDP}_{j,t}}.
  \label{eq:gcf}
\end{equation}
We recompute the weights $w_{i,t}$ each month over the set of countries that
are present in the estimation panel at $t$, so that $\sum_{i}w_{i,t}=1$ holds
by construction even when coverage is unbalanced (\Cref{sec:data-coverage}).
We convert nominal GDP to a common currency before weighting. The global
factor $\GCF_{t}$ is the international counterpart of the local $\CF_{i,t}$ and
provides us with a natural test of market integration. Under full integration,
the global factor should subsume the predictive content of the local factor
(\Cref{sec:fw-hypotheses}).

\subsection{Excess Returns: Local and US-Dollar Investor}
\label{sec:fw-returns}

\paragraph{Local-currency returns.}
The one-year excess return on an $n$-year bond for a domestic investor is given
by \eqref{eq:rx}. Because return volatility scales roughly with duration, we
follow \citet{cieslak2015} and work with \emph{duration-standardised} returns,
$\widetilde{\rx}^{(n)}_{i,t+12}=\rx^{(n)}_{i,t+12}/D^{(n)}$, and then average
across the maturity set to obtain a single, scale-comparable return series:
\begin{equation}
  \rxbar_{i,t+12} \;=\; \frac{1}{K}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}
                        \frac{\rx^{(n)}_{i,t+12}}{D^{(n)}},
  \qquad K=|\mathcal{N}|,\quad D^{(n)}=n.
  \label{eq:rxbar}
\end{equation}
The duration weight $D^{(n)}=n$ is the Macaulay duration of a continuously
compounded zero-coupon bond. For the construction, we use the averaging set
$\mathcal{N}=\{2,5,10\}$ years. These are the maturities for which a clean
one-year-ahead excess return is identified given the data menu, since each
requires the adjacent shorter yield $y^{(n-1)}$, i.e.\
$y^{(1)},y^{(4)},y^{(9)}$, which are all available.

\paragraph{Implementation note.} Our proposal specified a yield-adjusted
duration $D^{(n)}_{i,t}=n/(1+y^{(n)}_{i,t})$. However, under continuous
compounding the Macaulay duration of a zero-coupon bond is exactly $n$, so we
adopt $D^{(n)}=n$. This choice also keeps the standardisation free of the
contemporaneous yield and, therefore, exogenous to the return being scaled. As
\citet{cieslak2015} note, the results are insensitive to this convention, and a
simple (unstandardised) average leaves our conclusions unchanged.

\paragraph{US-dollar investor returns.}
We now consider a US investor who borrows for one year in US dollars, converts
to foreign currency, holds the foreign bond, and converts the proceeds back.
For this investor, the dollar excess return adds the realised currency return
and nets out the US short rate:
\begin{equation}
  \rx^{(n),\mathrm{USD}}_{i,t+12}
    \;=\; \rx^{(n)}_{i,t+12} + y^{(1)}_{i,t}
          + \big(s_{i,t+12}-s_{i,t}\big) - y^{(1)}_{\mathrm{US},t},
  \label{eq:rx-usd}
\end{equation}
where $s_{i,t}=\log(\text{USD per unit of currency }i)$, so that
$s_{i,t+12}-s_{i,t}$ is the one-year log currency return. We apply the same
duration-standardisation and maturity-averaging as in \eqref{eq:rxbar} to
\eqref{eq:rx-usd} and obtain $\rxbar^{\,\mathrm{USD}}_{i,t+12}$. This dollar
return is the object of interest for the international investor and the input
to the FX-adjusted factor below.

\subsection{The FX-Adjusted Global Cycle Factor}
\label{sec:fw-fxgcf}

A global factor built from local-currency returns ignores the currency risk
that an international investor bears. Because of this, we construct an
\emph{FX-adjusted global cycle factor} $\FXGCF_{t}$ that targets dollar
returns directly, in analogy to the FX-adjusted global factor (FXGCP) of
\citet{dahlquist2013}. We build the factor bottom-up, exactly as the global
factor in \eqref{eq:gcf}, but from country-level factors that are fitted to
dollar returns. For each country, we first estimate the \emph{USD cycle
factor} $\FXCF_{i,t}$ as the fitted value of the country's dollar excess
return on its own cycle predictors:
\begin{align}
  \rxbar^{\,\mathrm{USD}}_{i,t+12}
     &= \delta_{i,0} + \delta_{i,1}\,\cyc^{(1)}_{i,t}
        + \delta_{i,2}\,\cycbar_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+12},
        \label{eq:fxcf-reg}\\[2pt]
  \FXCF_{i,t} &= \hat\delta_{i,1}\,\cyc^{(1)}_{i,t}
              + \hat\delta_{i,2}\,\cycbar_{i,t}.
        \label{eq:fxcf}
\end{align}
We then aggregate the national USD cycle factors with the same GDP weights as
in \eqref{eq:gcf}:
\begin{equation}
  \FXGCF_{t} \;=\; \sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}} w_{i,t}\,\FXCF_{i,t}.
  \label{eq:fxgcf}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Implementation note.} Two design choices deserve comment. First,
\eqref{eq:fxcf-reg}--\eqref{eq:fxgcf} mirror the construction of the global
factor exactly. $\FXGCF_{t}$ is to the dollar returns what $\GCF_{t}$ is to
the local-currency returns, so any difference between the two factors can be
attributed to the return currency alone. Second, an alternative is the
``top-down'' construction of \citet{dahlquist2013}, in which the GDP-weighted
average dollar return is projected on the GDP-weighted average cycle
predictors. We examined this variant and found that it is nearly collinear
with the unadjusted global factor in our sample (correlation $0.99$), so it
carries almost no incremental information about currency risk. We therefore
adopt the bottom-up construction as our main specification and compare the
two constructions in \Cref{sec:rob-fxgcf-construction}. A third
specification, which our proposal wrote as
$\rxbar^{\,\mathrm{USD}}_{t+12}=\delta_{0}+\delta_{1}\GCF_{t}+\varepsilon_{t+12}$,
would make $\FXGCF_{t}$ an affine transform of $\GCF_{t}$ and, hence,
statistically indistinguishable from it; we do not pursue it.

\subsection{Hypotheses and Predictive Specifications}
\label{sec:fw-hypotheses}

In this final subsection, we map the three-factor hierarchy onto our research
questions through a sequence of one-year-ahead predictive regressions. We
estimate each of them with the overlapping-return inference described in
\Cref{sec:meth-inference}.

\paragraph{Phase~I --- Does the cycle factor predict returns locally?}
For each country, we estimate
\begin{equation}
  \rxbar_{i,t+12} = \alpha_{i} + \beta_{i}\,\CF_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+12},
  \label{eq:h-local}
\end{equation}
and test $H_{0}\!:\beta_{i}=0$. A rejection of this null establishes that the
macro-anchored cycle mechanism of \citet{cieslak2015} also operates outside the
United States.

\paragraph{Phase~II --- Does the global factor subsume the local factor?}
We run the horse race
\begin{align}
  \rxbar_{i,t+12} &= \alpha_{i} + \beta_{i}\,\CF_{i,t}
                     + \gamma_{i}\,\GCF_{t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+12},
                     \label{eq:h-horse}\\
  \rxbar_{i,t+12} &= \alpha_{i} + \gamma_{i}\,\GCF_{t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+12}.
                     \label{eq:h-global}
\end{align}
Under full market integration, the global factor should drive out the local
factor. In this case, adding $\CF_{i,t}$ to \eqref{eq:h-global} should not
raise the predictive power, i.e.\ $\beta_{i}=0$ in \eqref{eq:h-horse}. To make
this comparison clean, we orthogonalise the local factor with respect to the
global factor before the joint regression, so that $\beta_{i}$ measures the
incremental local content.

\paragraph{Phase~III --- Does currency risk break the model, and does the
FX-adjusted factor restore it?}
For the dollar investor, we estimate
\begin{align}
  \rxbar^{\,\mathrm{USD}}_{i,t+12}
     &= \alpha_{i} + \beta_{i}\,\CF_{i,t}
        + \gamma_{i}\,\GCF_{t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+12},
        \label{eq:h-usd-local}\\
  \rxbar^{\,\mathrm{USD}}_{i,t+12}
     &= \alpha_{i} + \gamma_{i}\,\GCF_{t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+12},
        \label{eq:h-usd-global}\\
  \rxbar^{\,\mathrm{USD}}_{i,t+12}
     &= \alpha_{i} + \delta_{i}\,\FXGCF_{t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+12}.
        \label{eq:h-fxgcf}
\end{align}
With specifications \eqref{eq:h-usd-local}--\eqref{eq:h-usd-global}, we ask
whether the local-currency factors retain their predictive power once currency
risk is introduced. With \eqref{eq:h-fxgcf}, we ask whether the purpose-built
FX-adjusted factor $\FXGCF_{t}$ recovers the predictability that an unadjusted
global factor loses on currency-converted returns. Together,
\eqref{eq:h-local}--\eqref{eq:h-fxgcf} constitute the empirical programme that
we test in \Cref{ch:results} and stress-test in \Cref{ch:robustness}.
