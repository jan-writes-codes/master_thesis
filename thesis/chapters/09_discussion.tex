\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

\section{Economic Interpretation}
\label{sec:disc-econ}


The central empirical result of \Cref{ch:results} is that the trend--cycle
mechanism of \citet{cieslak2015} is not a property of one market but of the
asset class as a whole. In every G10 market, the deviation of yields from
their trend-inflation anchor predicts next year's excess return with the same
sign structure, namely a negative loading on the one-year cycle and a
positive loading on the average cycle. Furthermore, the loadings are flat to
rising across the maturity curve, which is what we would expect from a single
premium that prices the whole curve. We would suggest the same reading that
\citet{cieslak2015} give for the United States, now with cross-sectional
support. Trend inflation pins down where investors expect yields to settle,
so the distance of today's curve from this anchor measures the compensation
demanded for holding duration, and this compensation mean-reverts at
business-cycle frequency. The uniformity across eleven countries with
different monetary histories seems hard to reconcile. It is, however, exactly what we would expect if the mechanism is
fundamental, as the falling-stars evidence of \citet{bauer2020} implies. Once
the slow-moving macro anchors are accounted for, the risk premium is
contained in the cyclical deviation.

The comparison with the forward-rate factor sharpens this interpretation. In
sample, the \citet{cochrane2005} factor fits better than the cycle factor
across the G10 ($R^{2}$ of $0.38$ against $0.25$ on average), which we would
expect from a six-predictor combination of prices compared to a two-predictor
one. Out of sample, however, the ranking reverses. Of the four
candidate factors only the global cycle factor beats the recursive
historical mean (\Cref{sec:rob-cf-cp}). We would suggest that the macro
anchoring is therefore essential to the result. Our evidence thus suggests that
the cycle performs better in real time.

\bigskip
Our second finding is that a single GDP-weighted global cycle factor matches
the explanatory power of eleven national factors and drives the local factor
out in eight of eleven markets. This re-establishes the integration result
of \citet{dahlquist2013} on the macro-anchored factor family. The economic
content of this finding is that the predictable component of government-bond
returns is compensation for a common risk. Bond risk premia in the G10 rise
and fall together, and a domestic investor in, for example, Sweden is
rewarded for exposure to the global cycle, not to a Swedish one. Economically, the
inputs to the factor are themselves synchronised. Inflation trends fell
together across the G10 over the sample, business cycles co-move, and
monetary policy responded to common shocks, so the national cycles share
their informative variation.

We consider the exceptions as informative as the rule. The local factor
retains incremental content in Italy, the Netherlands, and Belgium,
concentrated in periods of sovereign stress, which suggests that integration
is conditional on the prevailing regime rather than a universal property. When sovereign-credit risk reprice a market, the
compensation becomes local again, and a GDP-weighted factor dominated by the
core economies cannot price it alone. We take this conditionality as a
refinement of \citet{dahlquist2013} rather than a contradiction.

\bigskip
Our third finding refines the comparison with the forward-rate literature.
For \citet{dahlquist2013}, adjusting the global factor for currency risk
matters. Their FX-adjusted forward factor correlates only about $0.50$ with
the unadjusted one and recovers dollar-investor predictability. Once we
build the cycle-factor analogue bottom-up, it behaves in a comparable way:
our baseline $\FXGCF_{t}$ correlates $0.81$ with the unadjusted global
factor and substantially raises the dollar investor's in-sample fit
(\Cref{sec:res-phase3}). However, two qualifications separate the cycle
family from the forward family. First, the recovery depends on the
construction. The top-down variant, which projects the aggregate dollar
return on the aggregate cycle predictors, is nearly collinear with the
unadjusted factor (correlation $0.99$) and adds little
(\Cref{sec:rob-fxgcf-construction}). The currency-relevant information in
the cycles therefore lives in the cross-section of the country-level USD
regressions and is lost once the aggregation happens before the fit.
Second, the in-sample recovery translates into only a small and real-time edge, and it does not help in timing the
aggregate dollar portfolio (\Cref{ch:strategy}). A possible economic
explanation could lie in what each factor family retains. The forward-rate
factor is built from the level and shape of the forward curve, and
cross-country differences in rate levels are exactly the interest
differentials that forecast currency returns through the failure of
uncovered interest parity \citep{fama1984}, so an FX adjustment of a forward factor has
carry-relevant information to work with even at the aggregate level. The
cycle factor, in contrast, removes the level by construction, because
detrending by trend inflation strips out the persistent component and keeps the transitory deviation. The currency-relevant
cyclical information that remains is country-specific and regime-dependent,
which is why it shows up strongly in the in-sample cross-section, weakly in
real time, and hardly at all in the aggregate. The regime evidence supports
this reading, as the dollar-investor predictability is concentrated before
2008, when rate differentials were wide, and disappears in the post-crisis
regime of compressed, synchronised policy rates (\Cref{sec:rob-subsample}).
The FX adjustment seems to work well only in the high-rate regime, in
which cross-country differentials give currency returns something predictable
to say. In the low-rate regime that fills the second half of our sample, the
currency leg collapses onto the bond cycle and the adjustment becomes close to
redundant. When the two premia play different roles is thus itself the answer to
why the FX adjustment matters so much less for the cycle family, in our sample,
than for the forward family in \citet{dahlquist2013}.

\bigskip
When we assemble the four findings, our evidence reduces to one robust
result and a set of conditional ones. The GDP-weighted global cycle factor
predicts international bond returns in sample, out of sample, and in every
subsample except the 2008 panic. Furthermore, it beats the forward-rate
alternative in real time and carries economic value for a hedged investor
(\Cref{ch:strategy}). The local factors are real in sample but, for nine of
eleven markets, cannot be estimated precisely enough to use in real time. Their out-of-sample failure reflects the estimation noise in eleven
separately fitted national regressors rather than the absence of an
in-sample signal \citep{cochrane2006}.
The dollar-investor factors, in turn, depend strongly on the prevailing
currency regime. We would therefore not summarise our results as ``the
cycle factor works everywhere'' but in a more specific way. International bond risk premia contain a common,
macro-anchored, slowly-moving component that is large enough and stable
enough to estimate in real time, provided that we aggregate across markets,
measure the inflation anchor cleanly, and do not use it to forecast
exchange rates.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}

In the process of writing our thesis we faced four limitations that qualify
these conclusions. We assume that none of them overturns our results, but
each of them limits how far the conclusions should be pushed.

\bigskip
All our headline $t$-statistics use $18$-lag Newey--West HAC standard errors
\citep{neweywest1987}, whereas \citet{cieslak2015} use the
reverse-regression delta method of \citet{weiwright2013}, which requires the
underlying monthly returns and is not identified from the twelve-month
overlapping panel we construct (\Cref{sec:meth-inference}). For highly
persistent regressors the two can diverge, and HAC inference at long
horizons is known to over-reject in finite samples \citep{hodrick1992}. A
residual upward bias in the in-sample significance cannot be excluded. Because
of this, we base our conclusions primarily on the out-of-sample evidence.

\bigskip
Our panel covers
1989/1994--2024, which corresponds to roughly thirty years of monthly data.
The sample is also unbalanced (the nine non-US, non-Japanese markets begin only
in December 1994), and the five euro-area members share a currency and a
monetary policy from 1999 (and, before then, a single vendor-backfilled euro
exchange rate in place of their legacy currencies), so the eleven markets
contribute far fewer than eleven independent observations of the ``global''
cycle. The GDP-weighted
factor is to a significant degree a US--euro-area--Japan factor, and the
integration finding should be read accordingly. Alternative weightings
(equal, market-capitalisation, principal-component) were not explored.

\bigskip
Finally, the robustness
chapter itself draws the sharpest boundaries. The global factor's
out-of-sample edge is a property of expanding-window estimation and
disappears under ten- and fifteen-year rolling windows
(\Cref{sec:rob-oos-scheme}). Furthermore, it depends materially on measuring
the trend with core rather than headline inflation
(\Cref{sec:rob-core-headline}), and it fails inside the 2008 crisis window.
The FX-adjusted factor's small pooled gain is earned mainly in the 2008
window and is sensitive to the training minimum. 

\section{Implications}
\label{sec:disc-implications}

\paragraph{For the international bond investor.} The portfolio prescription
that follows from our evidence has three parts. First, we would advise
hedging the currency. The predictable component of international bond
returns is found in local-currency space. Dollar conversion destroys roughly
two-thirds of it in sample and almost all of it out of sample, and the
timing strategy's Sharpe ratio falls from $0.40$ hedged to about $0.24$
unhedged (\Cref{ch:strategy}). From the standpoint of cycle-based
predictability, currency exposure in a G10 bond portfolio therefore adds
risk without adding compensation. Second, we would advise timing the
aggregate rather than the cross-section. The factor that survives in real
time is a single global series. It supports varying overall duration
exposure, which raised the hedged Sharpe ratio from $0.34$ to $0.40$ with
a certainty-equivalent gain of about half a percent a year, achieved mainly
by avoiding drawdowns such as 2022. However, the out-of-sample evidence
gives no support for country selection on local cycle factors. Third, the
limits of the factor should be respected. It is slow, it requires a long
estimation history, and it offers no protection in a flight-to-quality
panic, when its real-time forecasts were at their worst.

\paragraph{For the term-premium literature.} Our findings carry two
messages back to the research literature. First, they add G10 evidence that
term premia are anchored by slow macro trends. This is the international
counterpart of the falling-stars mechanism of \citet{bauer2020} and a
factor-level complement to the trend-augmented forecasting of
\citet{zhang2021}: it is the deviation from the trend, aggregated globally,
that is priced, and it is priced in common across markets. Second, our
findings suggest that the in-sample horse race between forward-rate and
macro-anchored predictors is the wrong contest. In sample the forward
factor wins, while in real time only the macro-anchored global factor
survives. Studies that evaluate candidate state variables purely in sample
will therefore systematically favour price-based over macro-anchored
predictors. Out-of-sample evaluation reverses this verdict. A next step, which lies
beyond the scope of this thesis, would be to embed the global cycle in a
no-arbitrage term structure model with a shared international trend. This
would close the gap between the reduced-form evidence we assemble here and
the structural falling-stars framework.
