\chapter{Replication and Validation}
\label{ch:replication}

Before I take the macro-anchored cycle factor to the G10, I first have to show
that my pipeline produces comparable results. In this appendix, I therefore validate the
entire pipeline, namely the trend-inflation construction \eqref{eq:trendinf}, the
yield--cycle decomposition \eqref{eq:decomp}, the cycle factor \eqref{eq:cf}, the
forward-rate factor of \citet{cochrane2005}, and the global aggregation
\eqref{eq:gcf}, against the two studies it builds on. In \Cref{sec:rep-cp}, I
reproduce the United States evidence of \citet{cieslak2015}, who introduced the
cycle mechanism. In \Cref{sec:rep-dh}, I reproduce the international evidence of
\citet{dahlquist2013}, who established the global integration of bond risk premia
through the forward-rate factor. This exercise is, however, not a mechanical
re-run, since my data, my sample window, and my inference differ from the
originals. The relevant test is therefore agreement on signs, magnitudes. I restrict the United States return regressions to January
1990--December 2014 ($300$ months) to align with the window of
\citet{cieslak2015}. The yield--cycle decomposition of \Cref{tab:cp-t1}, which
needs no returns, uses the full US yield history from March 1989. Inference uses the block bootstrap of
\Cref{sec:meth-inference} and Newey--West HAC standard errors, with $18$ lags
for the Cieslak--Povala tables and $12$ lags for the Dahlquist--Hasseltoft
tables, matching each original specification.

\section{Cieslak--Povala (2015)}
\label{sec:rep-cp}

The cycle mechanism starts by splitting each yield into a slow trend component,
driven by trend inflation, and a transitory cycle. In \Cref{tab:cp-t1} I
reproduce Table~1 of \citet{cieslak2015}. Panel~A regresses each US yield on
trend inflation \eqref{eq:decomp}: the slope is large and significant at every
maturity ($1.63$--$1.88$, HAC $t$ from $5.8$ to $12.8$), and the share of yield
variation the trend explains rises from $58\%$ (one year) to $84\%$ (ten).
\Cref{fig:s2-yield-decomp} shows the same split for four representative markets
(Germany, the UK, Japan, and the US).

Panel~B confirms a single cycle factor: adjacent-maturity cycle correlations are
near unity ($0.99$) and fall to $0.74$ between the one- and ten-year cycles, and
the cycles are far less persistent than the yields (half-life $55\to10$ months
versus $\sim46$--$56$).

The fitted factor $\CF_{t}$ \eqref{eq:cf} correlates $0.69$ with the average
cycle in my US sample, close to the $0.61$ of \citet{cieslak2015}
(\Cref{fig:s3-cbar-cf}).

\section{Dahlquist--Hasseltoft (2013)}
\label{sec:rep-dh}

The international step rests on a different factor. \citet{dahlquist2013} work
with the forward-rate return-forecasting factor of \citet{cochrane2005} rather
than with the macro-anchored cycle. They build a GDP-weighted global version of
it and show that the global factor subsumes the local ones, which is the
integration result that I later re-establish for the cycle factor. By validating
their evidence, I therefore also validate my implementation of the
Cochrane--Piazzesi factor and of the global aggregation. The G10 yields
underlying this exercise match the summary statistics of their Table~1 in level
and dispersion: average yields rise with maturity in every market, they are
highest in Italy and the United States and lowest in Switzerland and Japan, and
the ten-year yields are strongly correlated across countries. The latter is the
data feature that makes a common factor plausible.

\citet{dahlquist2013}, Table~3, documents that the local forward factors
correlate strongly with the GDP-weighted global factor. In \Cref{tab:dh-t3} I
reproduce this, with local--global correlations of $0.76$--$0.97$ (highest for
the large core markets, lowest for Belgium and Italy).

\Cref{tab:dh-t7} reproduces Table~7, the US-dollar investor's regressions. The
unadjusted global forward factor predicts dollar returns only weakly, whereas the
dollar-return global factor, built top-down on the average dollar return, restores

collinear with the unadjusted global cycle factor in the G10, which is why I
build my dollar-return cycle factor bottom-up instead (\Cref{sec:fw-fxgcf},

Taken together, the two replications establish that my engine reproduces both
pillars: the macro-anchored cycle mechanism of \citet{cieslak2015} on US data
and the global-integration evidence of \citet{dahlquist2013} on the G10,
including the dollar-return construction. The differences from the originals, namely a
sample beginning in 1990 and HAC and bootstrap inference, do not disturb the qualitative agreement. With the engine
validated, I turn in \Cref{ch:results} to applying the macro-anchored cycle
factor, rather than the forward-rate factor, to the international cross-section.
