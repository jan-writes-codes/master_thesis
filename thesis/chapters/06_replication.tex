\chapter{Replication and Validation}
\label{ch:replication}

Before we take the macro-anchored cycle factor to the G10, we first have to show
that our estimation engine is sound. In this appendix, we therefore validate the
entire pipeline, namely the trend-inflation construction \eqref{eq:trendinf}, the
yield--cycle decomposition \eqref{eq:decomp}, the cycle factor \eqref{eq:cf}, the
forward-rate factor of \citet{cochrane2005}, and the global aggregation
\eqref{eq:gcf}, against the two studies it builds on. In \Cref{sec:rep-cp}, we
reproduce the United States evidence of \citet{cieslak2015}, who introduced the
cycle mechanism. In \Cref{sec:rep-dh}, we reproduce the international evidence of
\citet{dahlquist2013}, who established the global integration of bond risk premia
through the forward-rate factor. This exercise is, however, not a mechanical
re-run, since our data, our sample window, and our inference differ from the
originals. The relevant test is therefore agreement on signs, magnitudes, and
explanatory power rather than on every decimal. Where our numbers differ, we
state the reason. We restrict the United States replication to January
1990--December 2014 ($300$ months) to align with the window of
\citet{cieslak2015}; all inference uses the $18$-lag Newey--West HAC standard
errors and the block bootstrap of \Cref{sec:meth-inference}.

\section{Cieslak--Povala (2015)}
\label{sec:rep-cp}

The cycle mechanism starts by splitting each yield into a slow trend component,
driven by trend inflation, and a transitory cycle. In \Cref{tab:cp-t1}, we
reproduce Table~1 of \citet{cieslak2015}. In Panel~A, we regress each United
States yield on trend inflation $\tau^{\mathrm{CPI}}_{t}$ \eqref{eq:decomp}. We
can see that the slope is large and highly significant at every maturity (between
$1.63$ and $1.88$, with HAC $t$-statistics from $5.8$ at one year to $12.8$ at
ten) and that the fraction of yield variation explained by trend inflation rises
monotonically with maturity, from $58\%$ at one year to $84\%$ at ten. This is
the central premise of the decomposition. The long end of the curve is tied to
the persistent inflation trend, and the remaining part, the cycle, is the part of
the yield that carries cyclical, return-relevant information. In
\Cref{fig:s2-yield-decomp}, we can see this split for the United States, where
the fitted trend tracks the low-frequency movement of the ten-year yield and the
cycle oscillates around it.

Panel~B confirms that the cycles share a single factor, exactly as in the
original study. We can see that the cross-maturity correlations are near unity
for adjacent points ($0.99$ for the one- and two-year cycles) and decline only
gradually toward the ends of the curve ($0.74$ between the one- and ten-year
cycles). Thus, a small number of common components captures the cyclical
variation across the entire term structure. Furthermore, the cycles are markedly
less persistent than the yields they come from. The cycle standard deviation
falls from $1.6\%$ at one year to $0.8\%$ at ten, and the cycle half-life
shortens from $55$ months at the short end to $10$ months at the long end,
whereas the yields themselves mean-revert far more slowly (half-lives around
$46$--$56$ months at every maturity). The decomposition therefore does what it is
meant to do. It strips out the highly persistent, inflation-driven level and
isolates a faster-moving, mean-reverting cyclical residual.

When we collapse the two cycle predictors into a single return-forecasting
factor, we reproduce the central diagnostic of \citet{cieslak2015}. Their
Figure~2 shows that the fitted cycle factor $\CF_{t}$ \eqref{eq:cf} is highly
correlated with the average cycle $\cycbar_{t}$. In our United States sample, the
correlation is $0.69$, which is close to the $0.61$ they report and, if anything,
slightly stronger (\Cref{fig:s3-cbar-cf}). The factor is therefore well
approximated by a single cyclical summary, which is the property that makes a
one-factor representation of bond risk premia possible.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/s3_us_cbar_vs_cf.pdf}
  \caption{The return-forecasting cycle factor $\CF_{t}$ against the average cycle
    $\cycbar_{t}$ for the United States. The correlation is $0.69$, compared to the
    $0.61$ reported by \citet{cieslak2015}, Figure~2.}
  \label{fig:s3-cbar-cf}
\end{figure}

In \Cref{tab:cp-t2a}, we reproduce the predictive regressions of Table~2,
Panel~A, which establish that trend inflation carries information about future
returns over and above the yield curve. Here, we regress the maturity-averaged
excess return on five competing predictor sets. We can see that the forward curve
alone explains $25\%$ of the return variation, while adding trend inflation lifts
this to $46\%$, with a strongly significant negative loading on the trend
($t=-3.6$). The persistent inflation component is therefore not redundant with
the cross-section of yields. The two-predictor cycle representation of
column~(5), which uses the one-year cycle and the average cycle, recovers $27\%$
with both loadings significant ($t=-3.6$ on the short cycle and $t=4.1$ on the
average cycle) and with the opposite-signed structure that defines the mechanism.
Furthermore, the Bayesian information criterion, expressed as a relative
posterior probability \citep[][\Cref{sec:meth-inference}]{cieslak2015}, places
essentially all the weight on the yields-plus-trend specification. This confirms
that the gain from conditioning on trend inflation is not an artefact of adding
free parameters.

Because these regressions use overlapping returns and generated regressors, we
have to read the $R^{2}$ against the distribution it would take under the
expectations hypothesis. In \Cref{tab:cp-t2b}, we report the Monte Carlo of
\Cref{sec:meth-ehmc}. Here, we simulate data from a calibrated
expectations-hypothesis economy in which yields carry no risk-premium
predictability, and we run the predictive regression on each simulated path. At
the sample length of the original study, the $95$th percentile of the null
$R^{2}$ ranges from about $4\%$ to $13\%$ across calibrations. At our shorter
$300$-month window, the null distribution is mechanically wider, with the $95$th
percentile reaching $15\%$--$19\%$. Either way, the in-sample $R^{2}$ documented
above, $27\%$ to $46\%$, sits far in the right tail of this distribution. Thus,
the predictability is not an artefact of overlapping returns or of projecting
onto a fitted regressor. We note, consistent with the scope set out in
\Cref{sec:meth-ehmc}, that our Monte Carlo reproduces the shape and tail
behaviour of the null but is not calibrated to match the exact percentiles that
\citet{cieslak2015} report. However, the qualitative conclusion that the observed
fit is far beyond what the expectations hypothesis can generate is robust to this
gap, which we document rather than close.

Finally, in \Cref{tab:cp-t4} we reproduce Table~4, in which the individual excess
return at each maturity is predicted with the single cycle factor. We can see
that the factor loads positively and significantly at every maturity, with $0.69$
at two years ($t=2.5$), $1.37$ at five ($t=3.9$), and $1.42$ at ten ($t=5.1$).
The loadings rise across the curve, which is the signature of a single
return-forecasting factor that prices the whole term structure rather than one
segment. Additionally, the block-bootstrap confidence bands for the HAC
$t$-statistic \citep{politis1994} exclude zero at every maturity. The
decomposition in the lower panels confirms the sign structure of the mechanism.
The one-year cycle enters negatively and the longer cycle positively, and the
long-maturity return is the most predictable ($R^{2}$ of $32\%$ at ten years).
Similar to the results of \citet{cieslak2015}, we thus reproduce the United
States evidence in full: the trend--cycle decomposition, the single-factor
structure of the cycles, the incremental predictive content of trend inflation,
and the cycle factor that prices the curve.

\section{Dahlquist--Hasseltoft (2013)}
\label{sec:rep-dh}

The international step rests on a different factor. \citet{dahlquist2013} work
with the forward-rate return-forecasting factor of \citet{cochrane2005} rather
than with the macro-anchored cycle. They build a GDP-weighted global version of
it and show that the global factor subsumes the local ones, which is the
integration result that we later re-establish for the cycle factor. By validating
their evidence, we therefore also validate our implementation of the
Cochrane--Piazzesi factor and of the global aggregation. The G10 yields
underlying this exercise match the summary statistics of their Table~1 in level
and dispersion: average yields rise with maturity in every market, they are
highest in Italy and the United States and lowest in Switzerland and Japan, and
the ten-year yields are strongly correlated across countries. The latter is the
data feature that makes a common factor plausible.

\citet{dahlquist2013}, Table~3, documents that the local forward-rate factors are
strongly correlated with the GDP-weighted global factor. In \Cref{tab:dh-t3}, we
reproduce this result. We can see that each local factor correlates between
$0.76$ and $0.97$ with the global factor, that the correlation is highest for the
large core markets (Germany and the Netherlands at $0.92$, the United States at
$0.97$) and lowest for Japan ($0.82$), and that the pairwise correlations across
countries are uniformly high. A single global forward factor thus summarises the
common movement in expected bond returns, exactly as in the original study.

In \Cref{tab:dh-t4}, we reproduce Table~4, which contrasts the single-forward
Fama--Bliss regression \citep{famabliss1987} with the full Cochrane--Piazzesi
factor country by country. Similar to the results of \citet{dahlquist2013}, we
find that the Cochrane--Piazzesi factor dominates. Its predictive $R^{2}$ exceeds
the Fama--Bliss $R^{2}$ in almost every market and maturity (for the United
States at two years, $30\%$ against $8\%$; for Italy at ten years, $47\%$ against
$1\%$), and its loading is significant throughout. We also observe the strikingly
high fit in Japan ($R^{2}$ up to $69\%$) that they note as well. Therefore, the
full forward curve, and not just the spread, carries the predictable component of
returns.

The integration result itself is documented in Table~6, which we reproduce in
\Cref{tab:dh-t6}. Here, the local forward factor is compared against the global
one. We can see that the global factor is significant in every market and at
every maturity. Once it is included, the orthogonalised local factor adds little
in most countries. Its incremental loading is small and often insignificant for
the core European markets, Canada, and the United States, while a residual local
component survives in Italy and Japan. Furthermore, the joint test rejects the
no-predictability null everywhere. This is the central message of
\citet{dahlquist2013}, namely that government-bond risk premia are overwhelmingly
driven by a common global factor, with limited idiosyncratic local content. This
is also the very pattern that we re-establish for the cycle factor in
\Cref{ch:results}.

Table~7 takes the perspective of a United States investor who holds the foreign
bonds unhedged and earns the dollar excess return \eqref{eq:rx-usd}. It is the
table most directly relevant to the novel factor of our thesis, and we reproduce
it in \Cref{tab:dh-t7}. When the dollar excess returns are regressed on the
unadjusted global forward factor, they are only weakly predictable. The loading
is often insignificant at the short maturities and the $R^{2}$ is small, because
currency risk swamps the bond signal. The FX-adjusted global factor, built
top-down on the average dollar return as in the original study,
restores the predictability. Its loading is significant at almost every currency
and maturity (HAC $t$-statistics between $2.3$ and $4.7$), and the $R^{2}$ rises
materially, for the euro at ten years from $7\%$ to $17\%$ and for the Canadian
dollar from $27\%$ to $33\%$. This is the result that motivates the FX-adjusted
global \emph{cycle} factor of our thesis, and the replication confirms that the
construction works as intended for the forward factor. In addition to that, it
sets up a substantive contrast. For the cycle family, the same top-down
adjustment yields a factor that is nearly collinear with the unadjusted global
cycle factor in the G10 sample, which is why our thesis builds its FX-adjusted
cycle factor bottom-up instead (\Cref{sec:fw-fxgcf},
\Cref{sec:rob-fxgcf-construction}). We take up this difference between the two
factor families further in \Cref{ch:discussion}.

Taken together, the two replications establish that our engine reproduces both
pillars of the analysis: the macro-anchored cycle mechanism of \citet{cieslak2015}
on United States data, and the global-integration evidence of
\citet{dahlquist2013} on the G10, including the FX-adjusted construction. The
differences from the originals, namely a sample that begins in 1990 rather than
in the 1970s, the use of HAC and bootstrap inference, and the documented
expectations-hypothesis Monte Carlo level gap, do not disturb the qualitative
agreement. With the engine validated, we turn in \Cref{ch:results} to the
contribution of our thesis, namely applying the macro-anchored cycle factor,
rather than the forward-rate factor, to the international cross-section.
