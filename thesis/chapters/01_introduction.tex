\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

Investors
earn more on long-term government bonds than on rolling over short-term
bills, and this compensation varies predictably over time. According to
classical theory, this should not be the case. Under the expectations
hypothesis (EH), the yield on an $n$-year bond is the average of expected
future short rates plus at most a constant term premium,%
\footnote{This is the log-linearised expectations hypothesis: iterating the
  one-period relation $p^{(n)}_{t}=\E_{t}\!\left[p^{(n-1)}_{t+1}\right]-y^{(1)}_{t}$
  under a constant term premium and using $y^{(n)}_{t}=-p^{(n)}_{t}/n$ gives the
  average-expected-short-rate expression below. See \citet{campbell1991} for the
  standard treatment.}
\begin{equation*}
  y^{(n)}_{t} \;\approx\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}
  \E_{t}\!\left[y^{(1)}_{t+i}\right],
\end{equation*}
so excess returns from holding longer maturities should not be forecastable,
because whatever is known today about future short rates is already priced
into the curve. The EH is the natural null hypothesis of bond pricing.
However, it fails empirically. Four decades of evidence on US treasuries
document that excess returns are forecastable with information available at
the start of the holding period. This evidence begins with the forward--spot
spread of \citet{famabliss1987} and the yield spread regressions of
\citet{campbell1991}, whose slope coefficients carry the wrong sign relative
to the EH benchmark. The modern statement of the puzzle comes from
\citet{cochrane2005}, who show that a single ``tent-shaped'' linear
combination of forward rates, the CP factor, predicts one-year excess
returns across maturities with an $R^{2}$ of $0.35$ to $0.44$. From their
research we take that bond risk premia are time-varying and
countercyclical.

The CP factor sets a strong statistical benchmark. However, it tells us
little about the economics, since it is a reduced-form combination of prices
that explains predictability with other prices. Consequently, the question
remains which macroeconomic forces drive the premium.
\citet{ludvigson2009} show that factors extracted from a large macro panel
forecast bond returns above the information in the yield curve, and
\citet{cieslak2015} provide a clear economic mechanism. They
decompose nominal yields into a slow-moving trend-inflation component and
transitory cycles. The first is a discounted moving average of past core
inflation which serves as an anchor and the latter are the deviations from
it. A single \emph{cycle
factor} built from these detrended yields predicts US Treasury excess
returns more strongly than the CP factor and, in their sample, subsumes it.
The economic intuition is that the trend pins down where yields will settle,
so the distance of today's curve from its macro anchor measures the premium
investors demand for holding duration. The structural counterpart of this
view is the ``falling stars'' evidence of \citet{bauer2020}. Once the slow
drift in the long-run inflation and real-rate anchors ($\pi^{*}$ and $r^{*}$)
is accounted for, the predictable component of returns remains.

However, this macro-anchored evidence is almost exclusively evidence about
the United States. If the trend--cycle mechanism is real---if trend
inflation anchors yields and the cycles carry the risk premium---the same
mechanism should also operate in other developed bond markets, and a failure
to do so would cast doubt on the economic interpretation. The international
literature is encouraging, though it has pursued different predictors.
\citet{dahlquist2013} take
the forward-rate factor abroad. They construct local CP factors for four
markets, aggregate them into a GDP-weighted \emph{global} CP factor, and
show that the global factor predicts bond returns in every country and
largely drives out the local factors. These findings suggest that
government-bond risk premia are internationally integrated. They also
address a problem that is central to the international setting. An unhedged
US-dollar investor earns the foreign bond return plus a volatile currency
return. Because this currency component erodes predictability,
\citet{dahlquist2013} build an FX-adjusted global factor to recover it. On
the macro side,
\citet{zhang2021} document that the falling-stars trends improve return
forecasts in four markets outside the United States, and related evidence
extends to China \citep{zhang2022}. Most recently, \citet{randl2025} treat
developed-market government bonds as a distinct asset class. By deriving a
stochastic discount factor for currency-hedged G10 bond portfolios, they
show that the priced risks of international bonds are distinct from those of
currencies, which motivates studying the hedged (local-currency) bond
premium separately from the currency overlay, as we do.

When we place these strands side by side, we can identify a clear gap. The
cycle factor of \citet{cieslak2015}, a factor with a strong economic
rationale, has not yet been taken international. There is no country-level evidence across the G10, no
GDP-weighted \emph{global} cycle factor analogous to the global CP factor of
\citet{dahlquist2013}, and no FX-adjusted version of such a factor for the
dollar investor. \citet{zhang2021} come closest. However, they add raw
inflation trends to yield-curve regressions in four markets rather than
constructing the cycle factor itself, and they build no global or
currency-adjusted aggregate. A second gap is methodological. Generated
regressors such as the cycle factor are fitted on the very sample over which
they are evaluated, so in-sample $R^{2}$ can substantially overstate what a
real-time investor could have exploited. The out-of-sample discipline of
\citet{campbellthompson2008} is therefore not an optional robustness check
but part of the question itself. Whether macro-anchored bond return
predictability is a global phenomenon, and whether it survives in real time,
is thus still an open question. In our thesis, we aim to answer it.

% --- Research question -----------------------------------------------------

In our thesis, we ask one main question:
\begin{quote}
  \emph{Does the \citet{cieslak2015} cycle factor apply to international
  government bonds?}
\end{quote}
If the trend--cycle mechanism is a fundamental property of bond pricing
rather than a US idiosyncrasy, it should predict excess returns in other
developed markets with the same sign structure and a comparable magnitude.
To sharpen the international dimension, we pose three sub-questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{What is the role of the local cycle factor relative to a
    GDP-weighted global cycle factor?} Integrated bond markets suggest a
    shared risk factor across countries \citep{dahlquist2013}, and a
    GDP-weighted average of the national cycle factors is the natural
    summary of such a factor.
  \item \emph{Does the global cycle factor subsume the predictive power of
    the local cycle factors?} If markets are integrated, investors are
    compensated for global rather than local risk. In this case, adding the
    local cycle to a regression that already contains the global factor
    should not raise the predictive power.
  \item \emph{What distinguishes the interest-rate premium from the
    currency-inclusive premium, and when do the two play different roles?}
    The global cycle factor prices the hedged, local-currency return and thus
    reflects interest-rate-cycle risk; its FX-adjusted counterpart prices the
    unhedged dollar return and adds the currency leg. Whether the difference
    between the two carries independent, time-varying information, rather than
    being a redundant rescaling of the same factor, is what decides whether an
    FX adjustment matters, and the answer turns out to depend on the
    interest-rate regime.
\end{enumerate}
We operationalise these questions in three empirical phases
(\Cref{sec:fw-hypotheses}). In Phase~I, we test the cycle factor market by
market across the G10. In Phase~II, we run the horse race between the global
and the local factors. In Phase~III, we adopt the perspective of an unhedged
US-dollar investor, for whom currency risk intervenes between the foreign
bond and the realised dollar return, and we ask whether a purpose-built
FX-adjusted global cycle factor can restore the predictability that currency
conversion destroys. Because the cycle factors are generated regressors, we
evaluate every phase both in-sample and under a fully-recursive
out-of-sample protocol, in which the entire construction, that is the trend,
the cycles, the predictive coefficients, and the global weights, is rebuilt
each month from historically available data only.

% --- Contribution ----------------------------------------------------------

With our thesis, we contribute a three-factor hierarchy and the evidence to
evaluate it. At the base sits the \emph{local cycle factor} $\CF_{i,t}$,
which we construct for each of eleven G10 markets exactly as
\citet{cieslak2015} construct it for the United States, on a monthly panel
of zero-coupon yields, core inflation, exchange rates, and GDP spanning
1989--2026. One level up, the \emph{global
cycle factor} $\GCF_{t}$ aggregates the national factors with time-varying
GDP weights. With this factor, we transplant the integration test of
\citet{dahlquist2013} from the forward-rate family to the macro-anchored
cycle family. At the top, we create another new factor, the \emph{FX-adjusted global
cycle factor} $\FXGCF_{t}$. It fits a USD cycle factor per country. The
country's \emph{dollar} excess return is projected on its own cycles and
aggregated with GDP weights, and it thereby isolates the component of the
cycles that is informative for a US investor's currency-converted returns.

Three features distinguish our evidence from its closest predecessors. The
first is breadth. Relative to the four markets of \citet{dahlquist2013} and
\citet{zhang2021}, we cover the full G10 cross-section of eleven markets,
including the euro-area periphery. The second is the
predictive object. While \citet{zhang2021} add inflation and real-rate
trends to yield-curve regressions, we carry the cycle-factor construction
itself abroad, so that the US benchmark and the international evidence are
directly comparable factor by factor. The third is real-time discipline. We
re-estimate all three factors fully recursively and score them with the
\citet{campbellthompson2008} out-of-sample $R^{2}$ against a recursive
prevailing-mean benchmark. We complement this with a crisis-subsample
analysis, a horse race against the global forward-rate factor, a
core-versus-headline-inflation reconstruction and a market-timing strategy
that converts the statistical evidence into economic value. Furthermore, we
replicate the published US and international results
(\Cref{ch:replication}) to validate our empirical engine.

% --- Preview of findings & roadmap -----------------------------------------

We arrive at four main findings. First, we find that the cycle mechanism
also works outside the United States. The local cycle factor predicts
one-year excess returns with the exact sign pattern that \citet{cieslak2015}
document for the United States in all eleven markets, significantly so in
ten, with an average in-sample $R^{2}$ of $25\%$ (US: $33\%$, closely
matching the original study) and uniform loadings across the maturity curve.
Second, we find that the predictability is overwhelmingly global. The
GDP-weighted global cycle factor is significant in every market and matches
the explanatory power of the eleven separately estimated local factors.
Once it is included, genuinely local content survives in only three markets
(Italy, the Netherlands, Belgium). Integration is therefore the rule,
although it weakens under severe sovereign stress. Third, we find that
currency risk is the dominant obstacle for the dollar investor. Dollar
conversion erodes roughly two-thirds of the global factor's predictive
power. The FX-adjusted factor recovers much of it in sample, nearly doubling
the average dollar-investor $R^{2}$ from $8\%$ to $14\%$ and restoring
significance in ten of eleven markets, provided it is built bottom-up from
the country-level USD regressions. The two factors are, moreover,
distinct only in the high-rate regime: they correlate $0.45$ before 2008 but
$0.96$ afterwards, as policy rates converge to the zero lower bound and the
cross-country interest differentials that make currency returns predictable
compress. Out of sample, consistent with this, the
FX adjustment delivers only a small and conditional edge. Fourth, and in our view most importantly, only
the global factor survives in real time. Out of sample, the local factors
lose to the recursive historical mean almost everywhere, while the global
cycle factor beats it in ten of eleven markets. This real-time edge holds
across crisis subsamples, beats the global forward-rate factor, and depends
materially on measuring the trend with \emph{core} rather than headline
inflation. Moreover, it is economically exploitable. A timing strategy based
on it raises the Sharpe ratio of a hedged global bond portfolio from $0.31$
to $0.38$ with a positive certainty-equivalent gain.

The remainder of our thesis is organised as follows. In
\Cref{ch:literature}, we review the three literatures our thesis draws
together, namely US bond return predictability, macro-anchored yield
decompositions, and international bond risk premia, and locate the gap. In
\Cref{ch:data}, we describe the G10 panel and develop the formal apparatus,
which consists of the yield decomposition, the three-factor hierarchy, the
excess-return definitions for the local and the dollar investor, and the
predictive specifications, together with the inference for overlapping returns,
the expectations-hypothesis Monte Carlo, and the recursive out-of-sample
protocol. In \Cref{ch:results},
we present the three-phase main results. In \Cref{ch:strategy}, we convert
the surviving predictability into a real-time portfolio strategy, and in
\Cref{ch:robustness}, we stress-test our findings. In \Cref{ch:discussion},
we interpret the findings and state their limitations, before we conclude in
\Cref{ch:conclusion}. In \Cref{ch:replication}, we validate our empirical
engine against the published results of \citet{cieslak2015} and
\citet{dahlquist2013}.
