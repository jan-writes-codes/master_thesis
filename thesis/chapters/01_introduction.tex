% --- Motivation: the EH puzzle --------------------------------------------
\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro-motivation}

Why do investors earn more on long-term government bonds than on rolling over
short-term bills---and why does that compensation vary so predictably over
time? The classical answer is that it should not. Under the expectations
hypothesis (EH), the yield on an $n$-year bond is the average of expected
future short rates plus at most a constant term premium,
\begin{equation*}
  y^{(n)}_{t} \;\approx\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}
  \E_{t}\!\left[y^{(1)}_{t+i}\right],
\end{equation*}
so excess returns from holding longer maturities should be unforecastable:
whatever is known today about future short rates is already priced into the
curve. The EH is the natural null hypothesis of bond pricing, and it fails.
Four decades of evidence on US Treasuries document that excess returns are
forecastable with information available at the start of the holding period,
beginning with the forward--spot spread of \citet{famabliss1987} and the yield
spread regressions of \citet{campbell1991}, whose slope coefficients carry the
\emph{wrong} sign relative to the EH benchmark. The modern statement of the
puzzle is due to \citet{cochrane2005}: a single ``tent-shaped'' linear
combination of forward rates---the CP factor---predicts one-year excess
returns across maturities with an $R^{2}$ of $0.35$ to $0.44$. Bond risk
premia are large, time-varying, and countercyclical.

The CP factor sets a powerful statistical benchmark, but it is silent on
economics: it is a reduced-form combination of prices that explains
predictability with other prices. The natural follow-up question is what
macroeconomic forces drive the premium. \citet{ludvigson2009} show that
factors extracted from a large macro panel forecast bond returns over and
above the information in the yield curve, and \citet{cieslak2015} supply the
sharpest economic mechanism to date. They decompose nominal yields into a
slow-moving \emph{trend-inflation} component---a discounted moving average of
past core inflation that anchors the level of interest rates at long
horizons---and transitory \emph{cycles}, the deviations of yields from that
anchor. A single \emph{cycle factor} built from these detrended yields
predicts US Treasury excess returns more strongly than the CP factor and, in
their sample, subsumes it. The economic content is intuitive: the trend pins
down where yields will settle, so the distance of today's curve from its
macro anchor measures the premium investors demand for holding duration. The
structural counterpart of this view is the ``falling stars'' evidence of
\citet{bauer2020}, in which accounting for the drifting long-run anchors
$\pi^{*}$ and $r^{*}$ is precisely what uncovers the predictable component of
returns.

This macro-anchored evidence, however, is almost exclusively evidence about
the United States---one market, one monetary history, one disinflation. If
trend inflation truly anchors yields and cycles truly carry the risk premium,
the same mechanism should operate in every developed bond market, and its
failure to do so would cast doubt on the economic interpretation. The
international literature gives reason for optimism, but it has pursued a
different predictor. \citet{dahlquist2013} take the \emph{forward-rate}
factor abroad: they construct local CP factors for four markets, aggregate
them into a GDP-weighted \emph{global} CP factor, and show that the global
factor predicts bond returns in every country and largely drives out the
local factors---direct evidence that government-bond risk premia are
internationally integrated. They also confront the problem that defines the
international setting: an unhedged US-dollar investor earns the foreign bond
return \emph{plus} a volatile currency return, which erodes the
predictability, and they build an FX-adjusted global factor to recover it.

On the macro side, \citet{zhang2021} document that the falling-stars trends
improve return forecasts in four markets outside the United States, and
related evidence extends to China \citep{zhang2022}. Most recently,
\citet{randl2025} treat developed-market government bonds as a distinct
asset class: deriving a stochastic discount factor for currency-hedged G10
bond portfolios, they show that the priced risks of international bonds are
fundamentally distinct from those of currencies---independent support for
studying the hedged (local-currency) bond premium separately from the
currency overlay, as this thesis does.

Placing these strands side by side exposes a clear gap. The cycle factor of
\citet{cieslak2015}---the predictor with the strongest claim to an economic
mechanism---has not been taken international: there exists no country-level
evidence across the G10, no GDP-weighted \emph{global} cycle factor analogous
to the global CP factor of \citet{dahlquist2013}, and no FX-adjusted version
of such a factor for the dollar investor. \citet{zhang2021} come closest, but
they add raw inflation trends to yield-curve regressions in four markets
rather than constructing the cycle factor itself, and they build no global or
currency-adjusted aggregate. A second gap is methodological. Generated
regressors such as the cycle factor are fitted on the very sample over which
they are evaluated, so in-sample $R^{2}$ can substantially overstate what a
real-time investor could have exploited; the out-of-sample discipline of
\citet{campbellthompson2008} is therefore not an optional robustness check
but part of the question itself. Whether macro-anchored bond return
predictability is a global phenomenon---and whether it survives in real
time---is an open question. This thesis answers it.

The question is far from academic. The 2022 inflation surge delivered the
worst year for global government bonds in decades: as core inflation jumped
across the developed world and central banks raised policy rates in
near-unison, long bonds suffered double-digit losses, and the term premium
that had lain dormant through the zero-lower-bound decade returned to the
centre of asset-allocation debates. In an environment of higher and more
volatile inflation, whether the compensation for holding duration is
forecastable---and whether a disciplined, real-time signal can tell an
investor when that compensation is rich or thin---is of immediate consequence
for the pension funds, insurers, and central banks that hold the bulk of the
world's government debt. That episode recurs in \Cref{ch:strategy}, where the
global cycle factor steers the investor toward cash on the eve of the 2022
drawdown.

% --- Research question -----------------------------------------------------
\section{Research Question}
\label{sec:intro-rq}

The thesis asks one main question:
\begin{quote}
  \emph{Does the \citet{cieslak2015} cycle factor apply to international
  government bonds?}
\end{quote}
If the trend--cycle mechanism is a fundamental property of bond pricing
rather than a US idiosyncrasy, it should predict excess returns in other
developed markets with the same sign structure and comparable magnitude. Two
sub-questions, taken from the research proposal, sharpen the international
dimension:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{What is the role of the local cycle factor relative to a
    GDP-weighted global cycle factor?} Integrated bond markets suggest a
    shared risk factor across countries \citep{dahlquist2013}, of which a
    GDP-weighted average of the national cycle factors is the natural
    summary.
  \item \emph{Does the global cycle factor subsume the predictive power of
    the local cycle factors?} If markets are integrated, investors are
    compensated for global rather than local risk, and adding the local
    cycle to a regression that already contains the global factor should
    not raise predictive power.
\end{enumerate}
The questions are operationalised in three empirical phases
(\Cref{sec:fw-hypotheses}). Phase~I tests the cycle factor market by market
across the G10. Phase~II runs the global-versus-local horse race. Phase~III
adopts the perspective of an unhedged US-dollar investor, for whom currency
risk intervenes between the foreign bond and the realised dollar return, and
asks whether a purpose-built FX-adjusted global cycle factor restores the
predictability that currency conversion destroys. Because the cycle factors
are generated regressors, every phase is evaluated both in-sample and under a
fully-recursive out-of-sample protocol in which the entire
construction---trend, cycles, predictive coefficients, and global
weights---is rebuilt each month from historically available data only.

% --- Contribution ----------------------------------------------------------
\section{Contribution}
\label{sec:intro-contribution}

The thesis contributes a three-factor hierarchy and the evidence to evaluate
it. At the base sits the \emph{local cycle factor} $\CF_{i,t}$, constructed
for each of eleven G10 markets exactly as \citet{cieslak2015} construct it
for the United States, on a monthly panel of zero-coupon yields, core
inflation, exchange rates, and GDP spanning 1989--2026 (1994--2026 for most
markets). One level up, the \emph{global cycle factor} $\GCF_{t}$ aggregates
the national factors with time-varying GDP weights---transplanting the
integration test of \citet{dahlquist2013} from the forward-rate family to
the macro-anchored cycle family. At the top, the \emph{FX-adjusted global
cycle factor} $\FXGCF_{t}$ is, to the best of my knowledge, new: it projects
the GDP-weighted \emph{dollar} excess return on the global cycle factor,
isolating the component of global cycles that is informative for a US
investor's currency-converted returns.

Three features distinguish the evidence from its closest predecessors. First,
breadth: relative to the four markets of \citet{dahlquist2013} and
\citet{zhang2021}, the analysis covers the full G10 cross-section of eleven
markets, including the euro-area periphery, whose sovereign-debt crisis
provides a natural experiment on the limits of integration. Second, the
predictive object: where \citet{zhang2021} add inflation and real-rate trends
to yield-curve regressions, this thesis carries the cycle-factor
\emph{construction} itself abroad, so that the US benchmark and the
international evidence are directly comparable factor by factor. Third,
real-time discipline: all three factors are re-estimated fully recursively
and scored with the \citet{campbellthompson2008} out-of-sample $R^{2}$
against a recursive prevailing-mean benchmark, complemented by an
expectations-hypothesis Monte Carlo that calibrates how much in-sample
$R^{2}$ overlapping returns can produce under the null, crisis-subsample
analysis, a horse race against the global forward-rate factor, a
core-versus-headline-inflation reconstruction, and a market-timing strategy
that converts the statistical evidence into economic value. The replication
of the published US and international results (\Cref{ch:replication})
anchors the entire exercise.

% --- Preview of findings & roadmap -----------------------------------------
\section{Main Findings and Structure}
\label{sec:intro-roadmap}

Four findings emerge. \emph{First}, the cycle mechanism travels. The local
cycle factor predicts one-year excess returns with the exact sign pattern
that \citet{cieslak2015} document for the United States in all eleven
markets, significantly so in ten, with an average in-sample $R^{2}$ of
$25\%$ (US: $33\%$, closely matching the original study) and uniform
loadings across the maturity curve.

\emph{Second}, the predictability is
overwhelmingly global. The GDP-weighted global cycle factor is significant
in every market and matches the explanatory power of the eleven separately
estimated local factors; once it is included, genuinely local content
survives in only three markets (Italy, the Netherlands, Belgium). The
Italian exception is informative: its local predictability concentrates in
the 2010--2012 sovereign-debt crisis, when redenomination risk priced
locally what no global factor could capture---integration is the rule, but
it bends exactly when sovereign stress is at its height.

\emph{Third},
currency risk is the binding constraint for the dollar investor. Dollar
conversion erodes roughly two-thirds of the global factor's predictive
power, and the FX-adjusted factor recovers only part of it---because, in
this sample, it is nearly collinear with the unadjusted factor (correlation
$0.99$, against roughly $0.50$ for the forward-rate analogue in
\citet{dahlquist2013}). The FX adjustment that transforms the global
\emph{forward} factor adds little to the global \emph{cycle} factor, a
contrast that is itself a finding.

\emph{Fourth}, and most consequentially,
only the global factor survives in real time. Out of sample, the local
factors lose to the recursive historical mean almost everywhere, while the
global cycle factor beats it in ten of eleven markets; that real-time edge
holds across crisis subsamples, beats the global forward-rate factor,
depends materially on measuring the trend with \emph{core} rather than
headline inflation, and is economically exploitable---a timing strategy
based on it raises the Sharpe ratio of a hedged global bond portfolio from
$0.31$ to $0.38$ with a positive certainty-equivalent gain.

The remainder of the thesis is organised as follows. \Cref{ch:literature}
reviews the three literatures the thesis draws together---US bond return
predictability, macro-anchored yield decompositions, and international bond
risk premia---and locates the gap. \Cref{ch:framework} develops the formal
apparatus: the yield decomposition, the three-factor hierarchy, the excess
return definitions for the local and the dollar investor, and the predictive
specifications. \Cref{ch:data} describes the G10 panel and its construction;
\Cref{ch:methodology} sets out the inference for overlapping returns, the
expectations-hypothesis Monte Carlo, and the recursive out-of-sample
protocol. \Cref{ch:replication} validates the empirical engine against the
published results of \citet{cieslak2015} and \citet{dahlquist2013}.
\Cref{ch:results} presents the three-phase main results,
\Cref{ch:robustness} stress-tests them, and \Cref{ch:strategy} converts the
surviving predictability into a real-time trading strategy.
\Cref{ch:discussion} interprets the findings and states their limitations,
and \Cref{ch:conclusion} concludes.
