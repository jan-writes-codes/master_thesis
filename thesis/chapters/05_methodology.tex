\section{Excess Returns and the Expectations-Hypothesis Null}
\label{sec:fw-returns}
\label{sec:fw-eh}

Having described the data, we now develop the formal apparatus that it feeds.
Throughout, $i$ indexes countries in the G10 cross-section $\mathcal{G}$, time
$t$ is measured in months, and bond maturity $n$ in years. The empirical
maturity menu is $N=\{1,2,4,5,9,10\}$ years, and all yields are continuously
compounded and expressed in percent per annum. We let $y^{(n)}_{t}$ denote the time-$t$
continuously compounded yield on an $n$-year zero-coupon bond and
$p^{(n)}_{t}=-n\,y^{(n)}_{t}$ the corresponding log price. The one-year
holding-period excess return on an $n$-year bond,
\begin{equation}
  \rx^{(n)}_{t+12} \;=\; p^{(n-1)}_{t+12}-p^{(n)}_{t}-y^{(1)}_{t}
                  \;=\; n\,y^{(n)}_{t}-(n-1)\,y^{(n-1)}_{t+12}-y^{(1)}_{t},
  \label{eq:rx}
\end{equation}
should therefore satisfy $\E_{t}\!\left[\rx^{(n)}_{t+12}\right]=\text{const}$,
i.e.\ the expected excess return is the same at every date. The empirical
bond-pricing literature, however, rejects this restriction.

\paragraph{Local-currency returns.}
The one-year excess return on an $n$-year bond for a domestic investor is given
by \eqref{eq:rx}. Because return volatility scales roughly with duration, we
follow \citet{cieslak2015} and work with \emph{duration-standardised} returns,
$\widetilde{\rx}^{(n)}_{i,t+12}=\rx^{(n)}_{i,t+12}/D^{(n)}$, and then average
across the maturity set to obtain a single, scale-comparable return series:
\begin{equation}
  \rxbar_{i,t+12} \;=\; \frac{1}{K}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}
                        \frac{\rx^{(n)}_{i,t+12}}{D^{(n)}},
  \qquad K=|\mathcal{N}|,\quad D^{(n)}=n.
  \label{eq:rxbar}
\end{equation}
The duration weight $D^{(n)}=n$ is the Macaulay duration of a continuously
compounded zero-coupon bond. For the construction, we use the averaging set
$\mathcal{N}=\{2,5,10\}$ years. These are the maturities for which a clean
one-year-ahead excess return is identified given the data menu, since each
requires the adjacent shorter yield $y^{(n-1)}$, i.e.\
$y^{(1)},y^{(4)},y^{(9)}$, which are all available.

\paragraph{US-dollar investor returns.}
We now consider a US investor who borrows for one year in US dollars, converts
to foreign currency, holds the foreign bond, and converts the proceeds back.
Throughout the thesis, ``dollar'' refers to the US dollar.
For this investor, the dollar excess return adds the realised currency return
and nets out the US short rate:
\begin{equation}
  \rx^{(n),\mathrm{USD}}_{i,t+12}
    \;=\; \rx^{(n)}_{i,t+12} + y^{(1)}_{i,t}
          + \big(s_{i,t+12}-s_{i,t}\big) - y^{(1)}_{\mathrm{US},t},
  \label{eq:rx-usd}
\end{equation}
where $s_{i,t}=\log(\text{USD per unit of currency }i)$, so that
$s_{i,t+12}-s_{i,t}$ is the one-year log currency return. We apply the same
duration-standardisation and maturity-averaging as in \eqref{eq:rxbar} to
\eqref{eq:rx-usd} and obtain $\rxbar^{\,\mathrm{USD}}_{i,t+12}$. This dollar
return is the object of interest for the international investor and the input
to the dollar-return factor below.

With the return definitions in place, the remainder of this chapter builds the
predictors we use to document and interpret this predictability
internationally.

\section{The Cycle-Factor Hierarchy}
\label{sec:fw-hierarchy}

This section develops the three nested factors used throughout the thesis: the
local cycle factor, its GDP-weighted global aggregate, and the dollar-return
counterpart built for the US-dollar investor. All three rest on the same
yield decomposition, which we set out first.

\paragraph{Yield decomposition and interest-rate cycles.}
The central argument of \citet{cieslak2015} is that nominal yields are driven by
two forces. The first is a persistent trend-inflation component
$\trendinf_{i,t}$ that anchors the level of interest rates over long horizons,
and the second consists of transitory cycles $\cyc^{(n)}_{i,t}$ that
capture business-cycle conditions, real-rate movements, and variation in risk
appetite. For each country $i$ and maturity $n$, we decompose yields by the
linear projection
\begin{equation}
  y^{(n)}_{i,t} \;=\; \alpha_{i,n} + \beta_{i,n}\,\trendinf_{i,t}
                    + \cyc^{(n)}_{i,t},
  \qquad
  \cyc^{(n)}_{i,t} \;\equiv\; \varepsilon^{(n)}_{i,t},
  \label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
so that the cycle is, by definition, the residual of yields on trend inflation.
We estimate this projection separately for every country--maturity pair by
OLS.\footnote{Empirically the cycle is the fitted OLS
  residual $\hat\varepsilon^{(n)}_{i,t}$. We keep the population notation
  $(\alpha_{i,n},\beta_{i,n})$ in \eqref{eq:decomp} and reserve hats for the
  fitted factor in \eqref{eq:cf}.}

We construct trend inflation as a backward-looking, exponentially weighted
moving average of realised year-on-year core inflation $\pi_{i,t}$:
\begin{equation}
  \trendinf_{i,t} \;=\; \frac{1-v}{1-v^{M}}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1} v^{j}\,\pi_{i,t-j},
  \qquad M=120,\quad v=0.987.
  \label{eq:trendinf}
\end{equation}
The window spans $M=120$ months (ten years), and the geometric decay
$v=0.987$ has a half-life of roughly four and a half years.
Because the average never looks forward, $\trendinf_{i,t}$ is observable at $t$
and introduces no look-ahead bias. The normalising constant $(1-v)/(1-v^{M})$
makes the weights sum to one.

We run the decomposition in \eqref{eq:decomp}
country-by-country and maturity-by-maturity, so the loadings
$(\alpha_{i,n},\beta_{i,n})$, and thus the cycles, are local objects. The
cross-country structure only enters later, through the global factor. The decay
parameter $v=0.987$ follows \citet{cieslak2015}. In \Cref{ch:robustness}, we
examine the sensitivity of our results to this choice and to the window length
$M$.

\paragraph{The local cycle factor.}
\citet{cieslak2015} summarise the cross-section of cycles with two objects.
The first is the one-year cycle $\cyc^{(1)}_{i,t}$, which tracks the cyclical
position of the short rate, and the second is the average cycle
\begin{equation}
  \cycbar_{i,t} \;=\; \frac{1}{|N\setminus\{1\}|}
                      \sum_{n\in N,\, n\neq 1}\cyc^{(n)}_{i,t},
  \label{eq:cbar}
\end{equation}
which captures the common, longer-maturity cyclical variation and attenuates
maturity-specific measurement error. We define the local cycle factor as
the fitted value of the one-year-ahead excess return projected on these two
predictors:
\begin{align}
  \rxbar_{i,t+12} &= \gamma_{0} + \gamma_{i,1}\,\cyc^{(1)}_{i,t}
                     + \gamma_{i,2}\,\cycbar_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+12},
                     \label{eq:cf-reg}\\[2pt]
  \CF_{i,t}       &= \hat\gamma_{i,1}\,\cyc^{(1)}_{i,t}
                     + \hat\gamma_{i,2}\,\cycbar_{i,t}.
                     \label{eq:cf}
\end{align}
Here, $\rxbar_{i,t+12}$ is the duration-standardised, maturity-averaged excess
return defined in \eqref{eq:rxbar}. $\CF_{i,t}$ is, by construction, the component of the cycle
cross-section that is relevant for predicting returns. Because the factor is a
fitted value rather than an observed regressor, we treat it throughout as a
generated regressor. That means it is fitted and evaluated on the same data,
which is the reason for the in-sample inference of
\Cref{sec:meth-inference} and the fully-recursive out-of-sample tests of
\Cref{sec:meth-oos}.

\paragraph{The global cycle factor and GDP weights.}
If government-bond markets are internationally integrated, investors should be
compensated for exposure to a common source of risk rather than for
country-specific fluctuations \citep{dahlquist2013}. To capture this idea, we
aggregate the local cycle factors into a single global factor. We use
time-varying GDP weights so that larger economies contribute proportionally
more:
\begin{equation}
  \GCF_{t} \;=\; \sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}} w_{i,t}\,\CF_{i,t},
  \qquad
  w_{i,t} \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{GDP}_{i,t}}
                     {\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{GDP}_{j,t}}.
  \label{eq:gcf}
\end{equation}
We recompute the weights $w_{i,t}$ each month over the set of countries that
are present in the estimation panel at $t$, so that $\sum_{i}w_{i,t}=1$ holds
by construction even when coverage is not fully available across all countries
(\Cref{sec:data-coverage}). We convert nominal GDP to US dollars before
weighting. The global
factor $\GCF_{t}$ is the international counterpart of the local $\CF_{i,t}$ and
provides us with a test of market integration. Under full integration,
the global factor should subsume the predictive content of the local factor
(\Cref{sec:fw-hypotheses}).

\paragraph{The dollar-return global cycle factor.}
\label{sec:fw-fxgcf}
A global factor built from local-currency returns ignores the currency risk
that an international investor bears. Because of this, we construct a
dollar-return global cycle factor $\FXGCF_{t}$ that targets dollar
returns directly, in analogy to the dollar-return global factor (FXGCP) of
\citet{dahlquist2013}. We build the factor bottom-up, exactly as the global
factor in \eqref{eq:gcf}, but from country-level factors that are fitted to
dollar returns. For each country, we first estimate the USD cycle
factor $\FXCF_{i,t}$ as the fitted value of the country's dollar excess
return on its own cycle predictors:
\begin{align}
  \rxbar^{\,\mathrm{USD}}_{i,t+12}
     &= \delta_{i,0} + \delta_{i,1}\,\cyc^{(1)}_{i,t}
        + \delta_{i,2}\,\cycbar_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+12},
        \label{eq:fxcf-reg}\\[2pt]
  \FXCF_{i,t} &= \hat\delta_{i,1}\,\cyc^{(1)}_{i,t}
              + \hat\delta_{i,2}\,\cycbar_{i,t}.
        \label{eq:fxcf}
\end{align}
We then aggregate the national USD cycle factors with the same GDP weights as
in \eqref{eq:gcf}:
\begin{equation}
  \FXGCF_{t} \;=\; \sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}} w_{i,t}\,\FXCF_{i,t}.
  \label{eq:fxgcf}
\end{equation}

By construction,
\eqref{eq:fxcf-reg}--\eqref{eq:fxgcf} mirror the global
factor exactly. $\FXGCF_{t}$ is to the dollar returns what $\GCF_{t}$ is to
the local-currency returns, so any difference between the two factors can be
attributed to the return currency alone. An alternative is the
``top-down'' construction of \citet{dahlquist2013}, in which the GDP-weighted
average dollar return is projected on the GDP-weighted average cycle
predictors. We examined this variant and found that it is nearly collinear
with the unadjusted global factor in our sample (correlation $0.99$), so it
adds almost no incremental information about currency risk. We therefore
adopt the bottom-up construction as our main specification and compare the
two constructions in \Cref{sec:rob-fxgcf-construction}.

\section{Hypotheses and Predictive Specifications}
\label{sec:fw-hypotheses}

We map the three-factor hierarchy onto our research questions through a
sequence of one-year-ahead predictive regressions. Because the data are monthly
but the forecast horizon is twelve months, all of these regressions use
\emph{overlapping} returns. Consecutive observations share eleven months of the
realised return, so the residual is serially correlated by construction as an
MA(11) process. It is central to the HAC inference of \Cref{sec:meth-inference}. We estimate each specification
by OLS and report the slope point estimate with robust inference.

\paragraph{Phase~I: local predictability.}
For each country, we estimate
\begin{equation}
  \rxbar_{i,t+12} = \alpha_{i} + \beta_{i}\,\CF_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+12},
  \label{eq:h-local}
\end{equation}
and test $H_{0}\!:\beta_{i}=0$. A rejection of this null establishes that the
macro-anchored cycle mechanism of \citet{cieslak2015} also operates outside the
United States.

\paragraph{Phase~II: global versus local.}
We run the horse race
\begin{align}
  \rxbar_{i,t+12} &= \alpha_{i} + \beta_{i}\,\CF^{\perp}_{i,t}
                     + \gamma_{i}\,\GCF_{t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+12},
                     \label{eq:h-horse}\\
  \rxbar_{i,t+12} &= \alpha_{i} + \gamma_{i}\,\GCF_{t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+12}.
                     \label{eq:h-global}
\end{align}
Under full market integration, the global factor should drive out the local
factor. To make the comparison clean, we orthogonalise the local factor with
respect to the global factor before the joint regression. That is,
$\CF^{\perp}_{i,t}$ is the residual of the construction regression
\begin{equation}
  \CF_{i,t} \;=\; a_{i} + b_{i}\,\GCF_{t} + \CF^{\perp}_{i,t},
  \label{eq:cf-perp}
\end{equation}
so that $\beta_{i}$ in \eqref{eq:h-horse} measures the incremental local
content. Adding $\CF^{\perp}_{i,t}$ to \eqref{eq:h-global} should not raise the
predictive power under integration, i.e.\ $\beta_{i}=0$.

\paragraph{Phase~III: currency risk and the dollar-return factor.}
For the dollar investor, we estimate
\begin{align}
  \rxbar^{\,\mathrm{USD}}_{i,t+12}
     &= \alpha_{i} + \beta_{i}\,\CF_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+12},
        \label{eq:h-usd-local}\\
  \rxbar^{\,\mathrm{USD}}_{i,t+12}
     &= \alpha_{i} + \gamma_{i}\,\GCF_{t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+12},
        \label{eq:h-usd-global}\\
  \rxbar^{\,\mathrm{USD}}_{i,t+12}
     &= \alpha_{i} + \delta_{i}\,\FXGCF_{t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+12}.
        \label{eq:h-fxgcf}
\end{align}
With specifications \eqref{eq:h-usd-local}--\eqref{eq:h-usd-global}, we ask
whether the local-currency factors retain their predictive power once currency
risk is introduced. With \eqref{eq:h-fxgcf}, we ask whether the purpose-built
dollar-return factor $\FXGCF_{t}$ recovers the predictability that an unadjusted
global factor loses on currency-converted returns. Together,
\eqref{eq:h-local}--\eqref{eq:h-fxgcf} constitute the empirical programme that
we test in \Cref{ch:results} and stress-test in \Cref{ch:robustness}.

\section{Estimation and Inference}
\label{sec:meth-inference}

We estimate every predictive regression by OLS and tailor
the inference to the overlapping-return structure. The $t$-statistics use
heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) Newey--West standard
errors \citep{neweywest1987} with $18$ lags. For
multi-regressor specifications we add a joint HAC Wald test that all slopes are
zero. Because the cycle
factors are generated regressors, we re-evaluate them fully
recursively\label{sec:meth-oos}, rebuilding the entire factor chain
(decomposition, local regression, and global aggregation) from data available at
each date $t$ so that both the factor and the forecasting regression respect $t$
(a ``doubly out-of-sample'' forecast), and scoring it by the
\citet{campbellthompson2008} out-of-sample $R^{2}$ against the recursive
prevailing mean,
\begin{equation}
  R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}} \;=\; 1 -
     \frac{\sum_{t}\big(\rxbar_{t+12}-\widehat{\rxbar}^{\,\text{factor}}_{t+12}\big)^{2}}
          {\sum_{t}\big(\rxbar_{t+12}-\widehat{\rxbar}^{\,\text{mean}}_{t+12}\big)^{2}},
  \label{eq:meth-ctr2}
\end{equation}
pooled across countries so that each competes against its own recursive mean and
applied identically to the Cochrane--Piazzesi comparison factors for the
head-to-head evidence of \Cref{ch:robustness}.

\paragraph{Deviations from the original studies.}
\label{sec:meth-deviations}
For transparency, we collect the substantive departures from \citet{cieslak2015}
and \citet{dahlquist2013} here. We revisit each of them where it
bears on the results.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Maturity menu.} Yields are available at $\{1,2,4,5,9,10\}$ years
    rather than the finer grid of the original papers. We form individual excess
    returns for $n\in\{2,5,10\}$, the maturities with an observed adjacent
    shorter yield.
  \item \textbf{Sample.} The G10 panel spans 1989/1994--2026
    (\Cref{sec:data-coverage}), which is shorter than with the original US samples, so we expect the replicated levels to differ
    from the published numbers.
  \item \textbf{Inference.} We replace the exact reverse-regression delta method
    with HAC ($18$-lag Newey--West) $t$-statistics, for the identification
    reason given in \Cref{sec:meth-inference}.
  \item \textbf{Dollar-return factor.} We build $\FXGCF_{t}$ bottom-up, as the
    GDP-weighted average of per-country USD cycle factors, rather than
    top-down or as a projection on $\GCF_{t}$ (\Cref{sec:fw-fxgcf}). We
    compare the constructions in \Cref{sec:rob-fxgcf-construction}.
\end{itemize}
These choices keep our construction internally consistent and reproducible
while remaining as close to the source methodology as the data permit.
