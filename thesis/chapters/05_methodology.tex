\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:methodology}

In this chapter, we set out how we estimate the factors of \Cref{ch:framework}
and how we evaluate the predictive regressions. We first describe the
construction pipeline and the predictive-regression design. We then turn to the
inference machinery, namely HAC standard errors, model comparison, and
bootstrap intervals, and to the expectations-hypothesis Monte Carlo that
calibrates what predictive $R^{2}$ we should expect under the null. We close
the chapter with the out-of-sample protocol and a transparent statement of
where our implementation departs from the original studies.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Factor Construction Pipeline}
\label{sec:meth-pipeline}

We build the empirical objects in the layered sequence of \Cref{ch:framework},
where each layer feeds the next. (i) We smooth year-on-year core inflation into
trend inflation $\trendinf_{i,t}$ using the EWMA \eqref{eq:trendinf} ($M=120$,
$v=0.987$). (ii) For each country--maturity pair, we project yields on trend
inflation \eqref{eq:decomp} and take the residual as the cycle
$\cyc^{(n)}_{i,t}$; we retain the one-year cycle and the average cycle over
$\{2,4,5,9,10\}$ years. (iii) The local cycle factor $\CF_{i,t}$ is
the fitted value of the duration-standardised, maturity-averaged excess return
on these two cycle predictors \eqref{eq:cf-reg}--\eqref{eq:cf}. (iv) The global
factor $\GCF_{t}$ is the GDP-weighted average of local factors \eqref{eq:gcf},
and (v) the FX-adjusted factor $\FXGCF_{t}$ is the fitted value of the
GDP-weighted average dollar return on the GDP-weighted average cycle predictors
\eqref{eq:fxgcf-agg}--\eqref{eq:fxgcf}. We deliberately treat the cycle factors
as \emph{generated regressors}. We repeat the same estimation choices that
build the factor, recursively, in the out-of-sample exercise of
\Cref{sec:meth-oos}, so that no future information enters the real-time factor.

\section{Predictive-Regression Design}
\label{sec:meth-design}

All of our predictive regressions forecast the one-year-ahead excess return
from time-$t$ predictors,
\begin{equation}
  \rxbar_{i,t+12} \;=\; a + b'\,x_{t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+12},
  \label{eq:meth-pred}
\end{equation}
where $x_{t}$ collects the relevant cycle factor(s) and the dependent variable
is the duration-standardised, maturity-averaged return of \eqref{eq:rxbar} (or
its dollar counterpart). Because the data are monthly but the forecast horizon
is twelve months, we use \emph{overlapping} returns in the regressions.
Consecutive observations share eleven months of the realised return, and the
residual $\varepsilon_{i,t+12}$ is therefore serially correlated by
construction as an MA(11) process. This overlap is the central econometric
complication and the reason why ordinary least squares standard errors are
badly biased. It motivates the inference choices of the following section. We
estimate the specific specifications, the local regression \eqref{eq:h-local},
the global-versus-local horse race \eqref{eq:h-horse}--\eqref{eq:h-global}, and
the dollar-investor regressions \eqref{eq:h-usd-local}--\eqref{eq:h-fxgcf}, by
OLS and report the slope point estimates alongside the robust inference below.

\section{Statistical Inference}
\label{sec:meth-inference}

\subsection{HAC standard errors for overlapping returns}
For our headline $t$-statistics, we use heteroskedasticity- and
autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) standard errors of the Newey--West form
\citep{neweywest1987}, with a bandwidth of $18$ lags. The bandwidth comfortably
exceeds the $11$-month overlap, so it captures the induced serial correlation
in $\varepsilon_{i,t+12}$. We adopt $18$ lags as the convention for the
twelve-month-horizon regressions throughout. For a fitted regression, we report
the slope estimate $\hat b$, its HAC standard error, and the $t$-ratio
$\hat b/\mathrm{se}(\hat b)$.

\paragraph{Reverse-regression caveat.} \citet{cieslak2015} report $t$-statistics
from the reverse-regression delta method of \citet{weiwright2013}, which pairs
backward-summed regressors with \emph{one-period} (monthly) return innovations.
This estimator requires the underlying monthly returns. However, our
constructed panel stores only the twelve-month overlapping returns, so the
genuine reverse-regression form is not identified from the available data. We
therefore report Hansen--Hodrick/Newey--West HAC $t$-statistics, which are the
standard robust inference for overlapping returns, and treat them as such. The
signs, magnitudes, and the $R^{2}$, Wald, and BIC structure are faithful to the
original. Only the exact reverse-regression $t$-values, which would require the
paper's appendix, differ. We state this openly because HAC and
reverse-regression $t$-statistics can diverge for highly persistent regressors,
a point we revisit in \Cref{ch:discussion}.

\subsection{Joint significance: the Wald test}
For multi-regressor specifications, we report a joint Wald test that all slopes
are zero. With slope vector $\hat b_{s}$ (intercept excluded) and the
corresponding HAC covariance block $V_{s}$,
\begin{equation}
  W \;=\; \hat b_{s}'\,V_{s}^{-1}\,\hat b_{s}
  \;\sim\; \chi^{2}_{k},
  \label{eq:meth-wald}
\end{equation}
with $k$ the number of slopes, and we report $W$ with its $\chi^{2}$ p-value.
We apply the same statistic to the orthogonalised local factor in the horse
race \eqref{eq:h-horse} to measure whether the local factor adds explanatory
power beyond the global factor.

\subsection{Model comparison: BIC relative probabilities}
To compare the competing predictor sets, we use Bayesian information criteria
expressed as relative probabilities, following \citet{cieslak2015}. For model
$i$ estimated on a common sample of length $T$ with $n_{i}$ slope regressors,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BIC}_{i} \;=\; \ln\!\big(\hat\sigma^{2}_{i}\big)
     + \ln(T)\,\frac{n_{i}}{T},
  \qquad \hat\sigma^{2}_{i} = \mathrm{SSE}_{i}/T,
  \label{eq:meth-bic}
\end{equation}
and the relative probability of model $i$ is
$\exp\!\big\{(\mathrm{BIC}_{\min}-\mathrm{BIC}_{i})/2\big\}$, normalised so that
the lowest-BIC model maps to $1.0$ and the others to $(0,1]$. This criterion
rewards predictive fit while penalising the parameter count. It therefore
allows us to compare the parsimony of the two-regressor cycle factor directly
to the six-yield specification.

\subsection{Small-sample intervals: the stationary block bootstrap}
We obtain finite-sample distributions from the stationary block bootstrap of
\citet{politis1994}, which resamples contiguous blocks of random
(geometrically distributed) length with mean $L$ and wraps around the sample
end. We set the mean block length to at least the twelve-month overlap so that
the resampled series preserve the serial dependence of the overlapping returns.
We bootstrap two quantities:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item the predictive $t$-statistic ($L=12$), where we refit $\rxbar\sim\CF$ on
    each pseudo-sample and studentise the slope with the same $18$-lag HAC
    standard error as the headline estimate, reported as a $[5\%,95\%]$
    interval; and
  \item the adjusted $R^{2}$ ($L=18$), which we refit by OLS on each
    pseudo-sample and report with its $[5\%,95\%]$ interval, following the
    \citet{dahlquist2013} convention for the predictability tables.
\end{itemize}
It is essential to match the bootstrap HAC bandwidth to the headline bandwidth.
If we ignored the overlap inside the bootstrap, the $t$-statistic would be
spuriously inflated.

\section{Expectations-Hypothesis Monte Carlo}
\label{sec:meth-ehmc}

A predictive $R^{2}$ is only informative relative to what the expectations
hypothesis would itself produce in a finite sample. Even under the EH, that is,
with zero risk premia, realised twelve-month returns are non-zero because they
embed the future yield $y_{t+12}$. The in-sample predictive $R^{2}$ therefore
has a non-degenerate null distribution. We characterise this distribution by
Monte Carlo, following \citet{cieslak2015}, Section~1.

The data-generating process has two AR(1) state variables, trend inflation
$\tau_{t}$ (persistence $\phi_{\tau}$) and a real factor $r_{t}$ (persistence
$\phi_{r}$), driving the nominal short rate
$i_{t}=\delta_{0}+\delta_{\tau}\tau_{t}+\delta_{r}r_{t}$ with
$\delta_{0}=0,\ \delta_{\tau}=1.43,\ \delta_{r}=1$. Under the EH, the $n$-year
yield is the average expected future short rate and hence affine in the states,
\begin{equation}
  y^{(n)}_{t} = \delta_{0}
     + \delta_{\tau}\,\tau_{t}\,\bar{A}(\phi_{\tau},N)
     + \delta_{r}\,r_{t}\,\bar{A}(\phi_{r},N),
  \qquad
  \bar{A}(\phi,N) = \frac{1-\phi^{N}}{(1-\phi)\,N},
  \label{eq:meth-eh}
\end{equation}
with $N=12n$ months. We calibrate the innovation variances so that
$\mathrm{sd}(\tau)=1.90\%$ and the one-year cycle has $\mathrm{sd}=1.74\%$. The
latter pins down $\sigma_{r}$ through the one-year yield's loading on $r$,
since the one-year cycle is the part of $y^{(1)}$ orthogonal to $\tau$. For
each draw, we simulate the states (with a burn-in), build EH yields on the
maturity menu, form the duration-standardised maturity-averaged return $\rxbar$
exactly as in \Cref{ch:framework} ($D^{(n)}=n$, $n\in\{2,5,10\}$), regress it
on trend inflation and the real factor, and store the adjusted $R^{2}$. We
summarise the null distribution by its $5$th, $50$th, and $95$th percentiles
over $10{,}000$ draws, evaluated on the grid of persistence pairs tabulated by
\citet{cieslak2015} ($\phi_{\tau}\in\{0.80,0.975,0.999\}$ at $\phi_{r}=0.75$,
and $\phi_{r}\in\{0.60,0.75,0.90\}$ at $\phi_{\tau}=0.975$). The predictor map
is pluggable, so we can use the same engine to evaluate the cycle and six-yield
specifications for comparison.

\paragraph{Calibration gap.} The simulated null $R^{2}$ band is sensitive to the
exact affine mapping and to the calibration of the state variances. Our
implementation reproduces the \emph{qualitative} pattern (a null $95$th
percentile rising with persistence) but does not exactly match the published
band. We document this gap rather than close it, and we interpret the realised
predictive $R^{2}$ in \Cref{ch:results} against our own simulated band, not
the paper's.

\section{Out-of-Sample Evaluation}
\label{sec:meth-oos}

Because in-sample predictability can reflect over-fitting of generated
regressors, we re-evaluate the factors in real time. We rebuild the entire
chain recursively using only data available at each date $t$: we re-fit the
yield-cycle decomposition \eqref{eq:decomp} on an expanding window, we
re-estimate the local predictive regression \eqref{eq:cf-reg}, and we
reconstruct the global and FX-adjusted factors from the recursive local
factors. Trend inflation is already backward-looking, so we reuse it as is. We
respect two distinct cutoffs. The yield-cycle residual is contemporaneous
(regressors and yields are both observed at $t$), whereas the predictive
regression trains only on observations whose twelve-month outcome has already
been realised by $t$ (training dates satisfy $\text{date}+12\text{m}\le t$).
Because both the factor and the forecasting regression respect $t$, the
resulting forecast is ``doubly out-of-sample''.

We measure forecast accuracy by the \citet{campbellthompson2008} out-of-sample
$R^{2}$ against the recursive prevailing-mean benchmark,
\begin{equation}
  R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}} \;=\; 1 -
     \frac{\sum_{t}\big(\rxbar_{t+12}-\widehat{\rxbar}^{\,\text{factor}}_{t+12}\big)^{2}}
          {\sum_{t}\big(\rxbar_{t+12}-\widehat{\rxbar}^{\,\text{mean}}_{t+12}\big)^{2}},
  \label{eq:meth-ctr2}
\end{equation}
where the benchmark forecast is the recursive mean (a regression on a constant)
passed through the same outcome-lag training cutoff. A positive
$R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}$ means that the factor beats the historical average out
of sample. We pool country results by summing the forecast and benchmark sums
of squares across countries before forming the ratio, so that each country
contributes against its own recursive mean. We apply the same protocol to the
cycle factors and to the Cochrane--Piazzesi comparison factors, which gives us
the head-to-head out-of-sample evidence reported in \Cref{ch:robustness}.

\section{Deviations from the Original Studies}
\label{sec:meth-deviations}

For transparency, we collect the substantive departures from \citet{cieslak2015}
and \citet{dahlquist2013} in this section. We revisit each of them where it
bears on the results.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Maturity menu.} Yields are available at $\{1,2,4,5,9,10\}$ years
    rather than the finer grid of the original papers. We form individual excess
    returns for $n\in\{2,5,10\}$, the maturities with an observed adjacent
    shorter yield.
  \item \textbf{Sample.} The G10 panel spans 1989/1994--2026
    (\Cref{sec:data-coverage}), which is shorter than and not contemporaneous
    with the original US samples, so we expect the replicated levels to differ
    from the published numbers.
  \item \textbf{Inference.} We replace the exact reverse-regression delta method
    with HAC ($18$-lag Newey--West) $t$-statistics, for the identification
    reason given in \Cref{sec:meth-inference}.
  \item \textbf{Duration convention.} We use $D^{(n)}=n$ (continuous
    compounding) for the duration-standardisation rather than $n/(1+y^{(n)})$.
  \item \textbf{FX-adjusted factor.} We build $\FXGCF_{t}$ top-down on the
    average cycle predictors rather than as a projection on $\GCF_{t}$
    (\Cref{sec:fw-fxgcf}).
\end{itemize}
These choices keep our construction internally consistent and reproducible
while remaining as close to the source methodology as the data permit.
