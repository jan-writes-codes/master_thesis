\chapter{Empirical Findings}
\label{ch:results}

In this chapter, we present the empirical core of our thesis. In
\Cref{ch:replication}, we established that our estimation engine reproduces the
United States evidence of \citet{cieslak2015} and the international evidence of
\citet{dahlquist2013}. The local cycle factor recovers the published sign
pattern and a return-forecasting factor that correlates $0.69$ with the average
cycle, compared to the $0.61$ that \citet{cieslak2015} report. Building on this
validation, we now turn to the three research questions in the order set out in
\Cref{sec:fw-hypotheses}. In Phase~I, we ask whether the macro-anchored cycle
mechanism predicts bond returns market by market across the G10. In Phase~II, we
ask whether a single GDP-weighted global factor subsumes these local factors,
to test international market integration. In Phase~III, we
ask whether currency risk overturns the predictability for a US-dollar investor
and, if so, whether the FX-adjusted global factor restores it.

In all regressions, we forecast the one-year-ahead, duration-standardised,
maturity-averaged excess return $\rxbar_{i,t+12}$ of \eqref{eq:rxbar} from
time-$t$ predictors. We estimate them by ordinary least squares and evaluate
them with the $18$-lag Newey--West HAC inference of \Cref{sec:meth-inference}.
The result tables and figures are generated by \texttt{main\_results.R}.

\section{The Local Cycle Factor across the G10}
\label{sec:res-phase1}

The first question we examine is whether the cycle factor of \citet{cieslak2015},
which was designed for and tested on US Treasuries, also predicts
government-bond returns in each member of the G10. The relevant specification is
the local predictive regression \eqref{eq:h-local}, $\rxbar_{i,t+12}=\alpha_i+\beta_i\,\CF_{i,t}
+\varepsilon_{i,t+12}$. Because the local cycle factor $\CF_{i,t}$ is the fitted
value of the excess return on the two cycle predictors
\eqref{eq:cf-reg}--\eqref{eq:cf}, the regression on $\CF$ has slope identically
one and an $R^{2}$ equal to the fit of that underlying forecasting regression.
In \Cref{tab:mr-phase1}, we therefore report the informative object directly:
the country-by-country projection of $\rxbar_{i,t+12}$ on the one-year cycle
$\cyc^{(1)}_{i,t}$ and the average cycle $\cycbar_{i,t}$, of which $\CF$ is the
fitted combination.

\begin{table}[t]
  \caption{Phase~I: local cycle-factor predictability across the G10}
  \label{tab:mr-phase1}
  \centering
  \input{tables/mr_t1_phase1}
\end{table}


We find that the cycle mechanism extends to the international cross-section, and
it does so with a uniform structure. In \Cref{tab:mr-phase1} we can
see that the one-year cycle enters negatively ($\gamma_{1}<0$) and the average
cycle positively ($\gamma_{2}>0$) in every one of the eleven markets. This is
exactly the sign pattern that \citet{cieslak2015} document for the United
States, in which the level of the short-rate cycle and the slope of the cycle
curve carry opposite-signed information about future returns. The joint Wald
test rejects the no-predictability null at the one-percent level in nine
countries and at the five-percent level in ten. The only exception is
Switzerland, where the cycle slopes are correctly signed but jointly
insignificant ($p=0.20$). When we pool the panel with country fixed effects,
both loadings are highly significant ($t=-10.7$ on the one-year cycle and
$t=15.5$ on the average cycle).

The magnitude of this predictability is considerable. In
\Cref{fig:mr-r2-phase1}, we plot the in-sample $R^{2}$ of \eqref{eq:h-local} by
country. We can see that it ranges from $7\%$ in Switzerland to $34\%$ in the
Netherlands, averages $25\%$ across the G10, and reaches $33\%$ in the United
States, which closely matches the magnitude in the original study. Seven of the
eleven markets exceed an $R^{2}$ of $24\%$. The cross-country dispersion is itself
informative. Predictability is strongest in the large, liquid core markets (the
Netherlands, the United States, Canada, Belgium, Germany) and weakest in
Switzerland.

Furthermore, we find that the predictability holds at each individual maturity
and not only for the maturity-averaged return. In \Cref{tab:mr-phase1-mat}, we
repeat the exercise of \citet{cieslak2015}, Table~4, for the G10: we regress the
duration-standardised individual excess return $\rx^{(n)}_{i,t+12}/n$ on the
local cycle factor for $n\in\{2,5,10\}$ years, market by market. The factor
loads positively at all three maturities in every market and significantly so in
ten of the eleven (Switzerland is again the exception). The loadings are flat to
rising in maturity, near $0.6$--$0.9$ at two years, $1.1$--$1.3$ at five, and
$1.0$--$1.2$ at ten, with pooled values of $0.78$, $1.16$ and $1.06$
respectively. This is the signature of a single return-forecasting factor. We see the same
effect in \citet{cieslak2015} for the United States. The per-maturity $R^{2}$
tracks the maturity-averaged figures and
is largest at the five- and ten-year points, reaching $35\%$ at ten years for
the United States and $36\%$ at five years for the Netherlands.

We can therefore answer the first research question affirmatively. The
macro-anchored cycle of \citet{cieslak2015} is not a US-specific phenomenon. It
predicts one-year bond excess returns, with the theoretically predicted sign
structure and economically large $R^{2}$, throughout the G10, and it does so at
every point of the maturity curve.

\section{Does the Global Factor Subsume the Local Factor?}
\label{sec:res-phase2}

If government-bond markets are internationally integrated, the predictable
component of returns should reflect a common source of risk rather than
eleven separate national cycles \citep{dahlquist2013}. The GDP-weighted global
cycle factor $\GCF_{t}$ of \eqref{eq:gcf} is the natural summary of this common
component. In \Cref{fig:s5-gcf}, we can see that it tracks the centre of mass of
the country-level factors. It correlates most strongly with the large core
economies (the United States, $0.96$; Germany, $0.94$), but this is not purely a
weight effect: Sweden and Canada also correlate above $0.89$ despite small
weights, while Italy is the clear outlier at $0.21$, so the global factor
captures common business-cycle co-movement rather than the biggest economies
mechanically (\Cref{tab:mr-gcf-corr}). In Phase~II, we test integration directly
through the horse race \eqref{eq:h-horse}--\eqref{eq:h-global}.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/s5_gcf.pdf}
  \caption[The global cycle factor and the eleven local cycle factors]{The GDP-weighted global cycle factor $\GCF_{t}$ \eqref{eq:gcf} (heavy
    line) against the eleven country-level local cycle factors $\CF_{i,t}$
    (light lines).}
  \label{fig:s5-gcf}
\end{figure}

Under full integration, the global factor should drive out the local factor, so
that $\CF_{i,t}$ adds no predictive power once $\GCF_{t}$ is included. To
isolate the genuinely local content, we orthogonalise the local factor against
the global factor. Following the construction of \citet{dahlquist2013},
$\CF^{\perp}_{i,t}$ is the residual of $\CF_{i,t}$ on $\GCF_{t}$. We then
estimate the joint regression
$\rxbar_{i,t+12}=\alpha_i+\beta_i\,\CF^{\perp}_{i,t}+\gamma_i\,\GCF_{t}
+\varepsilon$ per country. \Cref{tab:mr-phase2} reports the results.

  \label{tab:mr-phase2}
  \centering

\end{table}

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/mr_f2_hr_tstats.pdf}
  \caption[Phase~II horse race: per-country HAC $t$-statistics]{Phase~II horse race: per-country HAC $t$-statistics on the
    orthogonalised local factor $\CF^{\perp}$ and the global factor $\GCF$ in the
    joint regression \eqref{eq:h-horse}. Dashed lines mark $\pm1.96$.}
  \label{fig:mr-hr-tstats}
\end{figure}

We find that the evidence favours a strong but incomplete form of integration.
In \Cref{tab:mr-phase2} we can see that the global factor is statistically
significant in every market, with HAC $t$-statistics between $2.1$ and $4.5$,
and that on its own it explains as much of the return variation as the local
factor does: the cross-country average $R^{2}$ is $25\%$ for the local factor
and a nearly identical $25\%$ for the global factor. A single,
GDP-weighted international factor thus reproduces the predictability of eleven
separately estimated national factors. Once the global factor is included, the
orthogonalised local factor is significant in only three of the eleven markets,
namely Italy ($t=3.7$), the Netherlands ($t=3.3$) and Belgium ($t=2.4$), and is
statistically indistinguishable from zero in the other eight. We can observe this contrast in
\Cref{fig:mr-hr-tstats}: the global bars clear the $1.96$ threshold everywhere,
whereas the local bars only do so for the three markets named.

In \Cref{fig:mr-r2-ladder}, we can trace the same conclusion through the $R^{2}$
ladder. For the eight markets in which the local factor is subsumed, adding
$\CF^{\perp}$ to the global factor lifts the $R^{2}$ only trivially. For the
three markets with surviving local content, the increment is material: Italy
rises from $7.4\%$ on the global factor alone to $31.3\%$ jointly, and the
Netherlands from $26.4\%$ to $35.0\%$. Furthermore, the joint Wald test rejects
in all eleven markets, and the rejections survive the Benjamini--Hochberg
correction for multiple testing across the panel. This suggests that the
combined cycle information is priced everywhere. On the pooled G10 panel with
country fixed effects, both factors enter significantly ($t=6.1$ on
$\CF^{\perp}$ and $t=14.5$ on $\GCF$), with a joint $R^{2}$ of $0.29$ of which
the global factor accounts for the larger part (\Cref{tab:mr-phase2}).

Two readings of the three exceptions are consistent with our framework. Italy,
Belgium and the Netherlands retain idiosyncratic cyclical predictability that
the GDP-weighted global factor, which is dominated by the largest economies,
does not capture. The broad message, however, is that of
\citet{dahlquist2013}: the predictable component of international bond returns
is overwhelmingly global. We therefore answer the second research question as
follows. The global cycle factor subsumes the local factor in the majority of
the G10 and leaves statistically robust local predictability in only a minority
of markets.

\section{The Global Investor and the FX-Adjusted Factor}
\label{sec:res-phase3}

In the first two phases, we adopted the perspective of a domestic investor:
returns are measured in local currency and currency risk is set aside. For a US
investor who holds foreign bonds unhedged, the relevant object is the dollar
excess return \eqref{eq:rx-usd}, which augments the local return with the
realised currency return and nets out the US short rate. Throughout, ``dollar''
refers to the US dollar. In Phase~III, we ask
two questions for this investor. First, how far does currency risk erode the
global-factor predictability we documented in Phase~II? Second, does the
FX-adjusted global cycle factor $\FXGCF_{t}$, which we build bottom-up as the
GDP-weighted average of the per-country USD cycle factors
(\Cref{sec:fw-fxgcf}), recover what an unadjusted factor loses?
\Cref{tab:mr-phase3} reports the dollar-investor regressions \eqref{eq:h-usd-global}
and \eqref{eq:h-fxgcf}.

\end{table}

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/mr_f3_usd_r2.pdf}

    global factor \eqref{eq:h-usd-global} versus the FX-adjusted global factor
    \eqref{eq:h-fxgcf}.}
  \label{fig:mr-usd-r2}
\end{figure}

We find that currency risk is a dominant influence on the dollar investor's
returns and that it sharply weakens the global factor. The cross-country average
predictive $R^{2}$ of the global factor falls from $25\%$ on local-currency
returns to $9\%$ on dollar returns, and the factor's HAC $t$-statistic drops
below the conventional threshold for the entire euro-area bloc, Switzerland and
Japan. Predictability survives currency conversion only where the exchange-rate
exposure is small or itself cyclical: in the United States, whose dollar return
mechanically equals its local return because it carries no currency leg
($R^{2}=33\%$), in Canada ($t=2.8$), and, more marginally, in Sweden and the
United Kingdom ($t\approx 2.0$). In these cases the currency return is either
absent (the United States carries no currency leg) or tends to move with the
domestic rate cycle, so that part of it is already captured by the bond factor
rather than adding independent noise; for the euro bloc, Switzerland, and Japan,
by contrast, the exchange rate moves largely independently of the bond cycle and
its volatility swamps the signal. We can see this collapse in
\Cref{fig:mr-usd-r2}, where the dark bars for the foreign markets are only a
fraction of their local-currency heights. This is the international-bond
analogue of the result that long-horizon currency-converted returns are hard to
predict: the volatility of the one-year currency return swamps the cyclical
signal in the bond.

The FX-adjusted factor was designed for exactly this problem, and we find that
it helps substantially. Across the panel, it raises the dollar-investor
$R^{2}$ in ten of the eleven markets and lifts the $t$-statistic in nine.
The cross-country average $R^{2}$ recovers from $9\%$ under the global factor
to $14\%$ under the FX-adjusted factor, a substantial gain in the
dollar-investor fit. Moreover, markets that were insignificant under
$\GCF_{t}$ become significant under $\FXGCF_{t}$: the entire euro-area bloc
moves to $t\approx 2.6$, Switzerland to $t=3.0$, and Japan to $t=2.8$, so
that the factor is significant in ten of eleven markets (the exception is the
United Kingdom). In \Cref{fig:mr-gcf-fxgcf}, we can see why new
information can be captured: the FX-adjusted factor correlates $0.81$ with the
unadjusted global factor, so the two series share the global cycle but the
FX-adjusted factor carries genuinely distinct information about the currency
leg of the dollar return. This is comparable in spirit to the correlation of
about $0.50$ that \citet{dahlquist2013} report for their FX-adjusted global
CP factor. We note that this property depends on the construction: the
top-down variant of the factor, which projects the average dollar return on
the average cycle predictors, is nearly collinear with the global factor in
our sample (correlation $0.99$) and adds almost nothing. We compare the
constructions in \Cref{sec:rob-fxgcf-construction} and take up the economic
interpretation in \Cref{ch:discussion}.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/mr_f4_gcf_fxgcf.pdf}
  \caption[The global and FX-adjusted global cycle factors]{The global cycle factor $\GCF_{t}$ \eqref{eq:gcf} and the FX-adjusted
    global cycle factor $\FXGCF_{t}$ \eqref{eq:fxgcf} over the common sample.
    The two series share the global cycle but are distinct (correlation
    $0.81$).}
  \label{fig:mr-gcf-fxgcf}
\end{figure}

Our answer to the third research question is therefore twofold. Currency risk
does break the global-factor predictability for an unhedged dollar investor in
most markets and reduces the average $R^{2}$ by roughly two-thirds. The
bottom-up FX-adjusted global cycle factor restores a large part of this
predictability in-sample, raising the average fit to $14\%$ and restoring
significance in ten of eleven markets. Before we ask whether this in-sample
recovery survives in real time, we look more closely at how the two global
factors relate to one another over the sample, because this relationship is
what decides whether the FX adjustment can matter at all.

The global factor and its FX-adjusted counterpart are two views of the same
cross-section of cycles, seen through two different currencies of measurement.
The unadjusted factor $\GCF_{t}$ prices the \emph{hedged}, local-currency
return, so it is a summary of \emph{interest-rate-cycle risk}, the compensation
for holding duration when yields sit far from their macro anchor. The
FX-adjusted factor $\FXGCF_{t}$ prices the \emph{unhedged} dollar return, so it
carries that same duration signal \emph{plus} the currency leg that a dollar
investor cannot avoid. Because the two are built identically except for the
currency of the return \eqref{eq:fxcf-reg}--\eqref{eq:fxgcf}, their difference
isolates the currency component,
\begin{equation}
  \FXGCF_{t}-\GCF_{t}
  \;=\; \sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}} w_{i,t}\bigl(\FXCF_{i,t}-\CF_{i,t}\bigr),
  \label{eq:fxgcf-wedge}
\end{equation}
which we call the \emph{wedge}. Reading $\GCF_{t}$ as the interest-rate factor
and the wedge as the FX factor lets us ask a sharper question than the
predictive tables answer on their own: not only whether the FX adjustment
helps on average, but \emph{when} the two factors carry genuinely different
information, and why.

\Cref{tab:fxd-properties} collects their properties. The wedge is not a small
residual: its standard deviation ($0.35$) is as large as that of the
interest-rate factor itself ($0.33$) and it is highly persistent (first-order
autocorrelation $0.99$, half-life of about five years), so the FX component is a
slow-moving second factor in its own right. Yet
the full-sample correlation between the two factors, $0.81$, hides considerable
time variation. \Cref{fig:fxd-rollcorr} plots the $36$-month rolling
correlation of $\GCF_{t}$ and $\FXGCF_{t}$. It swings from near zero in the
early 1990s and around the global financial crisis to essentially one for most
of the post-2008 decade: split at 2008, the two factors correlate $0.54$ in the
first half of the sample and $0.95$ in the second. In other words, the FX
adjustment does distinct work in the earlier sample, and almost none in the
later one.

The currency leg carries
independent information only to the extent that currency returns are themselves
predictable, and the classic source of that predictability is the cross-country
interest-rate differential through the failure of uncovered interest parity
\citep{fama1984}.
When policy rates are high and spread apart, differentials are wide, the carry
channel is active, and the currency component moves on its own; when rates
converge to a synchronised zero lower bound, differentials compress and the
currency component has little left to say that the bond cycle does not already
say. \Cref{fig:fxd-regime} shows exactly this pattern: the rolling correlation
of the two factors falls when the GDP-weighted G10 short rate is high and rises
as it approaches zero, with the two moving in opposite directions
(correlation $-0.42$). The regimes in \Cref{tab:fxd-properties} tell the same
story in levels: the low-correlation first half of the sample is the high-rate
regime (mean short rate $3.9\%$, cross-country yield dispersion $1.37$), and
the near-collinear second half is the low-rate regime (mean short rate $0.9\%$,
dispersion $0.71$). The brief decoupling at the right edge of
\Cref{fig:fxd-regime}, as rates rose again after 2022, fits the same logic.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/fxd_f2_regime.pdf}
  \caption[The FX premium decouples in the high-rate regime]{The FX premium decouples in the high-rate regime. Top: the
    $36$-month rolling correlation of $\GCF_{t}$ and $\FXGCF_{t}$. Bottom: the
    GDP-weighted G10 one-year yield ($36$-month mean). The two series correlate
    $-0.42$: the factors are most distinct when rates are high and
    differentials wide, and collapse onto each other at the synchronised zero
    lower bound.}
  \label{fig:fxd-regime}
\end{figure}

This dynamic reconciles two facts. It
explains why our full-sample FX adjustment recovers less than
\citet{dahlquist2013} report. Their forward-rate factor retains the
rate-level information that drives carry and their sample is weighted towards
the high-differential decades. Our detrended cycle factor removes the level and
our sample is dominated by the post-2008 low-rate regime, in which the currency
component seems nearly redundant.
It also anticipates the out-of-sample evidence below: a factor whose extra
information is concentrated in one rate regime, and largely absent in the
regime that fills the second half of the sample, should help only
intermittently in real time.

\section{Out-of-Sample Predictability}
\label{sec:res-oos}

The evidence so far is in-sample. Because the cycle factors are generated
regressors, fitted on the same sample over which they are then evaluated, an
in-sample $R^{2}$ can overstate what a real-time investor could have utilised.
In this section, we therefore re-evaluate the three phases out of sample,
using the fully-recursive protocol of \Cref{sec:meth-oos}: the
entire chain (the yield-cycle decomposition, the local predictive regression,
and the global aggregation) is rebuilt at each date from data available only up
to that date, and every recursive factor forecast is scored by the
\citet{campbellthompson2008} out-of-sample $R^{2}$ \eqref{eq:meth-ctr2}.
A positive value means that the factor's real-time forecasts are more accurate
than simply extrapolating the historical average, zero means a tie, and a
negative value means that the factor does worse than the mean.
Out of sample, even small positive values are economically meaningful and
negative values are common. \Cref{tab:mr-oos} should therefore be read down each
column for the sign and the cross-factor ranking, not across the two columns for
a change in magnitude. We compare the cycle factor against the forward-rate
factor, and stress-test the estimation scheme, in \Cref{ch:robustness}.

\Cref{fig:s8-oos} shows the pooled picture across the four specifications, and we
arrive at three main messages, one per phase. First, we find that the local
cycle factor does not survive the transition to real time: its pooled
$R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}$ is $-0.07$, and it beats the prevailing mean in only two
of the eleven markets (the United States and Canada). Second, the global factor is the exception, and a notable
one. Its pooled $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}$ is $+0.05$ and it is positive in ten of
the eleven markets, as we can see in \Cref{fig:mr-oos}. Aggregating the eleven
national cycles into one GDP-weighted factor averages away the country-specific
estimation noise that causes the local factor to fail, so the global factor
predicts out of sample where the local factor cannot. We consider this strong
support in our thesis for integration: the global cycle factor is not
merely the more efficient in-sample summary, it is the only cycle factor with
genuine real-time predictive content. Moreover, this real-time predictability is
economically exploitable. In \Cref{ch:strategy}, we turn the global factor into
a bond-timing strategy for a currency-hedged investor and show that it raises
the Sharpe ratio of a global bond portfolio and delivers a positive
certainty-equivalent gain out of sample. Third, for the dollar investor, the
out-of-sample picture qualifies the strong in-sample finding of Phase~III. The
recursive $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}$ is clearly negative for the unadjusted global
factor ($-0.07$), which beats the recursive mean in only two markets. The
FX-adjusted factor erases this loss: it turns positive at the pooled level
($+0.021$) and beats the mean in six of the eleven markets. The pooled margin
is, however, an order of magnitude smaller than the in-sample gain, and, as
\Cref{ch:robustness} shows, it is sensitive to the estimation scheme. The FX
adjustment therefore does what it was designed to do, but the real-time edge
it delivers for the currency-converted investor is more modest than the
in-sample fit suggests.

The recursive factors themselves show us the mechanism behind this divergence.
In \Cref{fig:s8-gcf-conv}, we overlay the global factor with its
fully-recursive counterpart $\GCF^{\mathrm{oos}}_{t}$. After the roughly ten-year
burn-in that is required to estimate trend inflation, the recursive series
converges to the full-sample factor and tracks it closely thereafter, which is
the signature of a stable real-time estimator. The recursive \emph{local} factors
converge far less cleanly. Their period-by-period correlation with the
full-sample local factor is materially below one for several markets (right
panel of \Cref{fig:s8-gcf-conv}), which is exactly why their out-of-sample
forecasts deteriorate while the aggregated global factor remains reliable.

For the dollar investor, \Cref{fig:s8-oos-usd} traces the same contrast across
the panel: the unadjusted global factor beats the recursive mean in only two
markets, whereas the FX-adjusted factor turns positive in six, recovering part
of the currency-converted predictability in real time.

\section{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:res-summary}

Taken together, the three phases give us a coherent picture of international
bond-return predictability through the lens of the macro-anchored cycle. First,
the cycle factor of \citet{cieslak2015} generalises cleanly to the G10: it
predicts one-year excess returns with the theoretically predicted sign structure
in all eleven markets and with an average in-sample $R^{2}$ of $25\%$. Second, we find that
this predictability is overwhelmingly a global phenomenon. A single GDP-weighted
global cycle factor is significant in every market and matches the explanatory
power of the local factors. Once it is included, genuinely local predictability
survives in only three of the eleven markets. The evidence thus supports strong,
though not complete, integration of government-bond risk premia. This is the
central message of \citet{dahlquist2013}, now established for the cycle factor
rather than the forward-rate factor. Third, the global investor's problem is
currency risk: dollar conversion erodes roughly two-thirds of the global
factor's predictive power. The bottom-up FX-adjusted factor recovers much of
it in-sample, substantially raising the average dollar-investor $R^{2}$ from $9\%$
to $14\%$ and restoring significance in ten of eleven markets.

Fourth, these conclusions survive out of sample and, in one respect, even
sharpen. The global cycle factor beats the recursive prevailing mean clearly and
robustly (pooled $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}=+0.05$, positive in ten of eleven
markets), while the local cycle factor and the \emph{unadjusted} dollar-investor
factor do not (both negative, positive in only two markets each). The
FX-adjusted factor sits in between: it turns the unadjusted dollar factor's
loss into a pooled gain ($+0.021$, positive in six of eleven markets), but
this real-time edge is much smaller than its in-sample fit and, as
\Cref{ch:robustness} shows, sensitive to the estimation scheme. Real-time
predictability is therefore clearly led by the global cycle factor, with the FX
adjustment recovering a genuine but conditional edge for the currency-converted
investor. The contrast between the local factor's strong in-sample fit and its
negative out-of-sample $R^{2}$ is a clear sign that global predictability
deserves our attention.

We develop the summary out-of-sample evidence above in full, per country, across
subsamples, and against the forward-rate factor, in \Cref{ch:robustness}, and we
take up the economic interpretation of all four findings in \Cref{ch:discussion}.
