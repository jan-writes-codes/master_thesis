\chapter{Main Results}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter presents the empirical core of the thesis. \Cref{ch:replication}
established that the estimation engine reproduces the United States evidence of
\citet{cieslak2015} and the international evidence of \citet{dahlquist2013}; the
local cycle factor recovers the published sign pattern and a return-forecasting
factor that correlates $0.70$ with the average cycle, against the $0.61$ that
\citet{cieslak2015} report. Building on that validation, the analysis now turns
to the three research questions in the order set out in
\Cref{sec:fw-hypotheses}. Phase~I asks whether the macro-anchored cycle
mechanism predicts bond returns market by market across the G10. Phase~II asks
whether a single GDP-weighted global factor subsumes these local factors, the
natural test of international market integration. Phase~III asks whether
currency risk overturns the predictability for a US-dollar investor and, if so,
whether the purpose-built FX-adjusted global factor restores it.

All regressions forecast the one-year-ahead, duration-standardised,
maturity-averaged excess return $\rxbar_{i,t+12}$ of \eqref{eq:rxbar} from
time-$t$ predictors, are estimated by ordinary least squares, and are evaluated
with the $18$-lag Newey--West HAC inference of \Cref{sec:meth-inference}; the
reverse-regression caveat stated there applies throughout. The result tables and
figures are generated by \texttt{main\_results.R}. Magnitudes of the predictive
$R^{2}$ are read against the expectations-hypothesis Monte Carlo of
\Cref{sec:meth-ehmc}, whose $95$th percentile under the null sits well below the
$R^{2}$ documented here.

\section{The Local Cycle Factor across the G10}
\label{sec:res-phase1}

The first question is whether the cycle factor of \citet{cieslak2015}, which was
designed for and tested on US Treasuries, predicts government-bond returns in
each member of the G10. The relevant specification is the local predictive
regression \eqref{eq:h-local}, $\rxbar_{i,t+12}=\alpha_i+\beta_i\,\CF_{i,t}
+\varepsilon_{i,t+12}$. Because the local cycle factor $\CF_{i,t}$ is the fitted
value of the excess return on the two cycle predictors
\eqref{eq:cf-reg}--\eqref{eq:cf}, the regression on $\CF$ has slope identically
one and an $R^{2}$ equal to the fit of that underlying forecasting regression.
\Cref{tab:mr-phase1} therefore reports the informative object directly: the
country-by-country projection of $\rxbar_{i,t+12}$ on the one-year cycle
$\cyc^{(1)}_{i,t}$ and the average cycle $\cycbar_{i,t}$, of which $\CF$ is the
fitted combination.

  \caption{Phase~I --- local cycle-factor predictability across the G10. Each
    row is the predictive regression $\rxbar_{i,t+12}=\gamma_{0}
    +\gamma_{1}\cyc^{(1)}_{i,t}+\gamma_{2}\cycbar_{i,t}+\varepsilon$ underlying
    the local cycle factor \eqref{eq:cf}, with Newey--West HAC $t$-statistics in
    parentheses and the joint Wald $p$-value that both slopes are zero. The final
    row pools the panel with country fixed effects.}
  \label{tab:mr-phase1}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/mr_f1_r2_phase1.pdf}
  \caption{In-sample $R^{2}$ of the local predictive regression
    \eqref{eq:h-local}, $\rxbar_{i,t+12}\sim\CF_{i,t}$, by country.}
  \label{fig:mr-r2-phase1}
\end{figure}

The cycle mechanism extends to the international cross-section, and it does so
with a strikingly uniform structure. The one-year cycle enters negatively
($\gamma_{1}<0$) and the average cycle positively ($\gamma_{2}>0$) in every one
of the eleven markets---exactly the sign pattern \citet{cieslak2015} document for
the United States, in which the level of the short-rate cycle and the slope of
the cycle curve carry opposite-signed information about future returns. The
joint Wald test rejects the no-predictability null at the one-percent level in
nine countries and at the five-percent level in ten; the lone exception is
Switzerland, where the cycle slopes are correctly signed but jointly
insignificant ($p=0.20$). Pooling the panel with country fixed effects, both
loadings are overwhelmingly significant ($t=-10.8$ on the one-year cycle and
$t=15.7$ on the average cycle).

The economic magnitude of this predictability is large. \Cref{fig:mr-r2-phase1}
plots the in-sample $R^{2}$ of \eqref{eq:h-local} by country. It ranges from
$7\%$ in Switzerland to $34\%$ in the Netherlands, averages $25\%$ across the
G10, and reaches $33\%$ in the United States---closely matching the magnitude in
the original study. Eight of the eleven markets exceed an $R^{2}$ of $24\%$.
These values lie far above the $95$th percentile of the predictive $R^{2}$ that
the expectations hypothesis generates in a sample of this length
(\Cref{sec:meth-ehmc}), so the predictability is not an artefact of overlapping
returns or of fitting a generated regressor in finite samples. The cross-country
dispersion is itself informative: predictability is strongest in the large,
liquid core markets (the Netherlands, the United States, Canada, Belgium,
Germany) and weakest in Switzerland, whose persistently low and compressed
yields leave little cyclical variation for the factor to explain.

The predictability holds at each individual maturity, not only for the
maturity-averaged return. \Cref{tab:mr-phase1-mat} repeats the exercise of
\citet{cieslak2015}, Table~4, for the G10: the duration-standardised individual
excess return $\rx^{(n)}_{i,t+12}/n$ is regressed on the local cycle factor for
$n\in\{2,5,10\}$ years, market by market. The factor loads positively at all
three maturities in every market and significantly so in ten of the eleven
(Switzerland again excepted). The loadings are flat-to-rising in maturity---near
$0.6$--$0.9$ at two years, $1.1$--$1.3$ at five, and $1.0$--$1.2$ at ten, with
pooled values of $0.79$, $1.16$ and $1.06$ respectively---which is the signature
of a \emph{single} return-forecasting factor that prices the whole curve rather
than one maturity segment, exactly as in the United States. The per-maturity
$R^{2}$ tracks the maturity-averaged figures and is largest at the five- and
ten-year points, reaching $35\%$ at ten years for the United States and $36\%$ at
five years for the Netherlands.

\begin{table}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{tables/mr_t1b_maturity.pdf}
  \caption{Phase~I by maturity --- predicting individual excess returns with the
    cycle factor. Each block regresses the duration-standardised excess return
    $\rx^{(n)}_{i,t+12}/n$ on the local cycle factor $\CF_{i,t}$ \eqref{eq:h-local}
    for $n\in\{2,5,10\}$; cells report the loading with its Newey--West HAC

  \label{tab:mr-phase1-mat}

The answer to the first research question is therefore affirmative. The
macro-anchored cycle of \citet{cieslak2015} is not a US-specific phenomenon: it
predicts one-year bond excess returns, with the theoretically predicted sign
structure and economically large $R^{2}$, throughout the G10, and it does so at
every point of the maturity curve.

\section{Does the Global Factor Subsume the Local Factor?}
\label{sec:res-phase2}

If government-bond markets are internationally integrated, the predictable
component of returns should reflect a \emph{common} source of risk rather than
eleven separate national cycles \citep{dahlquist2013}. The GDP-weighted global
cycle factor $\GCF_{t}$ of \eqref{eq:gcf} is the natural summary of that common
component; \Cref{fig:s5-gcf} shows it tracking the centre of mass of the
country-level factors. Phase~II tests integration directly through the horse
race \eqref{eq:h-horse}--\eqref{eq:h-global}.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/s5_gcf.pdf}
  \caption{The GDP-weighted global cycle factor $\GCF_{t}$ \eqref{eq:gcf} (heavy
    line) against the eleven country-level local cycle factors $\CF_{i,t}$
    (light lines).}
  \label{fig:s5-gcf}
\end{figure}

Under full integration, the global
factor should drive out the local factor, so that $\CF_{i,t}$ adds no predictive
power once $\GCF_{t}$ is included. To isolate the genuinely local content, the
local factor is orthogonalised against the global factor---$\CF^{\perp}_{i,t}$ is
the residual of $\CF_{i,t}$ on $\GCF_{t}$, following the construction of
\citet{dahlquist2013}---and the joint regression
$\rxbar_{i,t+12}=\alpha_i+\beta_i\,\CF^{\perp}_{i,t}+\gamma_i\,\GCF_{t}
+\varepsilon$ is estimated per country. \Cref{tab:mr-phase2} reports the result.

\begin{table}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{tables/mr_t2_phase2.pdf}
  \caption{Phase~II --- global versus local cycle factor. ``R2 CF'' and
    ``R2 GCF'' are the single-factor fits \eqref{eq:h-local} and
    \eqref{eq:h-global}; ``R2 joint'' is the horse race
    $\rxbar_{i,t+12}\sim\CF^{\perp}+\GCF$ \eqref{eq:h-horse}. HAC $t$-statistics;
    the joint Wald $p$-value and its Benjamini--Hochberg FDR adjustment across
    the eleven markets test no joint predictability.}
  \label{tab:mr-phase2}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/mr_f2_hr_tstats.pdf}
  \caption{Phase~II horse race: per-country HAC $t$-statistics on the
    orthogonalised local factor $\CF^{\perp}$ and the global factor $\GCF$ in the
    joint regression \eqref{eq:h-horse}. Dashed lines mark $\pm1.96$.}
  \label{fig:mr-hr-tstats}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/mr_f2_r2_ladder.pdf}
  \caption{Phase~II in-sample $R^{2}$ ladder by country: the local factor alone
    \eqref{eq:h-local}, the global factor alone \eqref{eq:h-global}, and the
    joint horse race \eqref{eq:h-horse}.}
  \label{fig:mr-r2-ladder}
\end{figure}

The evidence favours a strong but incomplete form of integration. The global
factor is statistically significant in \emph{every} market, with HAC
$t$-statistics between $2.1$ and $4.5$, and on its own it explains as much of the
return variation as the local factor does: the cross-country average $R^{2}$ is
$25\%$ for the local factor and an essentially identical $25\%$ for the global
factor. A single, GDP-weighted international factor thus reproduces the
predictability of eleven separately estimated national factors. Once the global
factor is included, the orthogonalised local factor is significant in only three
of the eleven markets---Italy ($t=3.7$), the Netherlands ($t=3.2$) and Belgium
($t=2.4$)---and is statistically indistinguishable from zero in the other eight,
where it sometimes even enters with the wrong sign. \Cref{fig:mr-hr-tstats}
displays this contrast: the global bars clear the $1.96$ threshold everywhere,
whereas the local bars do so only for the three markets named.

\Cref{fig:mr-r2-ladder} traces the same conclusion through the $R^{2}$ ladder.
For the eight markets in which the local factor is subsumed, adding
$\CF^{\perp}$ to the global factor lifts the $R^{2}$ only trivially (for example,
Germany from $29.1\%$ to $29.1\%$, Sweden from $29.6\%$ to $29.9\%$). For the
three markets with surviving local content the increment is material---Italy
rises from $7.8\%$ on the global factor alone to $31.7\%$ jointly, and the
Netherlands from $26.7\%$ to $34.9\%$. The joint Wald test rejects in all eleven
markets, and the rejections survive the Benjamini--Hochberg correction for
multiple testing across the panel, confirming that the combined cycle
information is genuinely priced everywhere.

Two readings of the three exceptions are consistent with the framework. Italy,
Belgium and the Netherlands retain idiosyncratic cyclical predictability that the
GDP-weighted global factor, dominated by the largest economies, does not capture;
for the euro-area members this residual plausibly reflects sovereign-spread and
redenomination dynamics that are local in origin yet absent from the common
factor. The broad message, however, is that of \citet{dahlquist2013}: the
predictable component of international bond returns is overwhelmingly global. The
answer to the second research question is that the global cycle factor subsumes
the local factor in the majority of the G10, leaving statistically robust local
predictability in only a minority of markets.

\section{The Global Investor and the FX-Adjusted Factor}
\label{sec:res-phase3}

The first two phases adopt a domestic investor's perspective: returns are
measured in local currency and currency risk is set aside. For a US investor who
holds foreign bonds unhedged, the relevant object is the dollar excess return
\eqref{eq:rx-usd}, which augments the local return with the realised currency
return and nets out the US short rate. Phase~III asks two questions of this
investor. First, how far does currency risk erode the global-factor
predictability documented in Phase~II? Second, does the FX-adjusted global cycle
factor $\FXGCF_{t}$---built top-down on the GDP-weighted average dollar return
(\Cref{sec:fw-fxgcf})---recover what an unadjusted factor loses?
\Cref{tab:mr-phase3} reports the dollar-investor regressions \eqref{eq:h-usd-global}
and \eqref{eq:h-fxgcf}.

  \caption{Phase~III --- US-dollar investor. The dependent variable is the
    dollar excess return $\rx^{\mathrm{USD}}_{i,t+12}$ \eqref{eq:rx-usd}. The
    GCF block regresses it on the global factor \eqref{eq:h-usd-global}; the
    FXGCF block on the FX-adjusted global factor \eqref{eq:h-fxgcf}. HAC
    $t$-statistics in parentheses. The US row carries no currency leg.}
  \label{tab:mr-phase3}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/mr_f3_usd_r2.pdf}
  \caption{Phase~III: US-dollar-investor in-sample $R^{2}$ by country, the
    global factor \eqref{eq:h-usd-global} versus the FX-adjusted global factor
    \eqref{eq:h-fxgcf}.}
  \label{fig:mr-usd-r2}
\end{figure}

Currency risk is the dominant influence on the dollar investor's returns, and it
sharply weakens the global factor. The cross-country average predictive $R^{2}$
of the global factor falls from $25\%$ on local-currency returns to $8\%$ on
dollar returns, and the factor's HAC $t$-statistic drops below the conventional
threshold for the entire euro-area bloc, Switzerland and Japan. Predictability
survives currency conversion only where the exchange-rate exposure is small or
itself cyclical: the United States, whose dollar return mechanically equals its
local return because it carries no currency leg ($R^{2}=33\%$); Canada
($t=2.9$); and, more marginally, Sweden and the United Kingdom ($t\approx 2.0$).
\Cref{fig:mr-usd-r2} visualises the collapse---the dark bars for the foreign
markets are a fraction of their local-currency heights. This is the
international-bond analogue of the result that long-horizon currency-converted
returns are hard to predict: the volatility of the one-year currency return
swamps the cyclical signal in the bond.

The FX-adjusted factor was designed for exactly this problem, and it helps---but
modestly. Across the panel it raises the dollar-investor $R^{2}$ in ten of the
eleven markets and lifts the HAC $t$-statistic in every foreign market; the
cross-country average $R^{2}$ recovers from $8\%$ under the global factor to
$10\%$ under the FX-adjusted factor, and several markets that were insignificant
under $\GCF_{t}$ move closer to, or past, significance under $\FXGCF_{t}$
(Sweden, $t=2.2$; Canada, $t=3.0$; the euro markets to $t\approx 1.8$--$1.9$).
The improvement is uniform in direction, consistent with the factor doing what it
is meant to do, but it is small in magnitude. \Cref{fig:mr-gcf-fxgcf} explains
why: in this G10 sample the FX-adjusted factor correlates $0.99$ with the
unadjusted global factor, so the two are near-perfect substitutes and the FX
adjustment has little room to add independent information. This contrasts with
the correlation of about $0.50$ that \citet{dahlquist2013} report for their
FX-adjusted global CP factor, and the discrepancy is a substantive finding in its
own right. It indicates that, over the 1994--2026 G10 sample and with the
top-down construction adopted here, the GDP-weighted average dollar return loads
on essentially the same cyclical combination as the local-currency global
factor. \Cref{ch:robustness} stress-tests this finding across the financial- and
euro-area-crisis subsamples and out of sample; the economic interpretation is
taken up in \Cref{ch:discussion}.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/mr_f4_gcf_fxgcf.pdf}
  \caption{The global cycle factor $\GCF_{t}$ \eqref{eq:gcf} and the FX-adjusted
    global cycle factor $\FXGCF_{t}$ \eqref{eq:fxgcf} over the common sample. The
    two series are near-collinear (correlation $0.99$).}
  \label{fig:mr-gcf-fxgcf}
\end{figure}

The answer to the third research question is therefore twofold. Currency risk
does break the global-factor predictability for an unhedged dollar investor in
most markets, reducing the average $R^{2}$ by roughly two-thirds. The FX-adjusted
global cycle factor restores part of this predictability---reliably in direction
and across markets---but, because it is nearly collinear with the unadjusted
factor in this sample, the restoration is partial rather than complete.

\section{Out-of-Sample Predictability}
\label{sec:res-oos}

The evidence so far is in-sample. Because the cycle factors are generated
regressors---fitted on the same sample over which they are then evaluated---an
in-sample $R^{2}$ can overstate what a real-time investor could have exploited.
This section re-evaluates the three phases out of sample, in summary form, using
the fully-recursive protocol of \Cref{sec:meth-oos}: the entire chain (the
yield-cycle decomposition, the local predictive regression, and the global
aggregation) is rebuilt at each date from data available only up to that date,
and every recursive factor forecast is scored by the
\citet{campbellthompson2008} out-of-sample $R^{2}$ \eqref{eq:meth-ctr2}. This
statistic compares the squared forecast errors of the recursive factor with
those of a competing real-time forecast---the \emph{recursive prevailing mean},
i.e.\ the historical average return updated each period using only past data---so
$R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}=1-\mathrm{SSE}_{\text{factor}}/\mathrm{SSE}_{\text{mean}}$.
A positive value means the factor's real-time forecasts are more accurate than
simply extrapolating the historical average; zero means a tie; a negative value
means the factor does worse than the mean. The OOS $R^{2}$ is therefore measured
against a far more demanding benchmark than the in-sample $R^{2}$, which is
variance explained around the full-sample mean using full-sample coefficients.
The two are \emph{not} comparable in level---out of sample, even small positive
values are economically meaningful and negative values are common---so
\Cref{tab:mr-oos} should be read down each column for the sign and the
cross-factor ranking, not across the two columns for a change in magnitude. The
per-country detail and the forward-factor comparison are developed in
\Cref{ch:robustness}.

\begin{table}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{tables/mr_t4_oos.pdf}
  \caption{Out-of-sample summary. In-sample $R^{2}$ is the cross-country mean of
    the single-factor fits (variance explained around the full-sample mean).
    Out-of-sample $R^{2}$ is the pooled \citet{campbellthompson2008} statistic,
    $1-\mathrm{SSE}_{\text{factor}}/\mathrm{SSE}_{\text{mean}}$, of the
    fully-recursive factor forecast \emph{relative to the recursive
    prevailing-mean forecast} \eqref{eq:meth-ctr2}; positive means the factor
    beats the real-time historical average. Pooling sums each country's squared
    errors against its own recursive mean before forming the ratio. The two
    columns use different benchmarks and are not comparable in level.}
  \label{tab:mr-oos}
\end{table}

Three messages emerge, one per phase. \emph{First}, the local cycle factor does
not survive the transition to real time: its pooled $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}$ is
$-0.07$, and it beats the prevailing mean in only two of the eleven markets (the
United States and Canada). The strong in-sample fit of \Cref{sec:res-phase1} is,
for most countries, the in-sample over-fit of a generated regressor.
\emph{Second}, the global factor is the exception, and a striking one. Its pooled
$R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}$ is $+0.05$ and it is positive in ten of the eleven markets
(\Cref{fig:mr-oos}). Aggregating the eleven national cycles into one
GDP-weighted factor averages away the country-specific estimation noise that
sinks the local factor, so the global factor predicts out of sample where the
local factor cannot. This is the strongest evidence in the thesis for
integration: the global cycle factor is not merely the more efficient in-sample
summary, it is the only cycle factor with genuine real-time predictive content.
This real-time predictability is, moreover, economically exploitable:
\Cref{ch:strategy} turns the global factor into a bond-timing strategy for a
currency-hedged investor and shows that it raises the Sharpe ratio of a global
bond portfolio and delivers a positive certainty-equivalent gain out of sample.
\emph{Third}, for the dollar investor the out-of-sample picture sharpens the
in-sample finding of Phase~III. The recursive $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}$ is clearly
negative for the unadjusted global factor ($-0.07$), beating the recursive mean
in only two markets; the FX-adjusted factor erases that loss, turning marginally
positive at the pooled level ($+0.004$) and beating the mean in nine of the
eleven markets. The pooled margin is thin and, as \Cref{ch:robustness} shows,
fragile---it is concentrated in the post-crisis period and does not survive a
rolling estimation window---but the FX adjustment does what it was designed to
do, converting the unadjusted factor's broad out-of-sample failure into a broad,
if economically marginal, success for the currency-converted investor.

\section{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:res-summary}

The three phases together trace a coherent picture of international bond-return
predictability through the lens of the macro-anchored cycle. \emph{First}, the
cycle factor of \citet{cieslak2015} generalises cleanly to the G10: it predicts
one-year excess returns with the theoretically predicted sign structure in all
eleven markets and with an average in-sample $R^{2}$ of $25\%$, well beyond what
the expectations hypothesis can produce. \emph{Second}, this predictability is
overwhelmingly a global phenomenon. A single GDP-weighted global cycle factor is
significant in every market and matches the explanatory power of the local
factors; once it is included, genuinely local predictability survives in only
three of the eleven markets. The evidence thus supports strong, though not
complete, integration of government-bond risk premia---the central message of
\citet{dahlquist2013}, now established for the cycle factor rather than the
forward-rate factor. \emph{Third}, the global investor's problem is currency
risk: dollar conversion erodes roughly two-thirds of the global factor's
predictive power, and the FX-adjusted factor recovers only part of it, because in
this sample it is nearly indistinguishable from the unadjusted global factor.

\emph{Fourth}, these conclusions survive---and in one respect sharpen---out of
sample. The global cycle factor beats the recursive prevailing mean clearly and
robustly (pooled $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}=+0.05$, positive in ten of eleven
markets), while the local cycle factor and the \emph{unadjusted} dollar-investor
factor do not (both negative, positive in only two markets each). The
FX-adjusted factor is the marginal case: it turns the unadjusted dollar factor's
loss into a slim pooled gain ($+0.004$, positive in nine of eleven markets),
though one that \Cref{ch:robustness} shows to be fragile. Real-time
predictability is therefore led, decisively, by the global cycle factor, with the
FX adjustment salvaging a thin and conditional edge for the currency-converted
investor. The contrast between the local factor's strong in-sample fit and its
negative out-of-sample $R^{2}$ is the clearest sign that the predictability worth
taking seriously is the \emph{global} one.

The summary out-of-sample evidence above is developed in full---per country,
across subsamples, and against the forward-rate factor---in
\Cref{ch:robustness}, and the economic interpretation of all four findings is
taken up in \Cref{ch:discussion}.
