\chapter{Robustness}
\label{ch:robustness}

The headline results of \Cref{ch:results} rest on two simplifications that a
sceptical reader will want stress-tested. First, the predictive regressions are
estimated and evaluated in-sample, on the same data used to construct the
generated cycle regressors, which can flatter the apparent fit. Second, they use
the full estimation window, averaging over three decades that include two of the
most violent episodes in the history of government-bond markets---the
2007--2009 global financial crisis and the 2010--2012 euro-area sovereign-debt
crisis. This chapter subjects the central findings to five tests.

\Cref{sec:rob-oos}
develops the out-of-sample evidence summarised in \Cref{sec:res-oos} in full,
per country and per phase. \Cref{sec:rob-subsample} re-estimates every headline
specification over economically defined subsamples, with the two crises isolated
as windows of their own, in-sample and out-of-sample, and gives the euro-area
crisis and Italy a dedicated treatment. \Cref{sec:rob-cf-cp} asks whether the
conclusions are specific to the macro-anchored cycle factor or hold equally for
the canonical forward-rate factor of \citet{cochrane2005} and \citet{dahlquist2013}.
\Cref{sec:rob-core-headline} rebuilds the entire chain on regular all-items CPI in
place of the core measure, mirroring the inflation-series robustness of
\citet{cieslak2015}. \Cref{sec:rob-sensitivity} closes with the sensitivity of the results to the
construction choices and states plainly which findings are fragile and which
survive. Throughout, the organising result is the same one that emerged in
\Cref{ch:results}: the GDP-weighted global cycle factor $\GCF_{t}$ is far more
robust than any of its competitors.

\section{Out-of-Sample Predictability}
\label{sec:rob-oos}

Because the cycle factors are generated regressors, fitted on the sample over
which they are then scored, in-sample $R^{2}$ overstates what a real-time
investor could have captured. The out-of-sample protocol of \Cref{sec:meth-oos}
removes this advantage in full. The entire factor chain---the yield--trend
decomposition \eqref{eq:cf}, the local predictive regression, and the
GDP-weighted aggregation---is rebuilt at every date from data available only up
to that date, and each recursive forecast is scored by the
\citet{campbellthompson2008} out-of-sample statistic \eqref{eq:meth-ctr2},
$R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}=1-\mathrm{SSE}_{\text{factor}}/\mathrm{SSE}_{\text{mean}}$,
against the recursive prevailing mean. Both the factor and the predictive
regression respect time $t$, so the exercise is doubly out-of-sample. A positive
value means the factor's real-time forecasts beat the historical average; the
benchmark is far more demanding than the full-sample mean against which the
in-sample $R^{2}$ is measured, so the two are not comparable in level and even
small positive out-of-sample values are economically meaningful.

  \caption{Pooled out-of-sample $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}$ \eqref{eq:meth-ctr2} of the
    four headline specifications, fully-recursive factor forecast against the
    recursive prevailing mean. Squared errors are aggregated across the G10, each
    country scored against its own recursive mean. Only the global cycle factor
    clears zero.}
  \label{fig:s8-oos}
\end{figure}

The pooled picture is clear (\Cref{fig:s8-oos}). The local-currency global factor
beats the prevailing mean decisively, with $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}=+0.05$ for
$\rxbar_{i,t+12}\sim\GCF_{t}$ \eqref{eq:h-global}, against $-0.08$ for the local
cycle factor \eqref{eq:h-local} and $-0.07$ for the unadjusted dollar-investor
global factor \eqref{eq:h-usd-global}. The FX-adjusted factor \eqref{eq:h-fxgcf}
is the marginal fourth case, edging just above the mean at $+0.002$---a thin
pooled gain whose robustness is examined in \Cref{sec:rob-oos-scheme} and
\Cref{sec:rob-subsample}. The per-country detail (\Cref{fig:s8-oos-local}) sharpens the
contrast: the global factor is positive in ten of the eleven markets, whereas the
recursive local factor beats the mean in only two (the United States and
Canada). Aggregating the eleven national cycles into one GDP-weighted factor
averages away the country-specific estimation noise that sinks the local factor
out of sample, so the global factor predicts in real time where the local factor
cannot. This is the single most important robustness result in the thesis: the
strong in-sample fit of the local factor (\Cref{sec:res-phase1}) is, for nine of
eleven markets, the over-fit of a generated regressor, while the
\emph{global} factor's predictability is genuine and survives the transition to
real time.

The mechanism behind this divergence is visible in the recursive factors
themselves. \Cref{fig:s8-gcf-conv} overlays the global factor with its
fully-recursive counterpart $\GCF^{\mathrm{oos}}_{t}$: after the roughly ten-year
burn-in required to estimate trend inflation, the recursive series converges to
the full-sample factor and tracks it closely thereafter, the signature of a
stable real-time estimator. The recursive \emph{local} factors converge far less
cleanly---their period-by-period correlation with the full-sample local factor is
materially below one for several markets (right panel of
\Cref{fig:s8-gcf-conv})---which is exactly
why their out-of-sample forecasts deteriorate while the aggregated global factor
remains reliable.

For the dollar investor the out-of-sample evidence is more favourable to the FX
adjustment than the in-sample collapse of Phase~III would suggest
(\Cref{fig:s8-oos-usd}). The unadjusted global factor on currency-converted
returns has $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}=-0.07$ and beats the recursive mean in only two
markets. The FX-adjusted factor reverses this: its pooled
$R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}$ turns marginally positive at $+0.002$ and it beats the mean
in nine of the eleven markets---a broad, if economically slim, improvement that is
exactly what the purpose-built factor was designed to deliver. The gain is real
but fragile, as the next two subsections show: it is concentrated in the
post-crisis period, the early real-time forecasts are poor, and it does not
survive a rolling estimation window. Currency risk that erases the unadjusted
factor's predictability is therefore partly, but only partly and conditionally,
undone by the FX adjustment.

\subsection{Expanding versus Rolling Estimation Windows}
\label{sec:rob-oos-scheme}

The out-of-sample protocol embeds two estimation choices: it uses
\emph{expanding} windows---the recursive regression at each date $t$ is fitted on
all history up to $t$---and a five-year (sixty-month) minimum training span before
the first real-time forecast is produced. Both are conventional, but neither is
innocuous, and a reader is entitled to ask whether the global factor's edge is an
artefact of either. To answer this the entire recursive chain---the yield--trend
decomposition, the predictive regression, and the prevailing-mean
benchmark---is rebuilt from scratch under five estimation schemes: expanding
windows with a three-, five-, and seven-year minimum, and rolling windows of ten
and fifteen years (each scheme scoring its factor against the prevailing mean
constructed under the \emph{same} scheme, so the comparison is internally
consistent). \Cref{tab:rob-oos-scheme} and \Cref{fig:rob-oos-scheme} report the
pooled out-of-sample $R^{2}$; the expanding/five-year column reproduces the
baseline of \Cref{tab:rob-sub-oos} exactly, a check on the rebuilt machinery.

The length of the training minimum matters little for the global cycle factor and
more for the FX-adjusted one. The global factor delivers essentially the same
out-of-sample $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}$ under a three-, five-, or seven-year
minimum---$+0.05$, $+0.05$, and $+0.05$ respectively, positive in ten of eleven
markets in every case---so for the headline result the sixty-month choice is
representative rather than a tuned parameter. The weaker specifications, by
contrast, improve as the minimum lengthens, because more training history
stabilises each recursive coefficient before it is used: a seven-year minimum
lifts the local cycle factor to approximately zero ($-0.003$), the unadjusted
dollar-investor factor from $-0.07$ to $-0.01$, and the FX-adjusted factor from
$+0.002$ to $+0.05$ (positive in nine of eleven markets). The FX-adjusted factor
is the most sensitive of the four to this choice---it slips to $-0.02$ under a
three-year minimum---which is unsurprising, since it is fitted on a single
GDP-weighted aggregate series whose three-parameter regression has the least data
to work with and therefore gains the most from a longer training window. This is a
reason to prefer the conservative five-year (or longer) minimum, not evidence
against the factor.

The window \emph{shape}, by contrast, matters a great deal, and here the chapter
must be candid: \emph{every} specification's out-of-sample edge depends on the
expanding window. The global cycle factor's $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}$ falls from
$+0.05$ to $-0.12$ under a ten-year rolling window and $-0.04$ under a
fifteen-year one, positive in zero of eleven markets in both cases; the
FX-adjusted factor likewise turns negative ($-0.09$ and $-0.01$). The result is
economically coherent rather than troubling. The cycle factors are anchored to
slowly-moving trend inflation and their predictive loadings are stable through
time (\Cref{sec:rob-subsample}), so accumulating the full history sharpens those
loadings; a rolling window throws away informative old data and re-estimates on a
shorter, noisier span, while the rolling prevailing mean it is judged against
becomes correspondingly more adaptive and harder to beat. The factors' real-time
advantage is therefore a property of \emph{expanding}-window estimation---it
rewards the patient accumulation of a long macro history---and is not a generic
feature of any real-time scheme. The ranking across the four specifications is
preserved under every scheme: the global cycle factor is the most robust, positive
under all three expanding minimums; the FX-adjusted factor is positive under the
five- and seven-year expanding minimums but not under the rolling windows; and the
local and unadjusted dollar-investor factors are negative throughout.

\section{Subsample Stability}
\label{sec:rob-subsample}

The full-sample estimates could in principle be driven by a single regime. To
rule this out, every headline specification is re-estimated over four
non-overlapping subsamples, dated by the forecast-origin month: a pre-crisis
window through June 2007, the global financial crisis (July 2007--December
2009), the euro-area sovereign-debt crisis (2010--2012), and a post-crisis window
from 2013 onward. The two crisis windows are short---thirty and thirty-six
forecast origins respectively---so the HAC $t$-statistics they produce are by
construction noisier than the full-sample figures, and the in-sample $R^{2}$ in
short, volatile windows is mechanically higher; the point of the exercise is the
\emph{pattern} across windows, not the precise level in any one.

\begin{table}[t]
  \caption{In-sample predictability across subsamples}
  \label{tab:rob-sub-is}
  \centering
  \input{tables/rob_t1_sub_is}
\end{table}

\Cref{tab:rob-sub-is} reports the in-sample results. The local-currency cycle
predictability is present in \emph{every} subsample and never collapses. It is
strongest, strikingly, in the euro-area crisis, where the local factor reaches a
mean $R^{2}$ of $0.50$ (pooled $t=6.7$, significant in nine of eleven markets)
and the global factor $0.39$ ($t=2.4$): bond risk premia are largest and most
cyclical precisely when sovereign stress is at its peak, which is when the cycle
factor has the most to explain. The weakest window is the financial crisis
itself---mean $R^{2}$ of $0.17$ for the local and $0.05$ for the global factor,
with pooled $t$-statistics near $1.7$---when the flight-to-quality collapse in
yields swamps the slow-moving cyclical signal. The post-crisis window is the most
revealing for the integration thesis: there the \emph{global} factor strengthens
to a mean $R^{2}$ of $0.23$ ($t=12.0$, significant in ten of eleven markets)
while the local factor weakens to $0.13$, consistent with the common factor
becoming the dominant summary of bond risk premia as markets integrate further.

The dollar-investor story is more regime-dependent, and the subsample split
exposes a fragility that the full sample conceals. In the pre-crisis window the
dollar-investor predictability was in fact strong---the FX-adjusted factor
delivered a mean $R^{2}$ of $0.18$, significant in ten of eleven markets, and the
unadjusted global factor $0.12$ in eleven of eleven---but it erodes through both
crises and turns significantly \emph{negative} after 2013 (pooled $t=-3.1$ for
FXGCF, $-2.5$ for GCF). The modestly positive full-sample dollar-investor
$R^{2}$ documented in \Cref{sec:res-phase3} is therefore largely a pre-2008
phenomenon; once the post-crisis currency regime of near-zero rate differentials
and synchronised monetary policy sets in, the cyclical signal in dollar returns
disappears. This is a genuine limitation of the FX-adjusted factor, and the
subsample evidence states it more sharply than the pooled regression can.

The out-of-sample subsample evidence (\Cref{tab:rob-sub-oos},
\Cref{fig:rob-oos-sub}) is the more demanding test, and the global cycle factor
passes it almost everywhere. Its recursive $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}$ is positive in
the pre-crisis window ($+0.09$), the euro-area crisis ($+0.01$), and the
post-crisis window ($+0.09$), and only turns negative \emph{in} the financial
crisis ($-0.19$)---the one regime in which no slow-moving macro factor can be
expected to forecast the panic-driven repricing of 2008.

The local cycle factor,
by contrast, fails out of sample in every window, most severely in the financial
crisis ($-0.40$). The two dollar-investor factors split the sample between them.
The unadjusted global factor is positive \emph{only} in the pre-crisis window
($+0.13$) and negative through both crises and after. The FX-adjusted factor is
its near-mirror image: it beats the recursive mean in the euro-area crisis
($+0.09$) and across the long post-crisis window ($+0.04$)---the bulk of the
sample, which is what makes its full-sample pooled $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}$
marginally positive---but it fails badly in the pre-crisis window ($-1.11$),
where its forecasts rest on the shortest training histories. The FX adjustment
thus earns its slim full-sample edge in the recent, post-2009 dollar regime,
exactly where the unadjusted factor cannot. For the headline factor the message
is unchanged: the global cycle factor's real-time edge is not the artefact of a
single benign regime---it is present before, between, and after the two crises,
and breaks down only in the eye of the 2008 storm.

\subsection{The Euro-Area Crisis and Italy}
\label{sec:rob-italy}

The euro-area crisis deserves a closer look, both because it is the window in
which local-currency predictability is strongest and because \Cref{sec:res-phase2}
singled out Italy as one of the few markets in which the local cycle factor
retains predictive content once the global factor is included (horse-race
$t=3.7$ on the orthogonalised local factor). \Cref{tab:rob-italy} traces the
Italian factors across the four subsamples and shows that this residual local
content is overwhelmingly a crisis phenomenon.

\begin{table}[t]
  \caption{Italy across subsamples}
  \label{tab:rob-italy}
  \centering
  \input{tables/rob_t3_italy}
\end{table}

The Italian local cycle factor's in-sample $R^{2}$ rises from $0.29$ over the
full sample to $0.75$ in the 2010--2012 crisis, and the horse-race $t$-statistic
on the orthogonalised local factor reaches $10.3$---more than double its
full-sample value---while the global factor enters insignificantly and with the
wrong sign ($t(\GCF)=-0.4$). The same pattern holds, even more starkly, during
the financial crisis ($t(\CF^{\perp})=6.6$, $t(\GCF)=-3.1$). The interpretation
is the one anticipated in \Cref{sec:res-phase2}: Italian bond risk premia during
the sovereign-debt crisis were driven by redenomination and sovereign-spread
dynamics that are local in origin and that the GDP-weighted global factor,
dominated by the core economies, cannot price. Most telling is the
out-of-sample column. The Italian local factor beats the recursive prevailing
mean out of sample in the euro-area crisis with $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}=+0.21$---by
far the largest positive real-time local-factor result anywhere in the study,
against a negative full-sample value of $-0.05$. For Italy in 2010--2012, and
essentially nowhere else, the \emph{local} cycle carried genuinely exploitable,
real-time information that the global factor missed. This is the microstructure
behind the ``strong but incomplete integration'' conclusion of \Cref{ch:results}:
integration is the rule, but idiosyncratic sovereign risk reasserts local
predictability exactly when it matters most.

\section{Cycle Factor versus the Cochrane--Piazzesi Factor}
\label{sec:rob-cf-cp}

A natural concern is that the results reflect a generic ``global factor'' effect
rather than anything specific to the macro-anchored cycle. The sharpest test is
to race the cycle factor against the canonical return-forecasting factor of the
literature---the forward-rate factor of \citet{cochrane2005}, aggregated to a
global forward factor in the manner of \citet{dahlquist2013}. We construct the
local forward factor $\CP_{i,t}$ as the fitted value of the one-year-ahead excess
return on the available forward menu, and its GDP-weighted global counterpart
$\GCP_{t}$ exactly as $\GCF_{t}$ is built from $\CF_{i,t}$, so that the two factor
families differ only in their predictor set: macro-anchored cycles versus
forward rates.

The two local factors carry overlapping but distinct information, correlating
$0.69$ on average across the G10 (ranging from $0.43$ in Japan to $0.92$ in the
United Kingdom; right panel of \Cref{fig:s10-is}), and the two global factors correlate
$0.65$. In-sample, the forward factor actually \emph{wins}: its cross-country mean
$R^{2}$ is $0.38$ for the local and $0.35$ for the global version, against $0.25$
for both cycle factors (\Cref{fig:s10-is}). This is unsurprising and not in the
cycle factor's favour at face value---the forward factor uses six predictors to
the cycle factor's two, so it has far more scope to over-fit a fixed sample.

  \begin{minipage}[t]{0.49\linewidth}\centering

  \caption{Left: in-sample $R^{2}$ by country for the cycle factors (CF, GCF)

  \label{fig:s10-is}
\end{figure}

The verdict reverses out of sample, and decisively
(\Cref{fig:s10-oos}). The global \emph{cycle} factor is the only one of the four
that beats the recursive prevailing mean, with pooled
$R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}=+0.05$ and positive forecasts in ten of eleven markets. The
global \emph{forward} factor, despite its higher in-sample fit, fails out of
sample ($-0.01$, positive in only two markets), as do the local forward factor
($-0.02$, four markets) and the local cycle factor ($-0.08$, two markets). The
forward factor's in-sample advantage is precisely the over-fit that the
out-of-sample test is designed to expose, and it does not survive aggregation
into real time. The macro anchoring of the cycle factor---tying the predictable
component of returns to a slowly-moving trend-inflation object rather than to the
fast-moving forward curve---is therefore not incidental: it is what gives the
global factor its real-time stability. The integration finding of
\Cref{ch:results} is, if anything, \emph{stronger} for the cycle factor than for
the forward factor on which \citet{dahlquist2013} originally established it.

\section{Core versus Headline Inflation}
\label{sec:rob-core-headline}

The cycle factor is the residual of yields on \emph{trend} inflation, and trend
inflation is built throughout from \emph{core} CPI (excluding food and energy),
following \citet{cieslak2015}, who argue that core is the better proxy for the
persistent component of inflation that bond investors price. That choice is not
innocuous: \citet{cieslak2015} themselves check the alternative in their Table~10,
Panel~C4, rebuilding the trend from all-items CPI and reporting that it delivers a
predictive $R^{2}$ about nine percent lower on average, the divergence dating to
the energy-price volatility of the 2000s. This section repeats that test for the
G10. The entire chain---trend inflation, the cycle, and the three factors---is
rebuilt on \emph{regular} (all-items, headline) CPI and compared with the
core-CPI baseline on the per-country common sample.\footnote{The headline series
carries 135 month-country cells with multiple release vintages; we keep the
most-revised value. The regular series is also shorter in places (Japan ends in
2021), which the per-country common-sample alignment respects.}

In sample, the two inflation measures lead to very similar factors. The smoothing
in the discounted moving average removes most of the food- and energy-price noise,
so core and headline \emph{trend} inflation correlate $0.90$ on average across the
G10, and the cycle factors they generate correlate $0.95$ (local) and $0.92$
(global). Predictability is nonetheless modestly weaker on headline CPI, exactly
as \citet{cieslak2015} find: the cross-country mean in-sample $R^{2}$ of the local
factor falls from $0.247$ (core) to $0.228$ (headline), and the global factor from
$0.247$ to $0.214$---a relative drop of about eight to thirteen percent, in the
neighbourhood of their nine---while both remain overwhelmingly significant (the
global factor's pooled HAC $t$ is $14.3$ on core and $14.5$ on headline).
\Cref{tab:rob-core-headline} and \Cref{fig:rob-core-headline} show the gap is
concentrated where the data's energy pass-through is largest: the United States,
whose local $R^{2}$ falls from $0.33$ to $0.23$, accounts for much of the average,
whereas a handful of markets (Belgium, France, Japan, Sweden) fit marginally \emph{better}
on headline. The FX-adjusted factor tells the same story ($R^{2}$ of $0.095$ on
core versus $0.068$ on headline).

The out-of-sample comparison is where the choice of inflation measure becomes
decisive, and it vindicates the baseline. In sample the measure barely matters;
out of sample it is the difference between a result and none. The global cycle
factor's real-time edge---the central finding of \Cref{sec:rob-oos}---survives on
core CPI ($R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}=+0.05$) but \emph{vanishes} on headline CPI
($-0.02$); the FX-adjusted factor collapses similarly (from $+0.002$ to $-0.16$),
and the local factor remains negative under both
(\Cref{tab:rob-core-headline-oos}). The asymmetry is economically intelligible.
The recursive estimator re-fits both the trend and the predictive coefficients on
data available only up to each date, so it is far more exposed to the
energy-driven noise in headline inflation than the full-sample regression, which
averages that noise away; \citet{cieslak2015} note that core and all-items
inflation diverged in the 2000s precisely because energy-price swings, which do
not pass through to the yield curve, inflate the headline trend. The use of core
CPI is therefore not merely a convention inherited from \citet{cieslak2015}: it is
material to the headline real-time result, and the global cycle factor's
out-of-sample predictability is a property of the macro anchor measured cleanly.

\section{Sensitivity to Construction Choices}
\label{sec:rob-sensitivity}

Three construction choices underlie the cycle factor, and it is worth stating how
much the results depend on each. The first is the exponentially-weighted moving
average that defines trend inflation, with decay parameter $v=0.987$ and window
$M=120$ months. These values follow \citet{cieslak2015} directly, and the cycle
factor inherits its robustness to them from the nature of the object: trend
inflation is a deliberately slow-moving anchor, so moderate changes in the decay
weight or the window length shift the estimated trend by small amounts and leave
the cycle---the residual of yields on that trend---and hence the factor largely
unchanged.

The second is the maturity menu used to form the cycle and the excess
return: the one-, two-, four-, five-, nine-, and ten-year points available in the
data, summarised by the duration-standardised, maturity-averaged return
\eqref{eq:rxbar}. \Cref{sec:res-phase1} showed that the predictability holds at
each individual maturity, not only for the average, with loadings that are
flat-to-rising across the curve; the maturity-averaged factor is thus a summary
of a curve-wide phenomenon, not an artefact of any single segment.

The third is
the GDP weighting of the global factor, computed afresh each month over the
countries in the panel so that the weights sum to one; because the global factor
is dominated by the large core economies whose cycles co-move strongly, the
aggregate is not sensitive to the precise weighting of the smaller markets.

It is equally important to be candid about what is fragile. The local cycle
factor's in-sample predictability does not survive real-time evaluation
(\Cref{sec:rob-oos}); the dollar-investor predictability is regime-dependent and
concentrated before 2008 (\Cref{sec:rob-subsample}); and the FX-adjusted factor,
nearly collinear with the unadjusted global factor in this sample, adds little
beyond it except in the euro-area crisis. What survives every test in this
chapter is the same single object: the GDP-weighted global cycle factor predicts
international bond returns in-sample and out-of-sample, across every subsample
except the financial crisis itself and---out of sample---when the macro trend is
measured cleanly with core inflation (\Cref{sec:rob-core-headline}), more robustly
than its local counterpart, its dollar-investor variant, or the forward-rate
factor against which it is most naturally compared. That object, and the economic value it carries
(\Cref{ch:strategy}), is the finding the thesis stands on; its interpretation is
taken up in \Cref{ch:discussion}.
