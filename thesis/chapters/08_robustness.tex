\chapter{Robustness}
\label{ch:robustness}

The headline results of \Cref{ch:results} rest on two simplifications that a
sceptical reader will want to see tested. First, we estimate and evaluate the
predictive regressions in-sample, on the same data that we use to construct the
generated cycle regressors, which can overstate the apparent fit. Second, we use
the full estimation window and therefore average over three decades that include
two of the most severe episodes in the history of government-bond markets, the
2007--2009 global financial crisis and the 2010--2012 euro-area sovereign-debt
crisis. In this chapter, we subject our central findings to five tests. In \Cref{sec:rob-oos},
we develop the out-of-sample evidence summarised in \Cref{sec:res-oos} in full,
per country and per phase. In \Cref{sec:rob-subsample}, we re-estimate every headline
specification over economically defined subsamples, with the two crises isolated
as windows of their own, in-sample and out-of-sample, and we give the euro-area
crisis and Italy a dedicated treatment. In \Cref{sec:rob-cf-cp}, we ask whether our
conclusions are specific to the macro-anchored cycle factor or hold equally for
the canonical forward-rate factor of \citet{cochrane2005} and \citet{dahlquist2013}.
In \Cref{sec:rob-core-headline}, we rebuild the entire chain on regular all-items CPI in
place of the core measure, mirroring the inflation-series robustness check of
\citet{cieslak2015}. In \Cref{sec:rob-sensitivity}, we close with the sensitivity of the results to our
construction choices and state plainly which findings are fragile and which
survive. Throughout the chapter, we arrive at the same organising result as in
\Cref{ch:results}: the GDP-weighted global cycle factor $\GCF_{t}$ is far more
robust than any of its competitors.

\section{Out-of-Sample Predictability}
\label{sec:rob-oos}

Because the cycle factors are generated regressors, fitted on the sample over
which they are then scored, the in-sample $R^{2}$ overstates what a real-time
investor could have captured. The out-of-sample protocol of \Cref{sec:meth-oos}
removes this advantage in full. We rebuild the entire factor chain, that is the
yield--trend decomposition \eqref{eq:cf}, the local predictive regression, and the
GDP-weighted aggregation, at every date from data available only up
to that date, and we score each recursive forecast by the
\citet{campbellthompson2008} out-of-sample statistic \eqref{eq:meth-ctr2},
$R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}=1-\mathrm{SSE}_{\text{factor}}/\mathrm{SSE}_{\text{mean}}$,
against the recursive prevailing mean. Both the factor and the predictive
regression respect time $t$, so the exercise is doubly out-of-sample. A positive
value means that the factor's real-time forecasts beat the historical average.
Because this benchmark is far more demanding than the full-sample mean against
which the in-sample $R^{2}$ is measured, the two are not comparable in level, and
even small positive out-of-sample values are economically meaningful.

In \Cref{fig:s8-oos} we can see the pooled picture, which is clear. The
local-currency global factor clearly beats the prevailing mean, with
$R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}=+0.05$ for
$\rxbar_{i,t+12}\sim\GCF_{t}$ \eqref{eq:h-global}, against $-0.08$ for the local
cycle factor \eqref{eq:h-local} and $-0.07$ for the unadjusted dollar-investor
global factor \eqref{eq:h-usd-global}. The FX-adjusted factor \eqref{eq:h-fxgcf}
is the intermediate case and beats the mean at $+0.019$. This pooled gain is
much smaller than the factor's in-sample fit, and we examine its robustness in
\Cref{sec:rob-oos-scheme} and \Cref{sec:rob-subsample}. The per-country detail (\Cref{fig:s8-oos-local}) sharpens this
contrast. The global factor is positive in ten of the eleven markets, whereas the
recursive local factor beats the mean in only two (the United States and
Canada). Aggregating the eleven national cycles into one GDP-weighted factor
averages away the country-specific estimation noise that hurts the local factor
out of sample, so the global factor predicts in real time where the local factor
cannot. We see this as the single most important robustness result of our thesis.
The strong in-sample fit of the local factor (\Cref{sec:res-phase1}) appears, for
nine of eleven markets, to be the over-fit of a generated regressor, while the
predictability of the \emph{global} factor is genuine and survives the transition
to real time.

The recursive factors themselves show us the mechanism behind this divergence.
In \Cref{fig:s8-gcf-conv}, we overlay the global factor with its
fully-recursive counterpart $\GCF^{\mathrm{oos}}_{t}$. After the roughly ten-year
burn-in that is required to estimate trend inflation, the recursive series
converges to the full-sample factor and tracks it closely thereafter, which is
the signature of a stable real-time estimator. The recursive \emph{local} factors
converge far less cleanly. Their period-by-period correlation with the
full-sample local factor is materially below one for several markets (right
panel of \Cref{fig:s8-gcf-conv}), which is exactly why their out-of-sample
forecasts deteriorate while the aggregated global factor remains reliable.

For the dollar investor, the out-of-sample evidence qualifies the strong
in-sample recovery of Phase~III (\Cref{fig:s8-oos-usd}). The unadjusted global
factor on currency-converted returns has $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}=-0.07$ and
beats the recursive mean in only two markets. The FX-adjusted factor reverses
this loss. Its pooled $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}$ turns positive at $+0.019$, and
it beats the mean in six of the eleven markets. This is what the purpose-built
factor was designed to deliver, but the margin is an order of magnitude
smaller than the in-sample gain of Phase~III. Moreover, as the next two
subsections show, the gain is sensitive to the estimation scheme, whereas the
global factor's edge is not. The currency risk that erases the unadjusted
factor's predictability is therefore only partly, and only conditionally,
undone by the FX adjustment in real time.

\subsection{Expanding versus Rolling Estimation Windows}
\label{sec:rob-oos-scheme}

Our out-of-sample protocol embeds two estimation choices. It uses
\emph{expanding} windows, meaning that the recursive regression at each date $t$
is fitted on all history up to $t$, and it requires a five-year (sixty-month)
minimum training span before the first real-time forecast is produced. Both
choices are conventional, but neither is innocuous, and a reader is entitled to
ask whether the global factor's advantage is an artefact of either. To answer
this question, we rebuild the entire recursive chain, that is the yield--trend
decomposition, the predictive regression, and the prevailing-mean benchmark,
from scratch under five estimation schemes: expanding windows with a three-,
five-, and seven-year minimum, and rolling windows of ten and fifteen years.
Each scheme scores its factor against the prevailing mean constructed under the
\emph{same} scheme, so the comparison is internally consistent.
\Cref{tab:rob-oos-scheme} and \Cref{fig:rob-oos-scheme} report the
pooled out-of-sample $R^{2}$. The expanding/five-year column reproduces the
baseline of \Cref{tab:rob-sub-oos} exactly, which serves as a check on the
rebuilt machinery.

The length of the training minimum matters little for the global cycle factor
and much more for the FX-adjusted one. The global factor delivers essentially
the same out-of-sample $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}$ under a three-, five-, or
seven-year minimum, namely $+0.05$, $+0.05$, and $+0.05$ respectively, and it
is positive in ten of eleven markets in every case. For the headline result,
the sixty-month choice is therefore representative rather than a tuned
parameter. A seven-year minimum also lifts the local cycle factor to
approximately zero ($-0.003$) and the unadjusted dollar-investor factor from
$-0.07$ to $-0.01$, because more training history stabilises each recursive
coefficient before it is used. The FX-adjusted factor, however, is the most
sensitive of the four to this choice: its pooled gain of $+0.019$ holds under
the baseline five-year minimum but turns negative under both a three-year
($-0.13$) and a seven-year ($-0.11$) minimum. Its eleven per-country USD
regressions have less stable recursive coefficients than the single global
aggregate, and shifting the training minimum shifts which early forecasts
enter the evaluation window. We read this as a caution: the FX-adjusted
factor's real-time edge exists at the baseline but is not a robust feature of
every reasonable scheme.

The window shape, by contrast, matters a great deal, and we have to be clear
about this point: the out-of-sample advantage of every specification depends on
the expanding window. The global cycle factor's $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}$ falls from
$+0.05$ to $-0.12$ under a ten-year rolling window and to $-0.04$ under a
fifteen-year one, and it is positive in zero of eleven markets in both cases. The
FX-adjusted factor is negative under the ten-year rolling window ($-0.05$) and
approximately flat under the fifteen-year one ($+0.01$). However, we see
this result as economically coherent rather than troubling. The cycle factors are
anchored to slowly-moving trend inflation and their predictive loadings are
stable through time (\Cref{sec:rob-subsample}), so accumulating the full history
sharpens those loadings. A rolling window, in contrast, throws away informative
old data and re-estimates on a shorter, noisier span, while the rolling
prevailing mean it is judged against becomes correspondingly more adaptive and
harder to beat. The factors' real-time advantage is therefore a property of
\emph{expanding}-window estimation, which rewards the patient accumulation of a
long macro history, and it is not a generic feature of any real-time scheme.
Across all five schemes, one ranking is stable: the global cycle factor is
always the strongest specification and is positive under all three expanding
minimums, while the local and unadjusted dollar-investor factors are negative
throughout. The FX-adjusted factor is the least stable of the four. It is
positive only under the baseline five-year expanding minimum and,
marginally, the fifteen-year rolling window, so its real-time edge should be
read as conditional on the estimation scheme.

\section{Subsample Stability}
\label{sec:rob-subsample}

The full-sample estimates could in principle be driven by a single regime. To
rule this out, we re-estimate every headline specification over four
non-overlapping subsamples, dated by the forecast-origin month: a pre-crisis
window through June 2007, the global financial crisis (July 2007--December
2009), the euro-area sovereign-debt crisis (2010--2012), and a post-crisis window
from 2013 onward. The two crisis windows are short, with thirty and thirty-six
forecast origins respectively, so the HAC $t$-statistics they produce are by
construction noisier than the full-sample figures, and the in-sample $R^{2}$ in
short, volatile windows is mechanically higher. The point of the exercise is
therefore the pattern across windows, not the precise level in any one of them.

In \Cref{tab:rob-sub-is}, we report the in-sample results. The local-currency
cycle predictability is present in every subsample and never collapses.
Strikingly, it is strongest in the euro-area crisis, where the local factor
reaches a mean $R^{2}$ of $0.50$ (pooled $t=6.7$, significant in nine of eleven
markets) and the global factor $0.39$ ($t=2.4$). Bond risk premia are, thus,
largest and most cyclical precisely when sovereign stress is at its peak, which
is when the cycle factor has the most to explain. The weakest window is the
financial crisis itself, with a mean $R^{2}$ of $0.17$ for the local and $0.05$
for the global factor and pooled $t$-statistics near $1.7$. In this window, the
flight-to-quality collapse in yields swamps the slow-moving cyclical signal. The
post-crisis window is the most revealing for our integration thesis. There, the
\emph{global} factor strengthens to a mean $R^{2}$ of $0.23$ ($t=12.0$,
significant in ten of eleven markets) while the local factor weakens to $0.13$,
which is consistent with the common factor becoming the dominant summary of bond
risk premia as markets integrate further.

The dollar-investor story is more regime-dependent, and the subsample split
exposes a fragility that the full sample conceals. In the pre-crisis window, the
dollar-investor predictability was in fact strong. The FX-adjusted factor
delivered a mean $R^{2}$ of $0.30$, significant in nine of eleven markets, and
the unadjusted global factor $0.12$ in eleven of eleven. However, this
predictability erodes through both crises and disappears after 2013, where the
mean $R^{2}$ falls to $0.04$ for both dollar factors and the pooled
$t$-statistics turn negative ($-1.7$ for FXGCF, $-2.5$ for GCF). The strong
full-sample dollar-investor fit that we document in \Cref{sec:res-phase3} is
therefore largely a pre-2008 phenomenon. Once the post-crisis currency regime
of near-zero rate differentials and synchronised monetary policy sets in, the
cyclical signal in dollar returns disappears. We see this as a genuine
limitation of the FX-adjusted factor, and the subsample evidence states it
more sharply than the pooled regression can.

The out-of-sample subsample evidence (\Cref{tab:rob-sub-oos},
\Cref{fig:rob-oos-sub}) is the more demanding test, and the global cycle factor
passes it almost everywhere. Its recursive $R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}$ is positive in
the pre-crisis window ($+0.09$), the euro-area crisis ($+0.01$), and the
post-crisis window ($+0.09$), and it only turns negative in the financial
crisis ($-0.19$), the one regime in which no slow-moving macro factor can be
expected to forecast the panic-driven repricing of 2008. The local cycle factor,
by contrast, fails out of sample in every window, most severely in the financial
crisis ($-0.40$). The two dollar-investor factors split the sample between them.
The unadjusted global factor is positive only in the pre-crisis window
($+0.13$) and negative through both crises and after. The FX-adjusted factor is
close to its mirror image. It is the only specification that stays positive
through the financial crisis ($+0.08$), when the flight into the dollar moved
currency and bond returns together and the per-country USD regressions had
something genuine to forecast, and it remains approximately flat in the
euro-area crisis and the post-crisis window ($+0.002$ and $+0.003$). It is
mildly negative pre-crisis ($-0.02$), where its forecasts rest on the shortest
training histories. Its small full-sample advantage is therefore earned mainly
in the 2008 repricing, not accrued steadily across regimes. For the headline
factor, the message is unchanged. The global cycle factor's real-time
advantage is not the artefact of a single benign regime. It is present before,
between, and after the two crises, and it breaks down only in the financial
crisis of 2008 itself.

\subsection{The Euro-Area Crisis and Italy}
\label{sec:rob-italy}

The euro-area crisis deserves a closer look, both because it is the window in
which local-currency predictability is strongest and because \Cref{sec:res-phase2}
singled out Italy as one of the few markets in which the local cycle factor
retains predictive content once the global factor is included (horse-race
$t=3.7$ on the orthogonalised local factor). In \Cref{tab:rob-italy}, we trace
the Italian factors across the four subsamples and show that this residual local
content is overwhelmingly a crisis phenomenon.

The Italian local cycle factor's in-sample $R^{2}$ rises from $0.29$ over the
full sample to $0.75$ in the 2010--2012 crisis, and the horse-race $t$-statistic
on the orthogonalised local factor reaches $10.3$, which is more than double its
full-sample value, while the global factor enters insignificantly and with the
wrong sign ($t(\GCF)=-0.4$). We observe the same pattern, even more starkly,
during the financial crisis ($t(\CF^{\perp})=6.6$, $t(\GCF)=-3.1$). Our
interpretation is the one we anticipated in \Cref{sec:res-phase2}: Italian bond
risk premia during the sovereign-debt crisis were driven by redenomination and
sovereign-spread dynamics that are local in origin and that the GDP-weighted
global factor, which is dominated by the core economies, cannot price. The
out-of-sample column is the most telling. The Italian local factor beats the
recursive prevailing mean out of sample in the euro-area crisis with
$R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}=+0.21$, which is by far the largest positive real-time
local-factor result anywhere in our study, against a negative full-sample value
of $-0.05$. For Italy in 2010--2012, and essentially nowhere else, the
\emph{local} cycle therefore carried genuinely exploitable real-time information
that the global factor missed. This is the mechanism behind the ``strong but
incomplete integration'' conclusion of \Cref{ch:results}: integration is the
rule, but idiosyncratic sovereign risk reasserts local predictability exactly
when it matters most.

\section{Cycle Factor versus the Cochrane--Piazzesi Factor}
\label{sec:rob-cf-cp}

A natural concern is that our results reflect a generic ``global factor'' effect
rather than anything specific to the macro-anchored cycle. The sharpest test we
can perform is to compare the cycle factor directly with the canonical
return-forecasting factor of the literature, the forward-rate factor of
\citet{cochrane2005}, aggregated to a global forward factor in the manner of
\citet{dahlquist2013}. We construct the
local forward factor $\CP_{i,t}$ as the fitted value of the one-year-ahead excess
return on the available forward menu, and its GDP-weighted global counterpart
$\GCP_{t}$ exactly as $\GCF_{t}$ is built from $\CF_{i,t}$, so that the two factor
families differ only in their predictor set: macro-anchored cycles versus
forward rates.

The two local factors carry overlapping but distinct information. They correlate
$0.69$ on average across the G10 (ranging from $0.43$ in Japan to $0.92$ in the
United Kingdom; right panel of \Cref{fig:s10-is}), and the two global factors correlate
$0.65$. In sample, the forward factor actually performs better: its cross-country
mean $R^{2}$ is $0.38$ for the local and $0.35$ for the global version, against
$0.25$ for both cycle factors (\Cref{fig:s10-is}). At face value, this is not in
the cycle factor's favour. However, it is also not surprising, because the
forward factor uses six predictors compared to the cycle factor's two and
therefore has far more scope to over-fit a fixed sample.

Out of sample, however, the verdict clearly reverses
(\Cref{fig:s10-oos}). The global \emph{cycle} factor is the only one of the four
that beats the recursive prevailing mean, with a pooled
$R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}=+0.05$ and positive forecasts in ten of eleven markets. The
global \emph{forward} factor, despite its higher in-sample fit, fails out of
sample ($-0.01$, positive in only two markets), as do the local forward factor
($-0.02$, four markets) and the local cycle factor ($-0.08$, two markets). The
forward factor's in-sample advantage is precisely the over-fit that the
out-of-sample test is designed to expose, and it does not survive the transition
to real time. The macro anchoring of the cycle factor, which ties the predictable
component of returns to a slowly-moving trend-inflation object rather than to the
fast-moving forward curve, is therefore not incidental. It is what gives the
global factor its real-time stability. The integration finding of
\Cref{ch:results} is, if anything, stronger for the cycle factor than for
the forward factor on which \citet{dahlquist2013} originally established it.

\section{Core versus Headline Inflation}
\label{sec:rob-core-headline}

The cycle factor is the residual of yields on \emph{trend} inflation, and we
build trend inflation throughout from \emph{core} CPI (excluding food and energy),
following \citet{cieslak2015}, who argue that core is the better proxy for the
persistent component of inflation that bond investors price. This choice is not
innocuous. \citet{cieslak2015} themselves check the alternative in their Table~10,
Panel~C4, where they rebuild the trend from all-items CPI and report that it
delivers a predictive $R^{2}$ about nine percent lower on average, with the
divergence dating back to the energy-price volatility of the 2000s. In this
section, we repeat this test for the G10. We rebuild the entire chain, that is
trend inflation, the cycle, and the three factors, on \emph{regular} (all-items,
headline) CPI and compare it with the core-CPI baseline on the per-country
common sample.\footnote{The headline series
carries 135 month-country cells with multiple release vintages, of which we keep
the most-revised value. The regular series is also shorter in places (Japan ends
in 2021), which the per-country common-sample alignment respects.}

In sample, the two inflation measures lead to very similar factors. The smoothing
in the discounted moving average removes most of the food- and energy-price noise,
so core and headline trend inflation correlate $0.90$ on average across the
G10, and the cycle factors they generate correlate $0.95$ (local) and $0.92$
(global). However, predictability is modestly weaker on headline CPI, exactly
as \citet{cieslak2015} find. The cross-country mean in-sample $R^{2}$ of the local
factor falls from $0.247$ (core) to $0.228$ (headline), and that of the global
factor from $0.247$ to $0.214$, a relative drop of about eight to thirteen percent
and thus in the neighbourhood of their nine, while both remain overwhelmingly
significant (the global factor's pooled HAC $t$ is $14.3$ on core and $14.5$ on
headline). In \Cref{tab:rob-core-headline} and \Cref{fig:rob-core-headline}, we
can see that the gap is concentrated where the energy pass-through in the data is
largest. The United States, whose local $R^{2}$ falls from $0.33$ to $0.23$,
accounts for much of the average, whereas a handful of markets (Belgium, France,
Japan, Sweden) fit marginally better on headline. The FX-adjusted factor tells
the same story ($R^{2}$ of $0.143$ on core versus $0.124$ on headline).

The out-of-sample comparison is where the choice of inflation measure becomes
important, and it supports our baseline choice. While the measure barely matters
in sample, out of sample it makes the difference between a result and none. The
global cycle factor's real-time advantage, which is the central finding of
\Cref{sec:rob-oos}, survives on core CPI ($R^{2}_{\mathrm{oos}}=+0.05$) but
disappears on headline CPI ($-0.02$). The local factor remains negative under
both, while the FX-adjusted factor is the exception: it is slightly
\emph{better} on headline ($+0.036$ versus $+0.019$), plausibly because the
energy-price component that contaminates the headline trend also moves
exchange rates and is therefore not pure noise for a dollar-return factor
(\Cref{tab:rob-core-headline-oos}). For the local-currency factors, the
asymmetry is economically intelligible.
The recursive estimator re-fits both the trend and the predictive coefficients on
data available only up to each date, so it is far more exposed to the
energy-driven noise in headline inflation than the full-sample regression, which
averages this noise away. \citet{cieslak2015} note that core and all-items
inflation diverged in the 2000s precisely because energy-price swings, which do
not pass through to the yield curve, inflate the headline trend. The use of core
CPI is therefore not merely a convention inherited from \citet{cieslak2015}. It
is material to the headline real-time result, and the global cycle factor's
out-of-sample predictability is a property of the macro anchor measured cleanly.

\section{Sensitivity to Construction Choices}
\label{sec:rob-sensitivity}

Three construction choices underlie the cycle factor, and it is worth stating how
much our results depend on each of them. The first is the exponentially-weighted
moving average that defines trend inflation, with decay parameter $v=0.987$ and
window $M=120$ months. These values follow \citet{cieslak2015} directly, and the
cycle factor inherits its robustness to them from the nature of the object.
Trend inflation is a deliberately slow-moving anchor, so moderate changes in the
decay weight or the window length shift the estimated trend by small amounts and
leave the cycle, the residual of yields on this trend, and hence the factor
largely unchanged. The second is the maturity menu used to form the cycle and the
excess return: the one-, two-, four-, five-, nine-, and ten-year points available
in the data, summarised by the duration-standardised, maturity-averaged return
\eqref{eq:rxbar}. In \Cref{sec:res-phase1}, we showed that the predictability
holds at each individual maturity, not only for the average, with loadings that
are flat-to-rising across the curve. The maturity-averaged factor is thus a
summary of a curve-wide phenomenon and not an artefact of any single segment. The
third is the GDP weighting of the global factor, which we compute afresh each
month over the countries in the panel so that the weights sum to one. Because the
global factor is dominated by the large core economies whose cycles co-move
strongly, the aggregate is not sensitive to the precise weighting of the smaller
markets.

\subsection{The Construction of the FX-Adjusted Factor}
\label{sec:rob-fxgcf-construction}

A fourth construction choice concerns the FX-adjusted factor itself, and we
examine it separately because it is the one choice that changes a result. The
$\FXGCF_{t}$ of \Cref{sec:fw-fxgcf} admits a two-by-two design: the factor can
be built bottom-up (a USD cycle factor per country, aggregated) or top-down
(one regression of the aggregated dollar return on the aggregated
predictors), and the aggregation can use GDP or equal ($1/n$) weights. In
\Cref{tab:rob-fxgcf-construction}, we compare all four variants on the
identical pipeline.

Two patterns stand out. First, the correlation with the unadjusted global
factor is determined by the construction axis, not by the weighting: the two
top-down variants correlate $0.94$--$0.99$ with $\GCF_{t}$, whereas the two
bottom-up variants correlate only $0.73$--$0.78$. A top-down factor projects
one aggregate dollar return on the same aggregate cycle information that
underlies $\GCF_{t}$, so it inherits the global cycle almost one for one and
has little room to load on currency dynamics. The bottom-up factor, in
contrast, lets each country's regression reweight its own cycles toward the
currency-relevant combination before aggregating, and this is where the
distinct information comes from. Second, the in-sample recovery for the
dollar investor is a bottom-up phenomenon: the mean $R^{2}$ is $0.14$ for
both bottom-up variants, against $0.10$ for the top-down ones, and the count
of significant markets rises from four to ten as we move from the top-down
GDP variant to our baseline. Out of sample, all four variants deliver only
small pooled gains ($+0.00$ to $+0.03$), and no construction dominates. We
adopt the bottom-up GDP-weighted variant as our baseline because it mirrors
the construction of $\GCF_{t}$ exactly, carries genuinely distinct
information, and is the strongest in-sample specification; the choice of
construction does not, however, change the real-time conclusion that the
FX-adjusted factor's out-of-sample edge is small.

It is equally important to state clearly what is fragile. The local cycle
factor's in-sample predictability does not survive real-time evaluation
(\Cref{sec:rob-oos}). The dollar-investor predictability is regime-dependent and
concentrated before 2008 (\Cref{sec:rob-subsample}). Furthermore, the
FX-adjusted factor's strong in-sample recovery translates into only a small
and scheme-sensitive real-time edge, earned mainly in the 2008 window. What survives every
test in this chapter is the same single object. The GDP-weighted global cycle
factor predicts international bond returns in-sample and out-of-sample, across
every subsample except the financial crisis itself and, out of sample, when the
macro trend is measured cleanly with core inflation (\Cref{sec:rob-core-headline}).
It does so more robustly than its local counterpart, its dollar-investor variant,
and the forward-rate factor against which it is most naturally compared. This
global factor, and the economic value it carries (\Cref{ch:strategy}), is
therefore the central finding of our thesis. We interpret it further in
\Cref{ch:discussion}.
