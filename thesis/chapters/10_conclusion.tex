\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

% Restate the research question and answer each sub-question explicitly:
%   (1) does the CF work locally across G10?
%   (2) does the global GCF subsume the local CF?
%   (3) does the FX-adjusted FXGCF help the USD investor?
% Summarize the contribution (unified global cycle factor + FX adjustment) and
% outline future work (more maturities, longer samples, no-arbitrage embedding).

In our thesis, we asked whether the cycle factor of \citet{cieslak2015}
applies to international government bonds. To answer this question, we
constructed the local cycle factor for eleven G10 markets, aggregated the national factors into a GDP-weighted global
cycle factor, and additionally built a novel FX-adjusted global cycle factor
for an unhedged US-dollar investor. We evaluated this three-factor hierarchy
in three empirical phases, both in-sample and under a fully recursive
out-of-sample pipeline, and we validated our empirical engine by replicating
the published US and international results (\Cref{ch:replication}). In this
chapter, we summarise our answers to the research question and its three
sub-questions, restate our contribution, and outline directions for future
work.

Our answer to the main question is affirmative. In all eleven markets, the
local cycle factor predicts one-year excess returns with the same sign
pattern that \citet{cieslak2015} document for the United States,
significantly so in ten, with an average in-sample $R^{2}$ of $25\%$ and
loadings that are uniform across the maturity curve. We therefore conclude that
the trend--cycle mechanism is not specific to the United States but a
property of the asset class. This is in line with the falling-stars evidence
of \citet{bauer2020}, according to which the predictable component of bond
returns appears once the slow-moving macro anchors are accounted for.

Regarding our first sub-question, on the role of the local relative to the
global cycle factor, we find that the predictability is overwhelmingly
global. The GDP-weighted global cycle factor is significant in every market
and matches the explanatory power of the eleven separately estimated local
factors. Regarding our second sub-question, we find that the global factor
does subsume the local factors in most markets. Once the global factor is
included, genuinely local content survives only in Italy, the Netherlands,
and Belgium, concentrated in periods of sovereign stress. Similar
to the findings of \citet{dahlquist2013} for the forward-rate family, we can
therefore conclude that government-bond risk premia are internationally
integrated, although sovereign influences may dominate predictability. Because
five of the eleven markets share the euro and a common monetary policy, they
furnish fewer than eleven independent tests of the global cycle, so this
integration result should be read with that in mind.

For the unhedged US-dollar investor, our results are mixed. Dollar
conversion erodes most of the global factor's predictive
power. The FX-adjusted global cycle factor, built bottom-up from the
country-level USD regressions, recovers much of it in sample. It
substantially raises the average dollar-investor fit and correlates only $0.81$ with the
unadjusted factor, comparable in spirit to the $0.50$ that
\citet{dahlquist2013} report for the forward-rate family. We also find,
however, that this recovery depends on the construction of the FXGCF and that it
translates into only a small edge that does not help in timing the aggregate
dollar portfolio. The practical implication we
draw from this is that the dollar investor should hedge the currency
exposure, since the exploitable component of international bond returns
is a local-currency phenomenon.

The finding we regard as most important concerns real time. Out of sample,
the local factors lose to the recursive historical mean almost everywhere,
while the global cycle factor beats it in ten of eleven markets. This edge
holds across crisis subsamples, beats the global forward-rate factor, and
depends on measuring the inflation trend with core rather than
headline inflation. It also carries economic value. A timing strategy
based on the global factor raises the Sharpe ratio of a hedged global bond
portfolio from $0.34$ to $0.40$ with a positive certainty-equivalent gain
(\Cref{ch:strategy}).

With our thesis, we thus contribute a unified, macro-anchored global cycle
factor together with its FX-adjusted counterpart, and we provide the first
G10-wide evidence on the international validity of the \citet{cieslak2015}
mechanism. Summarising our findings, we conclude that international bond
risk premia contain a common, macro-anchored, and slowly moving component
that is large enough and stable enough to estimate in real time, provided
it is aggregated across markets, its inflation anchor is measured with core
CPI, and it is not asked to forecast exchange rates.

Our conclusions are subject to the limitations we discussed in
\Cref{ch:discussion}, in particular the inference under overlapping
returns, the largely homogeneous macro regime covered by our sample, and the
dependence of the real-time result on expanding-window estimation and core
inflation.
Because of this, we see several directions for future work. First,
a richer maturity menu and longer samples would allow a more precise
estimation of the factor loadings and would add independent observations of
the global cycle. Second, alternative weightings of the global factor, such
as equal or market-capitalisation weights, could show how much the
integration result depends on the GDP weighting. Third, the global cycle
factor could be embedded in a no-arbitrage term structure model with a
shared international trend, which would close the gap between our
reduced-form evidence and the structural framework of \citet{bauer2020}. We
leave these extensions to future research.
