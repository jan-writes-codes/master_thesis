\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

% Restate the research question and answer each sub-question explicitly:
%   (1) does the CF work locally across G10?
%   (2) does the global GCF subsume the local CF?
%   (3) does the dollar-return FXGCF help the USD investor?
% Summarise the contribution (unified global cycle factor + FX adjustment) and
% outline future work (more maturities, longer samples, no-arbitrage embedding).

This thesis asked whether the cycle factor of \citet{cieslak2015}
applies to international government bonds. To answer this question, I
constructed the local cycle factor for eleven G10 markets, aggregated the national factors into a GDP-weighted global
cycle factor, and additionally built a novel dollar-return global cycle factor
for an unhedged US-dollar investor. I evaluated this three-factor hierarchy
in three empirical phases, both in sample and under a fully recursive
out-of-sample pipeline, and I validated my empirical engine by replicating
the published US and international results (\Cref{ch:replication}). In this
chapter, I summarise my answer to the main question and to each of the three
steps in which I addressed it, restate my contribution, and outline directions
for future work.

The first step takes the local cycle factor market by market across the G10, and
the main question can be affirmed on its evidence. In all eleven markets, the
local cycle factor predicts one-year excess returns, significantly so in ten,
with the short-rate cycle entering negatively and the average cycle
positively, as they do for US Treasuries. The average in-sample $R^{2}$ is
$25\%$ and the loadings are uniform across the maturity curve. I conclude that
the trend--cycle mechanism is not specific to the United States but a
property of the asset class. This is in line with the falling-stars evidence
of \citet{bauer2020}, according to which the predictable component of bond
returns appears once the slow-moving macro anchors are accounted for.

The second step asks what the local factor still contributes once a
GDP-weighted global cycle factor is available. I find that the predictability is
predominantly global. The global factor carries a significant slope in all
eleven markets, so a single series does the work of eleven, and it subsumes
the local factors in most of them. Once the global factor is
included, local content survives only in Italy, the Netherlands,
and Belgium, concentrated in periods of sovereign stress. I conclude, as
\citet{dahlquist2013} do for the forward-rate family, that government bond
risk premia are internationally
integrated, although sovereign influences may dominate predictability. Because
five of the eleven markets share the euro and a common monetary policy, they
furnish fewer than eleven independent tests of the global cycle, so this
integration result should be read with that in mind.

The third step takes the perspective of the unhedged US-dollar investor, and
here my results are mixed. Dollar
conversion erodes most of the global factor's predictive
power. The dollar-return global cycle factor, built bottom-up from the
country-level USD regressions, restores a good deal of the lost fit. It raises the average dollar-investor fit considerably and correlates $0.81$ with the
unadjusted factor, well above the $0.50$ that \citet{dahlquist2013} report for
the forward-rate family, so it is the less distinct of the two adjustments.
The bottom-up construction is what buys that distinctness, since the top-down
variants reach $0.95$ to $0.99$ (\Cref{sec:rob-fxgcf-construction}). I also find,
however, that this recovery depends on the construction of $\FXGCF_{t}$ and that it
converts into only a small edge that does not help in timing the aggregate
dollar portfolio. The practical implication I
draw from this is that the dollar investor should hedge the currency
exposure, since the exploitable component of international bond returns
is a local-currency phenomenon.

The finding I regard as most important concerns real time. Out of sample,
the local factors lose to the recursive historical mean almost everywhere,
while the global cycle factor beats it in ten of eleven markets. This edge
holds across every subsample except the 2007--09 financial crisis, beats the
global forward-rate factor, and depends on measuring the inflation trend with
core rather than headline inflation. It also carries economic value. A timing strategy
based on the global factor raises the Sharpe ratio of a hedged global bond
portfolio from $0.34$ to $0.40$ with a positive certainty-equivalent gain
(\Cref{ch:strategy}).

With this thesis, I contribute a nested family of macro-anchored cycle
factors -- local, global, and dollar-return -- and I provide the first
G10-wide evidence on the international validity of the \citet{cieslak2015}
mechanism. Summarising my findings, international bond
risk premia contain a common, macro-anchored, and slowly moving component
that is large enough and stable enough to estimate in real time, provided
it is aggregated across markets, its inflation anchor is measured with core
CPI, and it is not asked to forecast exchange rates.

My conclusions are subject to the limitations I discussed in
\Cref{ch:discussion}, in particular the inference under overlapping
returns, the largely homogeneous macro regime covered by my sample, and the
dependence of the real-time result on expanding-window estimation and core
inflation.
Because of this, I see several directions for future work. A richer maturity grid and longer samples would allow a more precise
estimation of the factor loadings and would add independent observations of
the global cycle. Then, alternative weightings of the global factor, such
as equal or market-capitalisation weights, could show how much the
integration result depends on the GDP weighting. And finally, the global cycle
factor could be embedded in a no-arbitrage term structure model with a
shared international trend, which would close the gap between my
reduced-form evidence and the structural framework of \citet{bauer2020}. I
leave these extensions to future research.
