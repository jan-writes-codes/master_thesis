### §2.1 — Expectations Hypothesis, predictability, spanning debate

#### Fama & Bliss (1987) — The Information in Long-Maturity Forward Rates **[EXISTING]**
- **Authors (year):** Eugene F. Fama, Robert R. Bliss — *American Economic Review* 77(4), 680–692.
- **Files:** `D-EH rejection/The_Information_in_Long-Maturi.pdf`
- **Full Abstract:** "Current 1-year forward rates on 1- to 5-year U.S. Treasury bonds are information about the current term structure of 1-year expected returns on the bonds, and forward rates track variation through time in 1-year expected returns. More interesting, 1-year forward rates forecast changes in the 1-year interest rate 2- to 4-years ahead, and forecast power increases with the forecast horizon. We attribute this forecast power to a mean-reverting tendency in the 1-year interest rate."
- **Conclusion:**
  - Founding EH-rejection result: the forward–spot spread predicts the matched-maturity one-year excess return.
  - Forward rates also forecast future spot-rate changes 2–4 years ahead; power rises with horizon.
  - Attributed to mean reversion of the one-year rate; established the zero-coupon data standard.
- **Relevant?** Yes — foundational. **Area:** Foundational EH rejection / forward-rate predictability. **Where:** §2.1. **Cited?** Yes (famabliss1987).

#### Campbell & Shiller (1991) — Yield Spreads and Interest Rate Movements: A Bird's Eye View **[EXISTING]**
- **Authors (year):** John Y. Campbell, Robert J. Shiller — *Review of Economic Studies* 58(3), 495–514 (NBER WP 3153, 1989).
- **Files:** `D-EH rejection/campbell_birdseye.pdf` **and** `D-EH rejection/w3153.pdf` — **same paper** (published article vs. NBER preprint); one citation.
- **Full Abstract (NBER WP version):** "The expectations theory of the term structure implies that the spread between a longer-term interest rate and a shorter-term interest rate forecasts two subsequent interest rate changes: the change in yield of the longer-term bond over the life of the shorter-term bond, and a weighted average of the changes in shorter-term rates over the life of the longer-term bond. For postwar U.S. data from McCulloch [1987] and just about any combination of maturities between one month and ten years we find that the former relation is not borne out by the data, the latter roughly is. When the yield spread is high the yield on the longer-term bond tends to fall, contrary to the expectations theory; at the same time, the shorter-term interest rate tends to rise, just as the expectations theory requires. We discuss several possible interpretations of these findings. We argue that they are consistent with a model in which the spread is a multiple of the value implied by the expectations theory. This model could be generated by time-varying risk premia which are correlated with expected increases in short-term interest rates, or by a failure of rational expectations in our sample period."
- **Conclusion:**
  - The yield spread predicts short-rate changes (as EH implies) but not long-yield changes — the "sign puzzle."
  - Consistent with the spread being a multiple of its EH-implied value.
  - Interpreted via time-varying risk premia or a failure of rational expectations.
- **Relevant?** Yes — foundational. **Area:** Foundational EH rejection (sign puzzle). **Where:** §2.1. **Cited?** Yes (campbell1991). *Note: two file copies = one bibliography entry.*

#### Ludvigson & Ng (2009) — Macro Factors in Bond Risk Premia **[EXISTING]**
- **Authors (year):** Sydney C. Ludvigson, Serena Ng — *Review of Financial Studies* 22(12), 5027–5067.
- **Files:** `D-Unspanned macro risk/hhp081.pdf`
- **Full Abstract:** "Are there important cyclical fluctuations in bond market premiums and, if so, with what macroeconomic aggregates do these premiums vary? We use the methodology of dynamic factor analysis for large datasets to investigate possible empirical linkages between forecastable variation in excess bond returns and macroeconomic fundamentals. We find that "real" and "inflation" factors have important forecasting power for future excess returns on U.S. government bonds, above and beyond the predictive power contained in forward rates and yield spreads. This behavior is ruled out by commonly employed affine term structure models where the forecastability of bond returns and bond yields is completely summarized by the cross-section of yields or forward rates. An important implication of these findings is that the cyclical behavior of estimated risk premia in bond returns and long-term yields depends importantly on whether the information in macroeconomic factors is included in forecasts of excess bond returns. Without the macro factors, risk premia appear virtually acyclical, whereas with the estimated factors risk premia have a marked countercyclical component, consistent with theories that imply investors must be compensated for risks associated with macroeconomic activity. (JEL E0, E4, G10, G12)"
- **Conclusion:**
  - Dynamic factor analysis on 132 macro series yields "real"/"inflation" factors that forecast excess bond returns.
  - Explains up to 26% of one-year-ahead 2-year excess-return variation; strong OOS.
  - CP + macro factors raise R² to 44% vs. 31% for CP alone — macro info is *not* subsumed by yields.
  - Risk premia look acyclical from yields alone but strongly countercyclical with macro factors (foundational unspanned-macro evidence).
- **Relevant?** Yes — underpins the thesis's unspanned-macro framing. **Area:** Unspanned macro risk. **Where:** §2.1 / §2.3. **Cited?** Yes (ludvigson2009).

#### Joslin, Priebsch & Singleton (2014) — Risk Premiums in DTSMs with Unspanned Macro Risks **[NEW — high priority]**
- **Authors (year):** Scott Joslin, Marcel Priebsch, Kenneth J. Singleton — *Journal of Finance* 69(3), 1197–1233.
- **Files:** `A-1 UIP failure : carry/…JOSLIN….pdf` **and** `D-Unspanned macro risk/…JOSLIN….pdf` (same paper, duplicate file).
- **Full Abstract:** "This paper quantifies how variation in economic activity and inflation in the United States influences the market prices of level, slope, and curvature risks in Treasury markets. We develop a novel arbitrage-free dynamic term structure model in which bond investment decisions are influenced by output and inflation risks that are unspanned by (imperfectly correlated with) information about the shape of the yield curve. Our model reveals that, between 1985 and 2007, these risks accounted for a large portion of the variation in forward terms premiums, and there was pronounced cyclical variation in the market prices of level and slope risks."
- **Conclusion:**
  - Canonical arbitrage-free macro-finance model relaxing the counterfactual macro-spanning restriction (output/inflation unspanned by yield PCs).
  - Unspanned output and inflation explain a large share of forward-term-premium variation, with pronounced business-cycle variation in the prices of level/slope risk.
  - Enforcing spanning produces materially different (often counterfactual) term-premium dynamics, especially post-2007.
  - Concludes macro spanning is empirically too restrictive — macro risk beyond the curve is first-order.
- **Relevant?** Yes — the leading formal "unspanned macro risk" reference the thesis leans on. **Area:** Spanning debate / unspanned macro. **Where:** §2.1 (alongside duffee2011, ludvigson2009). **Cited?** No — strong candidate (e.g. `joslin2014`).

#### Fama (2006) — The Behavior of Interest Rates **[NEW — medium]**
- **Authors (year):** Eugene F. Fama — *Review of Financial Studies* 19(2), 359–379.
- **Files:** `D-EH rejection/hhj019.pdf`
- **Full Abstract:** *No formal abstract.* Opening summary: "The evidence in Fama and Bliss (1987) that forward interest rates forecast future spot interest rates for horizons beyond a year repeats in the out-of-sample 1986–2004 period. But the inference that this forecast power is due to mean reversion of the spot rate toward a constant expected value no longer seems valid. Instead, the predictability of the spot rate captured by forward rates seems to be due to mean reversion toward a time-varying expected value that is subject to a sequence of apparently permanent shocks that are on balance positive to mid-1981 and on balance negative thereafter."
- **Conclusion:**
  - Extends Fama–Bliss OOS to 1986–2004; forward rates still forecast spot-rate changes.
  - "Mean reversion toward a constant" is no longer supported.
  - The spot rate reverts to a *time-varying, non-stationary* long-term mean driven by permanent shocks — a conceptual precursor to the trend-inflation-anchored cycle.
- **Relevant?** Yes — motivates the trend–cycle decomposition. **Area:** Non-stationary time-varying mean-reversion anchor. **Where:** §2.1 / §2.3. **Cited?** No — candidate (`fama2006`); complements fama1984 & famabliss1987.

#### Bulkley, Harris & Nawosah (2011) — Revisiting the EH **[NEW — medium]**
- **Authors (year):** George Bulkley, Richard D.F. Harris, Vivekanand Nawosah — *Journal of Banking & Finance* 35, 1202–1212.
- **Files:** `D-EH rejection/1-s2.0-S037842661000381X-main.pdf`
- **Full Abstract:** "The expectations hypothesis of the term structure has been decisively rejected in a large empirical literature that spans several decades. In this paper, using a newly constructed dataset of synthetic zero-coupon bond yields, we show that evidence against the expectations hypothesis is substantially weaker in data generated after the widespread publicity of its failure. These results are consistent with the idea that asset pricing anomalies tend to disappear once they are widely recognized."
- **Conclusion:**
  - Extends McCulloch–Kwon zero-coupon yields to 2009.
  - Evidence against the EH is much weaker in 1991–2009 than in 1946–1991.
  - Attributes the convergence to financial innovation and arbitrage capital — anomalies are transitory once public.
- **Relevant?** Yes — bears directly on the stability of the predictability the thesis exploits. **Area:** Erosion of the EH-rejection anomaly over time. **Where:** §2.1; Ch.9 (Discussion, anomaly stability). **Cited?** No — candidate (`bulkley2011`).

#### Jondeau & Ricart (1999) — EH tests on US/German/French/UK Euro-rates **[NEW — low]**
- **Authors (year):** Eric Jondeau, Roland Ricart — *Journal of International Money and Finance* 18, 725–750.
- **Files:** `D-EH rejection/1-s2.0-S0261560699000297-main.pdf`
- **Full Abstract:** "This paper tests the expectations hypothesis of the term structure on US, German, French, and UK Euro-rates. Three tests are examined: the first is based on forward rates and the other two are based on the interest rates spread. First, we show that the 'sign puzzle' highlighted by Campbell and Shiller (Campbell, J.Y., Shiller, R.J., 1991. Yield spreads and interest rate movements: a bird's eye view. Review of Economic Studies 58, 495–514) for US data does not arise in the cases of French and UK short-term rates. Second, we propose tests for the expectations hypothesis based on error-correction models. With these tests the sign puzzle disappears, but the 'country puzzle' remains."
- **Conclusion:**
  - EH tested on 1975–1997 Euro-rates (US, DE, FR, UK), standard + error-correction approaches.
  - Standard tests: EH rarely rejected for France/UK, generally rejected for US/Germany.
  - ECM removes the sign puzzle but the "country puzzle" persists.
- **Relevant?** Marginal — early international EH evidence, short maturities/different countries. **Area:** International EH rejection / country heterogeneity. **Where:** §2.1. **Cited?** No — low-priority candidate.

#### Bulkley, Harris & Nawosah (2015) — Can behavioral biases explain EH rejections? **[NEW — low]**
- **Authors (year):** George Bulkley, Richard D.F. Harris, Vivekanand Nawosah — *Journal of Banking & Finance* 58, 179–193.
- **Files:** `D-EH rejection/1-s2.0-S0378426615000953-main.pdf`
- **Full Abstract:** "We test whether the rejections of the expectations hypothesis can be explained by two behavioral biases: the law of small numbers and conservatism. We use the term structure to decompose excess bond returns into components related to expectation errors and expectation revisions, enabling a direct test of behavioral models using the expectations of market participants. We find systematic patterns in expectation errors and expectation revisions, which are consistent with these two biases. We show that a trading strategy that exploits these biases delivers significant economic profits and that our results are unlikely to be driven by a time-varying risk premium."
- **Conclusion:**
  - Decomposes excess bond returns (1952–2012) into expectation-error and expectation-revision components.
  - Finds patterns consistent with the law of small numbers and conservatism; behavioral strategies beat CP / CP+macro strategies.
  - Alphas stay significant — the behavioral component is not fully a time-varying risk premium.
- **Relevant?** Marginal — a competing behavioral explanation (foil to the risk-premium view). **Area:** Behavioral vs. risk-premium explanation of EH rejection. **Where:** §2.1. **Cited?** No — optional.

#### Brown, Cyree, Griffiths & Winters (2008) — Very short-term rates EH test **[NEW — omit]**

### §2.2 — Cochrane–Piazzesi forward-rate factor

#### Sekkel (2011) — International Evidence on Bond Risk Premia **[NEW — high priority]**
- **Authors (year):** Rodrigo Sekkel — *Journal of Banking & Finance* 35, 174–181.
- **Files:** `D-EH rejection/1-s2.0-S0378426610002955-main.pdf`
- **Full Abstract:** "This paper revisits the study of time-varying excess bond returns in international bond markets. Using newly available yield curve data from 10 different countries with independent monetary policy, I test the robustness of Cochrane and Piazzesi (2005). For most countries in my sample, I find more modest predictive power for forward rates than originally found by Cochrane and Piazzesi (2005) for the US. Their single-factor model captures well the predictability in international data, and this factor also tends to have a tent-shape in most countries of my sample. CP factors are more idiosyncratic across countries than yields or forward rates. Finally, I show that the recent financial crisis has significantly affected the predictability of excess bond returns."
- **Conclusion:**
  - Tests the CP single-factor model on a ~G10 panel (AU, CA, DE, JP, NZ, NO, SE, CH, UK, US, 1978–2009) — nearly the thesis's own country set.
  - CP factor forecasts excess returns of every maturity in all countries; on average smaller R² than the US original, but the tent shape recurs.
  - CP factors are more idiosyncratic across countries than yields/forwards.
  - Predictability weakened after the 2007–09 crisis (parameter instability).
- **Relevant?** Yes — the closest precedent to the thesis's G10 CP/cycle extension. **Area:** International/multi-country CP predictability. **Where:** §2.2 and §2.5 (key benchmark). **Cited?** No — high-priority candidate (`sekkel2011`).

#### Kessler & Scherer (2009) — Varying Risk Premia in International Bond Markets **[NEW — high priority]**
- **Authors (year):** Stephan Kessler, Bernd Scherer — *Journal of Banking & Finance* 33, 1361–1375.
- **Files:** `D-EH rejection/1-s2.0-S0378426609000260-main.pdf`
- **Full Abstract:** "Cochrane and Piazzesi [Cochrane, J.H., Piazzesi, M., 2005. Bond risk premia. American Economic Review 95, 138–160] use forward rates to forecast future bond returns. We extend their approach by applying their model to international bond markets. Our results indicate that the unrestricted Cochrane and Piazzesi (2005) model has a reasonable forecasting power for future bond returns. The restricted model, however, does not perform as well on an international level. Furthermore, we cannot confirm the systematic tent shape of the estimated parameters found by Cochrane and Piazzesi (2005). The forecasting models are used to implement various trading strategies. These strategies exhibit high information ratios when implemented in individual countries or on an international level and outperform alternative approaches. We introduce an alternative specification to forecast future bond returns and achieve superior risk-adjusted returns in our trading strategy. Bayesian model averaging is used to enhance the performance of the proposed trading strategy."
- **Conclusion:**
  - First systematic international test of CP (2005) across 7 markets.
  - Unrestricted CP works internationally; the restricted single-factor form is less robust and the tent shape is not confirmed.
  - CP-based trading strategies achieve high information ratios, beating slope/momentum/Diebold–Li benchmarks; Bayesian model averaging improves stability.
- **Relevant?** Yes — precedent for both the international CP extension *and* the timing-strategy evaluation. **Area:** International CP extension; trading strategy. **Where:** §2.2 / §2.5; Ch.8b (Strategy). **Cited?** No — high-priority candidate (`kessler2009`).

#### Rebonato & Nyholm (2025) — Why Does the CP Model Predict Treasury Returns? **[NEW — high priority]**
- **Authors (year):** Riccardo Rebonato, Ken Nyholm — *Journal of Empirical Finance* 84, 101650.
- **Files:** `D-EH rejection/1-s2.0-S0927539825000726-main.pdf`
- **Full Abstract:** "We explain why the Cochrane–Piazzesi (CP) model, which uses a single tent-shaped linear combination of forward rates, is so effective at predicting bond excess returns. By using a novel statistical test coupled with a popular resampling technique, we rule out the possibility that the high predictability may be an artefact of in-sample overfitting. Then we find that, contrary to explanations proposed in the original CP paper, neither the specific tent shape of the factor loadings nor the four-to-five-year yield spread are essential for the model's predictive power. Instead, our analysis shows that the predictive power of the CP model lies in its ability to identify the cointegration relationship among the quasi-unit-root forward rate regressors needed to produce the stationary process of excess returns. To support this interpretation we show that cointegration relationships among forward rates directly predict strong predictors of excess returns, and we propose that the cointegration modes of attraction generate at least part of the excess returns. Our findings shed new light on the source of bond return predictability captured by the CP factor and highlight the link between cointegration properties and the dynamics of yields."
- **Conclusion:**
  - Confirms CP's OOS predictive power is not an overfitting artefact (novel test + resampling).
  - Neither the tent shape nor the 4–5y spread is essential; extends to 10y maturities.
  - Proposes a cointegration explanation: CP identifies cointegrating combinations of near-unit-root forwards that produce a stationary excess-return process.

If neither returns nor dividend growth are predictable, then the dividend-price ratio is a constant. If the null turns off return predictability, it must turn on the predictability of dividend growth, and then confront the evidence against such predictability in the data. I find that the absence of dividend growth predictability gives much stronger statistical evidence against the null, with roughly 1-2% probability values, than does the presence of return predictability, which only gives about 20% probability values. I argue that tests based on long-run return and dividend growth regressions provide the cleanest and most interpretable evidence on return predictability, again delivering about 1-2% probability values against the hypothesis that returns are unpredictable. I show that Goyal and Welch's (2005) finding of poor out-of-sample R2 does not reject return forecastability. Out-of-sample R2 is poor even if all dividend yield variation comes from time-varying expected returns."
- **Conclusion:**
  - The no-predictability null implies dividend-growth predictability, which the data reject more strongly.
  - Long-run regressions give the cleanest evidence for predictability.
  - **Poor OOS R² does not reject in-sample forecastability** — OOS R² is poor even under true time-varying expected returns.
- **Relevant?** Yes — the classic defense of in-sample predictability against weak OOS R²; directly relevant to interpreting the thesis's local-factor IS-vs-OOS divergence. **Area:** Methodology of predictability testing (IS vs. OOS R²). **Where:** Ch.5 (OOS R² discussion) and Ch.9 (Discussion). **Cited?** No — candidate (`cochrane2006`); complements campbellthompson2008.
